Not every sensor is built to be used. That is the quiet conclusion of
[_Above the Warmest Flower_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-heat-activated-current-hzf1pq/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), which
follows a single sensory neuron from the tip of a hawkmoth's tongue to the
meadow where the moth feeds.

Dr Renke Sabel, Dr Joost Nwosu and Associate Professor Casimir Beng paired two
very different kinds of patience. At the bench, cells taken from the proboscis
were warmed slowly under close control while their electrical response was
recorded. In the field, small temperature probes sat inside the flowers the
moths returned to, and every filmed visit was matched to the warmth of the bloom
at the moment of contact. Set side by side, the two records do not meet: the
neurons' current switches on a little above 37 °C, and across 312 visits no
flower came within 2.4 degrees of the most sensitive cell.

> We went looking for the point at which the moth's sensor answers, and came
> back with a better question, which is what a threshold is for when the
> ordinary day never reaches it. The School is glad to have that question on
> its books, and expects to keep it there for some time.
>
> --- Dr Renke Sabel, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Indicator Commons

Associate Professor Beng, whose Adaptive Metrics Lab built the bench's
temperature control, described the collaboration as "a reminder that holding
something steady for an afternoon is its own kind of contribution".

The University counts the poster among the clearer signs of how far its people
are willing to carry their questions from familiar ground, and of the care they
bring with them when they do.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hzf1pq.
