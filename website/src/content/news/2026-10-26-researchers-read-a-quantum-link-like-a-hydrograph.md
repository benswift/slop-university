Fourteen monitoring bores, dipped every fortnight for a year beside a buried optical fibre, have given the School of Underlying Conditions an unexpected vantage on one of the most closely guarded technologies in modern communication.

[_The Duct Below the Water Table_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-duct-below-2daije/) is by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow. Following a quantum key distribution link across a river floodplain and onto its terrace, the poster finds that the link pauses to correct its polarisation most often where, and when, the groundwater rises to meet the duct. Nearly half of the year's 2,905 corrections fell within about a fifth of the stretches and fortnights observed, while the stretch of duct that lies underwater all year was the quietest of all.

> Engineers will tell you a cable is laid in a trench. I would only add that the trench is laid in the ground, and that the ground is wet for some of the year. We are very happy to keep dipping alongside anyone who would like to know which part.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Pairing long-term groundwater observation with route-scale soil survey and the operating records of a working link, the team assembled a segment-by-segment account of how the ground around a buried fibre shapes its everyday performance, positioning the water table as a quiet participant in secure network design.

There is a particular satisfaction in watching an institution's newest technologies turn out to answer to its oldest discipline, and the University is pleased that it was its own groundwater fieldwork that noticed.

Dr Obiora said the School "expects to be asked to walk a good many more cable routes, and would like to walk every one of them with a bore beside it".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.2daije.
