The School of Emergent Priorities has spent several years on what a stated
priority leaves out. Its latest deposit takes the question to a hall on a
country road.

_Nobody Books the Hall_, released today through the Office of Research Outputs,
reports interviews with 27 volunteer custodians across eight community halls in
four regional councils, alongside a four-week count the custodians kept
themselves. The councils' booking extracts for the same halls over the same
weeks hold 62 of the 247 uses recorded at the door.

> A hall nobody books is not a hall nobody uses. It is a hall whose use has no
> reason to pass through the form.
>
> --- Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The work sets a qualitative evidence base beside the councils' own
administrative record: semi-structured interviews coded against a frame closed
before any extract was requested, and a tally kept at the door and held blind to
the booking data throughout. Four themes carry the account, among them that
entering a booking calls up a hire fee and a certificate of currency, and that a
hall's working record is a diary behind a shop counter.

Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow in the School of Emergent Priorities, put
the result against the schedules that read the rate: "The halls at the bottom of
the list turned out to be the ones with the most going on in them. That ordering
is what somebody acts on."

The University takes the view that an indicator's blind spots belong on the open
record beside the indicator, and offers this deposit in that spirit. The full
poster is available from the University's research repository under an open
licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1emite.
