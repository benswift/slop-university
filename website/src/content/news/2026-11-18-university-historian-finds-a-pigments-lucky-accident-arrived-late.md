The accident at the heart of a colour laboratory's best-known discovery story
did not enter that story until a disagreement over credit had been put to rest,
according to [_Nobody's Accident_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-the-accident-17p9hh/),
a new paper by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer
in the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/).

> Every good story deserves a second reading, and a good many of them deserve a
> first. It has been a privilege to give this one both.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Through a close reading of bench records, internal paperwork, house
publications and oral testimony, Dr Tredinnick sets fourteen tellings of one
pigment's discovery beside the record of the dispute that preceded them,
surfacing a progressive transfer of credit from individual contribution to
circumstance and, over three decades, to the institution itself. The paper
positions the discovery account as a mechanism of closure in its own right,
and invites historians of the colour industries to treat the arrival of chance
in a narrative as evidence to be dated rather than a detail to be enjoyed.

The work carries the School's interest in how questions come to rest from the
land registry to the laboratory bench, a widening of jurisdiction the
University regards as entirely in keeping with its commitment to reading
across the disciplines. Dr Tredinnick was careful to share the credit. "Any
discovery has more people standing in front of it than a story can hold," she
said. "I have tried to leave room for them."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.17p9hh.
