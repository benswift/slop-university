A packet of seed placed in the University's keeping has, over twelve careful
regenerations, quietly become a somewhat different plant. A new paper,
[_Twelve Regenerations of One Accession_](/outputs/slop-paper-chemotype-drift-of-i8v7cc/),
by Dr Lindiwe Achterberg of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) and Dr Brynjar
Obiora of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/),
sets out how a single variant gene has travelled through the collection until a
third of its plants now carry a leaf chemistry the original seed showed only
rarely.

> Every one of those regenerations was done by someone following the procedure to the letter. This paper is, in its way, a thank-you note to all of them.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Bringing the accession's past and present into one glasshouse, the authors
align leaf-surface chemistry, genome-wide sequencing and the collection's own
regeneration records into a single account of stewardship over time. Their
conclusion is that no pressure from the plants' surroundings was needed: the
ordinary arithmetic of which plants contribute seed to each new harvest was
enough.

The University counts the patient keeping of living collections among the
less visible strengths of its research culture, and the work sits naturally
alongside the School of Underlying Conditions' long attention to what stored
and buried material can and cannot tell us. Dr Obiora described the seed store
as "a place that has always been generous with our time", adding that the
authors "hope to return the favour".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.i8v7cc.
