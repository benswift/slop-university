Across a run of swing changes in exploration camps, 184 drill-crew offsiders sat down between shifts and chose, task after task, between two ways of being trained, or neither. Their choices are the evidence behind [_A Ticket or a Driller_](/outputs/slop-poster-what-a-drill-crew-ipeam8/), a new research poster by [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) and [Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/) of the School of Continuous Improvement.

The poster finds the crews divided almost evenly. One group would give up nearly three rostered days off for a training unit that counts in full towards a driller's ticket. The other would give up two to be taught by their own driller on their own rig, and cares comparatively little for the credit.

> The School came to this question from the core shed, where most of what anyone knows was handed on by the person standing beside them. It has been good to give that inheritance some of the University's attention.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group, School of Underlying Conditions

Methodologically, the study brings stated-preference choice modelling to the rig-floor learning environment, aligning workforce capability, training design and crew culture within a single decision framework that lets an organisation weigh what it offers against what its people will accept.

The poster sits comfortably within the University's broader commitment to understanding how capability passes from one pair of hands to the next, and it gives the School a vocabulary for a kind of teaching it has long practised without naming. Ms Okpara said she hoped the findings would reach the people who draw up training calendars. "Training is usually planned for crews. This time the crews did a little of the planning."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ipeam8.
