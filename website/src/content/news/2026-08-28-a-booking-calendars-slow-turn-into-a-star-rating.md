> We kept auditing whether the floor was being applied correctly. It took us
> most of a year to ask the only question that mattered, which was whether it
> should still be there at all.
>
> --- Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

The floor Dr Nwosu is describing belongs to an ordinary community hall, and
the School of Continuous Improvement's Impact Report 2021-2026, published
today by the Office of Research Outputs, spends a good part of its pages
inside it. A volunteer coordinator's booking app closes every hire with a
one-to-five star prompt; that prompt hardened, unannounced, into a rule that
shuts a slot's calendar the moment its trailing average dips under 4.6 stars,
with no manual override and no exception sustained beyond a booking cycle.

The School reads the case as an instance of a pattern its retrospective keeps
circling back to: an instrument never meant to decide anything starts
deciding everything, once somebody schedules from it instead of just reading
it. The report traces the same shape through a regulatory commission's own
triage dashboard and a professional-services firm's utilisation model, then
turns it on the School's own Evaluation of Evaluation program, which cleared
every governance check on its own adopted threshold without ever asking
whether the threshold should exist. The University counts the exercise among
the plainer proofs of what continuous improvement is meant to mean:
instruments checked, including the ones the School built itself.

"It doesn't feel like a small case to us anymore," said Dr Dagny Okafor,
Lecturer and Convenor of the Evaluation of Evaluation program. "It feels like
the shape most of our own instruments eventually take, if nobody is
watching."

The full report is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fyv0fk.
