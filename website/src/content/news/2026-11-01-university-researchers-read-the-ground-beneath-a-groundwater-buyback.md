Seventy-two sealed envelopes, a single auction round and a close reading of the
sediment beneath every bore that bid: these are the materials of
[_The Cheapest Bore Was Already Going Dry_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-reverse-auction-5a2clo/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/). It asks what
a district actually buys when it invites irrigators to name their price for
giving up a bore, and finds that the lowest prices came, for the most part,
from the thinnest ground.

The work is led by
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, with [Dr Runa Adegoke](/people/runa-adegoke/), Senior
Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey, and
[Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/), Director of the
Master of Priority Studies, both of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> Every evaluation I have run has turned, sooner or later, on what people know
> and are never asked. It is heartening to see that question put in sealed
> envelopes, and the envelopes taken seriously.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

The poster sets auction design alongside hydrogeological record-keeping,
positioning the bid itself as a carrier of local knowledge and opening a
pathway toward retirement rounds that weigh what is offered against what the
ground could still have given.

It stands as a further expression of the University's conviction that a
market, read patiently enough, is also a geological record.

"The ground keeps its own counsel, and the people who farm above it keep a fair
share of it too," said Associate Professor Uzoma. "We are glad to have begun
listening to both at once."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.5a2clo.
