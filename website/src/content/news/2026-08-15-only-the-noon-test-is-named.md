What a preparedness return counts as community education and what a community
has actually learned are, on the evidence of a study released today, two
different lists. _Correct at Noon_, from the School of Emergent Priorities,
played 384 residents across eleven district towns the four signals their local
siren system carries and asked them to name each one.

> A signal that only ever sounds when nothing is wrong is still teaching people
> something. Our interest is in what a town has been taught, and in the return
> that records the lesson as delivered.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The team ran nine weeks of hall sessions and street stalls, presenting
standardised recordings in randomised order at a fixed level and taking a forced
choice from five labels lifted word for word from the district's own community
education leaflet. The weekly noon test was named correctly by 91.4 per cent of
respondents. The emergency alert managed 37.8 per cent, the all clear 30.6, and
the call that turns out the volunteer brigade 22.7. Alert and all clear were not
reliably separated at all.

"Length of residence helps with exactly one of the four," said Dr Runa Adegoke,
Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey. "That is the finding
we would want a district to sit with before it schedules more of what it is
already doing."

For the School, the work extends its longer effort to put a figure on the
distance between what an institution records itself as doing and what that doing
achieves, and a comparison across towns that sound the test at different hours
is already scoped.
