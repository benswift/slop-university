For one field season a camera on a pole looked along the face of ninety-six soil
pits while fourteen surveyors described what they saw there, and two researchers
later wrote down every occasion on which one of them put something down. The
account of those 6,154 occasions is now published as _Where the Knife Goes: A
Link Analysis of the Soil Pit as a Workstation_, by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior
Research Fellow, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, both of the School of
Underlying Conditions.

> I have carried the same knife into a great many pits and would not, before
> this, have described us as collaborators. I do not expect either of us to
> behave any differently now that it is written down.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings the layout methods of the factory bench to a place of work that
is dug in the morning and filled in by the afternoon. It maps the resting places
of nine objects, sets out how surveyors apportion the work between a hand kept
for soil and a hand kept for paper, and carries those observations through to a
pit-edge board with a position for each item. In trial the board reduced the
time surveyors spent looking for things, the pencil above all, and left the
length of the task as it was. Its slot for the knife went unused, the surveyors
preferring to leave the knife in the pit face at the depth they meant to measure
next.

The University is glad to see one of its schools give this order of attention to
its own working arrangements, and notes that the board has since entered service
with the School's soil survey. Dr Emenike said the pencil "remains a matter on
which the School is keeping an open mind, and for the most part an ear."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.sgmu6q.
