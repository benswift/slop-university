It takes a shift supervisor a minute or so to ask a warehouse worker a few
food-safety questions and tick a box. Working out what the tick means took
[Dr Dagny Okafor](/people/dagny-okafor/),
[Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu](/people/ingeborg-nwachukwu/) and
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/) rather longer, and
_Ticked, Not Tested, Explained_, a new research explainer from the School of
Continuous Improvement, now walks viewers through what their paper,
[_Ticked, Not Tested_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-warehouse-s-food-handling-amx2a8/),
found.

> The questions this study raises are ones the School expects to be living with
> for some time, and we welcome every one of them. Some of the best will come
> from people who have stood on either side of a check like this one.
>
> --- [Dr Dagny Okafor](/people/dagny-okafor/), Lecturer and Convenor,
> Evaluation of Evaluation

Three months after their refresher, nine hundred workers sat a written retest,
and the explainer lines up their scores, ticked against unticked: the two sit
almost on top of one another. Recordings made with everyone's consent show the
tick to be a dependable record that a conversation took place. What emerges is a
careful account of the difference between certifying that a procedure happened
and knowing what it achieved, carried from the loading dock into the wider work
of the School.

Slop University is delighted that a finding with such everyday reach can now be
watched as readily as it can be read.

"We spent a long time listening to recordings of very short conversations," said
[Associate Professor Beng](/people/casimir-beng/). "This time we got to do the
talking."

The full explainer is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.vooaav.
