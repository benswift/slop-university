Take the outline of the cell off a textbook diagram, leave everything else where it was, and fewer of its readers will conclude that the virus leaving at the end is the one that arrived at the start. So reports _The Same Thing, Further Along: Object Identity across the Arrows of a Retroviral Replication-Cycle Diagram_, a paper by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, with Dr Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer, and Eivind Onwuka, Doctoral Candidate, both of the School of Continuous Improvement.

> There are a great many diagrams. We have now attended closely to one of them, and it would be premature to say what the others will make of that.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper aligns the experimental study of how people read pictures with a figure most students of biology meet early and few are asked about afterwards. Across two experiments and 277 readers it establishes which of three things an arrow is understood to say, and how far that understanding rests on the lines an illustrator draws around the arrows for the sake of orientation. With the outlines in place, 61% of readers described a round trip; without them, 39% did.

The University regards the paper as a considered addition to what it knows about how its own teaching materials are met by the people they are handed to. Dr Adeyemi said the School of Continuous Improvement had "been glad to lend a second pair of eyes to a question that turned out to be about eyes".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m6lgbb.
