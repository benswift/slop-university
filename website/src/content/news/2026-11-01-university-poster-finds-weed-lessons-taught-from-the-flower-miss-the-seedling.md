Across one autumn sowing, 141 second-year agronomy students learned sixteen weeds
from one of three kinds of teaching material and were then sent into a sown
paddock to name them as seedlings.
[_Recognised at the Wrong Stage_](/outputs/slop-poster-recognised-at-the-jbturg/)
is by [Dr Sten Okwuosa](/people/sten-okwuosa/), Senior Research Fellow and
Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), with
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and
[Dr Torun Ezeigwe](/people/torun-ezeigwe/), Senior Lecturer and Director of the
Master of Applied Measurement.

> Our thanks go first to the students, who knelt at a great many quadrats on
> cold mornings and were generous with their uncertainty, and to the tutors who
> taught the same practical three different ways without complaint.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas

The study sets the University's herbarium-style reference collections, its
glasshouse seedling trays and a set of photographic plates side by side as
pathways into identification practice, following each from the practical
class to the paddock and, seven weeks on, back again. Its central observation
is a reversal: the material that best prepares a student to recognise a plant
in flower prepares them least well for the same plant at two leaves, and
leaves them most sure of themselves there.

The University regards the poster as a welcome sign of its research schools
taking a closer interest in the teaching farm, and of the teaching farm
returning the favour.

"I learned my weeds from the soil up, more or less by accident," said
Dr Obiora. "It is pleasing to find that the accident has something to
recommend it."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jbturg.
