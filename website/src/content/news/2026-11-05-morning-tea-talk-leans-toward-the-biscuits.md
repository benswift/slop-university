A new paper brings researchers from three of the University's Schools to one
ordinary table. In
[_Talking Toward the Plate_](/outputs/slop-paper-whom-a-speaker-ipbb7x/),
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), with
[Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), and
[Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), take the
workplace morning tea as a serious setting for the study of choice.

Over 160 recorded conversations, eight office volunteers each talked with two
listeners who agreed with them at carefully varied rates. The speakers shared
out their attention much as the field's established models predict. They also
leaned, consistently and by a measurable margin, toward whichever listener was
sitting beside the plate.

> None of this would exist without the people who gave up their morning tea to
> sit with us, week after week, and simply talk. We are very grateful to them.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The study connects a long-standing body of behavioural theory with the shared
rituals of working life, translating a laboratory framework for allocation and
preference into the everyday settings where colleagues actually meet. The
University counts it among a widening set of studies that take the smallest
customs of the working day as objects of patient inquiry.

"So much of a working week is settled over a cup of tea, and it seemed only
right to give that table the same care as any other field site," said Ms
Anyanwu.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ipbb7x.
