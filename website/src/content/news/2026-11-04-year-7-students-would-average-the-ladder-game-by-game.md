> A ladder is one of the few places a twelve-year-old can watch fairness being
> worked out in public, by someone they know, every Sunday. It has been a
> privilege to sit beside them while they worked it out again for themselves.
>
> --- Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

That privilege is now a paper. _Fairer by the Game_, from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) and the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), brings
the percentage column of a junior football ladder into the Year 7 mathematics
classroom and asks what students make of it.

Across four classes at two schools, the study invited 96 students to calculate
a fair figure for two teams finishing level on seven wins, to weigh the
competition's own figure against theirs, and to reflect on it further in
one-to-one conversations. The most common response averaged each game's ratio,
and most of the students who took that route found the official ladder unfair.
The authors trace that judgement to a principled commitment to treating every
game alike, and position the ladder as a natural setting for the weighted mean.

It is the kind of patient classroom scholarship the University is pleased to
count among its own, and it extends the School's continuing interest in the
community competitions where so many young people first meet a ranking.

"Our School has spent some years asking what a figure owes the people it
places in order," said Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director of the
Master of Applied Measurement. "It is a pleasure to find that question already
being asked, with some force, in Year 7."

The paper's co-authors include
[Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/).

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ekuim8.
