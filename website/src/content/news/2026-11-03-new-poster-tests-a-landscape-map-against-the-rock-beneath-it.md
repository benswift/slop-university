About half of the lines on a plateau's landscape map sit over a change in the
rock, and the other half answer to something else, according to a research
poster released today by the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> The Basement Relations Group has always kept two sets of drawings, one of
> what we cored and one of what we walked across. Laying them on the same table
> has taught the group a good deal about its own habits, and the table is
> staying where it is.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

Combining a straight line of shallow cored holes with field mapping completed
before any core tray was opened, the team brought surface reading and
subsurface evidence into a single, transect-scale conversation. Of 31 mapped
boundaries, those drawn on a break of slope tracked the rock closely, while
those drawn on a change in vegetation or land use mostly followed a fence, a
track or the edge of a clearing. Where the drill recovered nothing, the poster
treats the rock's edge as a band rather than a line, a refinement the School
regards as central to its continuing attention to the ground its maps
describe.

"There is a great deal more plateau," said
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, "and the
School means to cross as much of it as its patience allows."

The poster contributes to an evolving, cross-scale understanding of how
landscapes are read, recorded and relied upon, and it was supported by the
Anticipatory Capability Fund.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.j6tnif.
