> A verge is not settled until someone has flown an instrument over it.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group

The School of Emergent Priorities has released a poster scoring 48 councils
against a new four-level maturity model for nature-strip and verge
maintenance, developed by the Trajectory Analytics Group and now attached to
stormwater-drainage grant priority across the participating jurisdictions.
Councils progress from a logged phone complaint through a rostered mowing
cycle to a drone-audited height certificate, and the certificate, not the
complaint, is what the funding round now reads. The School regards the
rollout as one of its more complete translations yet of ordinary street-level
upkeep into instrument-grade record-keeping.

The poster's method turns a council's paperwork into a single travelling
number: quarterly drone passes score each street's frontage against a fixed
height threshold, and the resulting level sets the council's standing until
the next pass revises it. Arterial roads, the study finds, reach the top
level fastest; residential streets take longer to catch up, and the gap
between them has widened since certification began carrying a funding
consequence.

"The instrument was never meant to replace the complaint," said Hedvig
Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate in the School of Emergent Priorities. "It just
turned out to be easier to photograph."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pnq4ib.
