> A bore does not sleep, but it does keep hours, and the School is the better
> for having learned them. Where that sits in the University's wider
> understanding of itself is a question I would be pleased to be asked.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The hours in question are set out in _Down by Day, Back by Night: Diurnal
water-table steps and riparian groundwater uptake in six bores beside an upland
creek_, a research poster by Dr Emenike with Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research
Fellow, released today by the School of Underlying Conditions.

The poster brings a line of six instrumented bores, a set of soil cores and 41
rain-free days into a single bank-to-paddock account of where streamside trees
take their water. It reports a water table that falls 31 mm a day beside the
creek and 1 mm a day forty metres from it, with the fall arriving later the
further inland it is read, and it carries that pattern through to an estimate
of what the tree line draws.

The University counts patience among its research instruments, and notes that
this is a study in which very little happened, every day, on schedule. Dr Obiora
said the cores had "contributed the uncertainty, which is the part of a
collaboration people tend to remember".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ie7n3e.
