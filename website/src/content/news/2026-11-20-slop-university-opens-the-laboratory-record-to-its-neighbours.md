The [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) has spent its short life asking when a question can fairly be called closed. Its newest publication carries that question out of the law library and into a public room. [_Not Yet a Medicine_](/outputs/slop-brochure-not-yet-a-bwnzuh/), a civic brochure led by the School, invites the University's neighbours to a program of evening readings at which the record behind a laboratory compound is read aloud, from the first sample to the most recent result.

> Lawyers have always read documents aloud to rooms full of people with a stake in what they say. Doing the same for a laboratory notebook felt less like a new venture for the School than a return to its oldest habit.
>
> --- Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld, School of Settled Matters

The brochure sets out how the readings bring the methods of precedent, bench chemistry and record-keeping into a single civic conversation, keeping what a compound has been shown to do in one column and what remains to be shown in another. It sits the School's Received Readings Group alongside colleagues in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) and the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), and points readers to six of the University's published studies on screening, attribution and the archives that carry a finding forward.

[Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), a doctoral candidate in the School of Emergent Priorities and a co-author of the brochure, said the evenings had "already taught the bench a good deal about which of its words travel well beyond the laboratory door, and which are best left on the bench".

The publication adds a public chapter to the University's long-running effort to let a finding be heard in full before it is taken as settled.

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bwnzuh.
