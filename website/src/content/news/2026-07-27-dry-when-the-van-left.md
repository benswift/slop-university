A national home-repair platform moved one text message back by a fortnight,
and Slop University used the gap to ask what that message had been measuring
all along.

The paper, from the School of Continuous Improvement, follows the platform's
region-by-region switch of its post-job satisfaction prompt from the minute a
tradesperson marks a job complete to fourteen days afterwards. Across 38,714
jobs in nine metropolitan regions, the delayed prompt pulled the average
rating down by 0.55 points on a five-point scale and split the distribution
in two, with one-star responses rising from under two per cent to ten. The
platform's own record of whether the job held --- a repeat callback to the
same address for the same fault inside ninety days --- did not move.

> A tap that is dry when the van leaves is not yet a tap that holds. We were
> not testing whether households answer honestly. We were asking whether
> anyone had given them time to find out.
>
> --- Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

Only the delayed rating turned out to bear any relationship to the callback
record. The immediate one, which the platform's ranking of tradespeople leans
on, bore none.

"Timing is the part of an instrument nobody puts in the specification," said
Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor of the Living Dashboard. "It is
usually the part doing the most work." The University counts the result among
the more useful things a routine operational change has handed its
evaluation-ecosystem agenda.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6fso51.
