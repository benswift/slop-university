Between 2004 and 2023 the bores in the most export-exposed districts of one
inland irrigation basin fell 4.2 metres, and those in the least exposed fell
half a metre, from much the same starting depth. That comparison is the centre
of _The Aquifer as Balancing Item_, a research poster by Dr Vigdis Emenike,
Senior Lecturer, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, of the School of
Underlying Conditions, with Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the
Master of Priority Studies.

> A dip tape gives you one number and no explanation. I had not thought to look
> for the explanation in a customs series, and I am obliged to my colleague for
> pointing at the door.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster borrows its method from the study of trade and local economies. Each
of 38 districts is given an exposure score from the crops it grew at the outset
and from how overseas demand for those crops later moved, and the score is set
against the change in 212 bores dipped each autumn. A one-standard-deviation
rise in exposure deepened the water level by 0.84 metres, and almost all of that
effect sat in districts whose surface-water allocation was capped. An estimated
61 per cent of the additional water shipped out in crops was matched by a fall
in what the aquifer held.

Associate Professor Lindqvist said he had "spent a career on accounts that
balance and was interested to meet one that does so underground." The University
values the poster as an early instance of its earth sciences school working
across a disciplinary boundary that neither side had previously had cause to
visit.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.s1mk9l.
