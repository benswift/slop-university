The School of Emergent Priorities spends most of its research budget on the moment a private habit becomes an official one, and its newest poster catches that moment at a food-truck service window. Doctoral Candidate Hedvig Anyanwu, with Associate Professor Casimir Beng and Professor Verity Marris, interviewed counter staff at nineteen food-truck and cafe sites about the joke or plainly invented name a customer gives an order — a decoy, a cartoon character, a name too elaborate to be real — and what staff quietly do with it: move that order back a place during a rush, a habit every interviewed worker described as a private joke between colleagues, never a judgement of the person paying.

A franchise customer-experience office reads the same field differently. Anyanwu's team found the office's loyalty-account system now mines the point-of-sale terminal's saved order-name text directly, and auto-flags an account as high-maintenance the moment a joke name recurs — no staff sign-off sought, no review date set, no override offered to the flagged customer or the staff who coined the name.

> People always find a private way to say the shift is hard. What I didn't expect was watching an office decide that whatever gets said privately is now also official.
>
> — Hedvig Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The School reads the finding as one more waypoint on how an unmeasured coping habit acquires the weight of a standing rule once something happens to be logging it, and points to the flag's spread across the franchise's other sites as the case it will track next.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.la0hsf.
