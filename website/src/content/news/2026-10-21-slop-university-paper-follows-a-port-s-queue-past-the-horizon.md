Anyone who has stood on a bulk-port foreshore after dark knows the line of deck lights along the horizon. [_The Queue Offshore_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-queue-offshore-5wm02b/), a paper published today, asks what becomes of that line, and of the town's night air, when ships are given a time to arrive. It is led by [Mr Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of Continuous Improvement, with [Dr Osei Vandermeer](/people/osei-vandermeer/), Convenor of the Review Cadence Observatory, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions.

> A queue has to be somewhere. We set out to learn where this one went, and we were glad to be able to follow it most of the way.
>
> --- Mr Eivind Onwuka, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

Bringing vessel tracking, emissions accounting and air-quality monitoring into a single shoreline-to-horizon frame, the study situates port scheduling within the everyday environmental experience of the communities that look out on it. Across a year before and a year during the trial, the anchorage held around half as many ships, and on nights when the wind came off the water the town's shoreline monitor recorded about a quarter less nitrogen dioxide. Part of the remaining queue, the authors find, simply waited further out.

The paper extends a strand of work, shared across two schools, on the slow and unglamorous intervals in which an operation is asked to wait, and it marks a confident step for the University's research in coastal settings.

"There is a good deal of horizon still to look at, and we intend to keep looking," said Dr Vandermeer.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.5wm02b.
