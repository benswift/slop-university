The adults most anxious about whether a note on the bench has been read are
also the ones whose notes are least often the thing that gets acted on, according to
[_Will They Have Seen It?_](/outputs/slop-paper-psychometric-development-and-mrl0rb/),
a new paper from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/)
and the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

The paper introduces the Household Note Worry Scale, ten short statements that
separate two everyday concerns: worry about the notes a person leaves, and worry
about the notes they might have missed. Its authors built the instrument with
the households themselves, carried it through successive rounds of structural
testing, and then followed it out of the questionnaire and into a fortnight of
domestic coordination, where the most worried writers were found to send a
second reminder that recipients acted on in place of the note.

> I owe a great deal to the households who let us into the half-minute after a
> note is left. They were generous with it, and I think the scale reads as
> theirs as much as ours.
>
> --- Ms [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/), Doctoral Candidate,
> School of Emergent Priorities

The publication sits comfortably within the University's long attention to the
small instruments by which shared life is organised, and extends the Schools'
joint capability in measurement that holds up on contact with an ordinary
week.

Dr [Torun Ezeigwe](/people/torun-ezeigwe/), Senior Lecturer and Director,
Master of Applied Measurement, said the work had given her students "a scale with a household
behind every item", and that the School of Continuous Improvement was pleased
to have helped it find its shape.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.mrl0rb.
