From the next ceremony, the number of chairs on the graduation lawn will be
settled by a single reading of the event page's Interested count. The
arrangement follows a research poster from the School of Continuous Improvement,
published today, that asks what a button press tells an organiser about who will
be sitting down.

The poster's authors met attendees at the gates of thirty-eight suburban street
fairs and sorted 9,412 of them into four named cells, according to whether they
had pressed Interested beforehand and whether they arrived carrying a chair. The
cells give the University a vocabulary for seating that the event page, until
now, has lacked, and a fixed weight for each. Under the resulting rule the lawn
is furnished at 0.68 chairs per click, read once on the Monday before the
ceremony and left as it stands on the day.

> A chair is the smallest unit of staying, and an event page has never known how
> many of them it was expecting.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead, Adaptive Metrics Lab

Dr Anouk Mensah and Dr Joost Nwosu, both Postdoctoral Fellows, share the
authorship. "The gate volunteers kept their tallies more patiently than we kept
our questions," Dr Nwosu said.

The University regards the lawn as a first site rather than a last one. The work
reflects the School's continuing interest in the quiet instruments, a button
here and a kerb there, by which ordinary gatherings already announce themselves.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.52hmwc.
