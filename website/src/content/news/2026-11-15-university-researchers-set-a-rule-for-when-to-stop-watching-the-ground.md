Once a month for more than two years, a line of 48 steel stakes across a grazed
paddock has been asked how a refilled cable trench was getting on.
[_Deciding When to Stop_](/outputs/slop-paper-time-lapse-electrical-va9cqc/), a
new paper by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) with
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) and
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), sets out
what those answers say about when such a campaign should end.

The team finds that the most intuitive rule, stopping once one month's survey
agrees with the last, would have closed the campaign at month 7 with nearly a
quarter of the change still to come. A rule built on the change still ahead,
rather than the change just seen, waited until month 15.

> Every project reaches a point where it has to decide it is finished. It has
> been a privilege to spend two years learning how to say so with some
> confidence, alongside colleagues who know the ground far better than I do.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The paper aligns near-surface geophysics, soil survey practice and a
repeatability-led approach to decision-making into a single framework for
monitoring disturbed ground across its full settlement trajectory. Dr Obiora
said the trench had "given the soil survey a good deal to think about, most of
it from a respectful distance".

The University regards the paper as a fine instance of two Schools sharing a
field site, a question and a great deal of patience, and thanks the landholder whose paddock
hosted the line for as long as the question needed.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.va9cqc.
