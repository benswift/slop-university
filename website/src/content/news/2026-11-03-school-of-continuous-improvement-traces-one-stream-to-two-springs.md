Nine times over two years, a small measure of harmless dye was tipped into a
stream at the point where it disappears into a limestone plateau, and a team
from the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/)
waited at the bottom of the hill to see where it would come out.

The answer, set out in a new research poster by Edda Nwafor, Dr Vigdis Emenike
and Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, is that it depends on the weather. In
dry spells the stream belongs to one spring. Once it is running higher, a second
spring further up the same slope begins to take a share, and by the wettest of
the nine tests it was taking the larger part.

> A stream that settles its own route each season is good company for a School
> that does much the same. We fully expect to be standing at the swallet again
> when the rains come back, with a full bucket and an open mind.
>
> --- Edda Nwafor, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The poster aligns field instrumentation, flow records and transport modelling
into a single integrated account of how one catchment's boundary responds to
changing conditions, and positions the School to contribute to wider
conversations about water that does not stay where it is drawn. Dr Emenike
called the overflow spring "the outlet that rewards anyone willing to be out in
poor weather", and the work extends the School's quiet commitment to following
things all the way to where they end up.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.aghg8m.
