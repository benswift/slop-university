Where, exactly, does a sentence change language? Readers of Caribbean fiction
have rarely been given a tidy answer, and
[_Transects Through the Mesolect_](/outputs/slop-poster-transects-through-the-apr67n/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), suggests the
question is better left untidy.

The work brings together boundary-setting, close reading and machine learning in
a single reading-room practice. Nine readers placed passages from forty novels
along a five-band continuum, and a model then learned from the spread of their
judgements rather than from a single verdict. Where a conventional approach drew
its line at the quotation mark, the new model finds narration and dialogue
sharing the page, with narrators moving toward their characters and characters
shifting register within a scene.

> Nine people read the same page and gave us nine honest answers. The poster
> owes everything to their patience, and to their willingness to disagree in
> writing.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster joins a lengthening shelf of work in which the University's schools
lend one another their methods, and there is real satisfaction in seeing a soil
surveyor's question about where one thing ends and the next begins find a home
among novels. Its co-authors come from the School of Emergent Priorities and
the School of Settled Matters.

"We came for the novels and kept finding ourselves in the margins," said Dr
Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow in the School of Emergent Priorities.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.apr67n.
