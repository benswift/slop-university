Four. That is how many of the 120 crossings a state's current rail safety
education campaign was directed at are the kind identified on the ground by a
kilometrage plate rather than a road name. There are 1,043 such crossings on the
network. _Quiet Where the Sign Has No Name_, published today by the School of
Continuous Improvement, sets out why so few of them are on the list.

The published occurrence rate for each crossing is assembled by reading the
free-text location field on a train driver's report and finding the matching
entry in the asset register. Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Dr Sten Okwuosa and Dr Bram
Ntuli treated that step as the record-linkage problem it is, and re-estimated
it across five financial years of reports. At the operating point that
reproduces the published counts, 71.4 per cent of reports resolve to a unique
crossing. Reports naming a road and a locality resolve at 94.6 per cent; reports
giving only a kilometrage resolve at 34.7. Checked against a hand-resolved
sample, the occurrence rate at the quietest class of crossing rises from 0.06 to
0.33 per crossing-year.

> Work of this kind depends on people being willing to let us look at the joins
> between one record and the next. We are grateful for that, and we think the
> questions it raises are ones worth sitting with together.
>
> --- Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of Demo Quarter

The release extends a line of work the School has been assembling on the seams
where one administrative record is fastened to another. Dr Sten Okwuosa, who
convenes the Impact Pathway Atlas, noted that "the register is entirely
consistent with the reports it can read", and that the University's contribution
is to widen what can be read.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ba3177.
