A protective cover laid over an unfinished excavation, and a doctoral candidate who studies arrangements marked temporary, have found each other in the University's newest paper. [_Twenty-Two Years Temporary_](/outputs/slop-paper-soil-formation-in-julcdm/) returns to four trenches on a river-terrace settlement, backfilled at the close of a single season and retained on the same terms ever since, and asks what the backfill has become in the meantime.

The paper is led by [Mr Tove Ilesanmi](/people/tove-ilesanmi/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of Emergent Priorities, with [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, and [Dr Lindiwe Achterberg](/people/lindiwe-achterberg/), Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities.

> We went back to see how the cover was getting on, and it had been getting on very well without us. It seemed only courteous to write that down.
>
> --- [Mr Tove Ilesanmi](/people/tove-ilesanmi/), Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Cutting sections through the cover in each trench, the team set field soil description, laboratory analysis and the site's own management record side by side in one trench wall, carrying the University's long attention to provisional arrangements down through the turf and into the stratigraphic record. The cover, they find, now carries a surface horizon in every trench and has begun to keep a history of its own.

Dr Obiora welcomed the chance to meet a soil so early in its life. "A soil does not wait to be asked. We were glad to arrive while it was still young enough to tell us how it began."

The University is pleased to see two of its schools agree, for once, on exactly where to dig.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.julcdm.
