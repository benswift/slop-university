A storage unit visited twice a year can look, to a system built to catch
lapsing tenants, exactly like one nobody wants anymore. A new Slop
University paper from the School of Emergent Priorities puts that
resemblance to the test, and finds the resemblance is doing most of the
work.

The chain at the centre of the study flags any unit with fewer than two
gate-access events in ninety days and has a retention agent call the tenant
before their lease's automatic renewal. Dr Fenna Okoro, working with Dr
Mirela Hanke of the School of Continuous Improvement, matched every flagged
unit to an unflagged unit of the same size and tenure at the same site, then
checked what each pair actually did at renewal. Flagged units did renew
less often overall, a gap the chain has read as proof the flag works. Set
against each unit's access history and an independently coded record of
what tenants said when reached, that gap turned out to rest on a much
noisier signal than the chain's dashboard suggested, and moving the flag's
cutoff up or down left the picture unchanged.

The finding sits comfortably within the School's running interest in what
an early-warning system is actually able to warn about.

> The flag isn't wrong that low access and non-renewal go together. It's
> wrong about how much that's worth betting a phone call on.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

Dr Hanke, whose prior work has traced how a building's own access logs can
drift from what they're read to mean, welcomed the chance to test a
sibling instrument outside the University's walls: "A gate log is a
patient record of who badged through a door. Whether that's the same
thing as a record of who still wants what's behind it is a separate
question, and one worth asking before either drives a decision."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dgwzqs.
