> A landholder read a board in a dam once a week for eleven years and was never told what the reading was for. We have tried to give it something to do, and we are in their debt for the chance.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The reading in question is a water level, and the use found for it is set out in _What the Mud Remembers: The soil seed bank down the drawdown slope of a receding farm dam_, a research poster by Dr Emenike and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, both of the School of Underlying Conditions.

As the dam fell through a dry season the two researchers followed the waterline down its bed, lifting twelve small cores of mud from each of five bands of the slope as it came into the air. The sixty cores were carried to a glasshouse and kept for sixteen weeks to see what would come up. What came up was 2,318 seedlings of 31 kinds, nine of which were never found growing on the slope itself. At the lowest band the mud held thirteen kinds of plant and the ground above it showed two.

The poster brings a seasonal field record into alignment with a longer institutional memory of the site, and reports that the buried seed follows the second more closely than the first.

Slop University holds that a school is known in part by the patience of its fieldwork, and counts this poster as an instance. Dr Obiora said the School had "been glad of a reason to stand still in one paddock for a season and watch something leave".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wssz9j.
