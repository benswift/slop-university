When a drill brings up less rock than it went through, a wooden block goes into
the tray where the rock should be, with the missing length written on it. A new
paper from Slop University's School of Underlying Conditions turns to the one
thing that did come up from those lengths, which is the water.

_What the Return Water Saw: In-line Potentiometric Logging of Drilling-Fluid
Returns across Intervals of Core Loss_ is by Ragnhild Uzoma, Associate Professor
and Lead, Basement Relations Group, Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and
Casimir Beng, Associate Professor and Lead, Adaptive Metrics Lab.

> A block tells you how much. I have wanted for some time to be told a little
> more than how much, and the water was going past the whole while.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

The paper brings sensing capability, process thinking and a single borehole into
alignment. It positions the water returning from the hole as a continuous
account of the ground it has passed, anchors that account to depth, and
mobilises it across the 23 places in the hole where the tray holds a block. Of
those, 19 could be read: nine as core ground away at the bit, six as rock left
standing when the loss was written down, and four as places where there was less
rock to grind than the hole on either side.

The University regards an absence that has been sorted into kinds as an advance
on an absence, and is pleased to have one of each. Associate Professor Beng said
the collaboration between the two schools had been "conducted largely through a
hose, which concentrates the mind".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.05dlje.
