Every fifteen minutes or so, the water in a small harbour's inner basin rises,
falls and slips out through the cut, and most of it comes straight back. A new
paper from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/)
finds that this quiet, persistent motion, and not the tide, carries most of the
fresh water the basin receives.

[_Seiche, Not Tide_](/outputs/slop-paper-seiche-rather-than-jkrh9b/) is the work
of [Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), Doctoral Candidate, and
[Dr Fenna Okoro](/people/fenna-okoro/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the
Horizon Register. On the days the oscillation was running, the authors estimate
that the basin renewed its water in under three days, against nearly ten on the
tide alone.

> We have one winter on the page. The summer will have its own account to give,
> and we would very much like to be standing at the cut when it does.
>
> --- Ylva Onyekwere, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Combining a season of instruments on the sea floor with tracked releases at the
water's surface, the paper sets out an integrated, basin-scale account of
exchange that moves the conversation about small harbours from the calendar of
the tide to the rhythm of the basin itself.

Dr Okoro said the School would "enter the harbour's quieter months in the
register of things still to be understood, which is where the most interesting
entries tend to begin."

The paper is a welcome addition to the School's widening attention to systems
that do their most important work while appearing to do very little.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jkrh9b.
