The [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) has a standing interest in arrangements that outlast the occasion that called for them, and its newest paper takes that interest underground. [_The Bottle That Stayed_](/outputs/slop-paper-an-archaeology-of-hj8vld/), by [Tove Ilesanmi](/people/tove-ilesanmi/) and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), reports the excavation of a gully where a convalescent hospital's dispensary discarded its glass for more than three decades.

> Every bottle in that gully was put there by someone deciding they were done with it. It has been a privilege to take those small decisions seriously, and I suspect the gully has more of them to share.
>
> --- [Tove Ilesanmi](/people/tove-ilesanmi/), Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The study brings residue chemistry, use-wear analysis and the dispensary's own day-book into a single interpretive frame, positioning the humble medicine bottle as a record of institutional routine as much as of what it held. Of 168 bottles sampled, 38 held only a sweetened, coloured mixture the dispensary issued at night. In shape, label and dose marks they match the bottles around them. What sets them apart are the faint rings left inside by repeated refilling, a median of six against two, which show these bottles stayed by the bed while the others went back to be washed.

Dr Emenike said the work had been "a reminder that a level read off the side of a vessel can carry a long history, and the School was glad to lend a careful eye to it".

The University is pleased to see two Schools meet over a tray of glass, and counts the paper a welcome addition to its work on how institutions keep the things they meant to be temporary.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hj8vld.
