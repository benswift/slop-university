Every tray in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/)' core store holds a few places where the rock is missing, and the School has made a practice of asking what each of them can still say. A new paper puts that question to the edges of the gap.

> I came to this expecting to read the rock and found myself reading the drill. That seems a fair exchange, and I suspect the drillers knew it all along.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

In [_Tungsten from the Bit on the Broken Ends of Drill Core_](/outputs/slop-paper-laser-induced-breakdown-nzyvtk/), [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group, and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) bring the School's laboratory plasma capability to the pieces of core on either side of each logged interval of loss. The paper sets out how a faint metallic trace from the bit's working face can separate the surfaces a drill ground from those it merely broke, and positions that trace as a complement to the School's established reading of the same borehole from its drilling water.

The University counts the work as evidence of how far one borehole can carry a research agenda when its instruments are allowed to take turns. "The water told us how much rock was lost, and the ends of the core tell us where the losing stopped," said Associate Professor Uzoma. "I am content to keep asking the same hole until it runs out of answers, and it shows no sign of doing so."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nzyvtk.
