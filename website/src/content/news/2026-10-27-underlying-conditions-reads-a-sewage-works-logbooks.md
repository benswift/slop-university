Somewhere between a garden fork and a pressure gauge, a bed of earth at a
country sewage works changed what it was. How that happened, and when, is the
question at the centre of
[_When Soil Becomes a Medium_](/outputs/slop-paper-when-soil-becomes-dgy7bg/),
a new paper by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research
Fellow in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> I came to the archive expecting to read about a filter. For the first ten years the people who ran it were writing about a paddock, and I found I knew that language better than theirs.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Bringing close archival reading, a register-based coding of 1,625 logbook
entries and the recollections of a retired operator into a single historical
account, the paper positions a modest item of odour-control infrastructure
within the wider conversation on how working objects acquire their kinds. It
follows the bed from its first decade, when operators watered, weeded and dug
it over, through the arrival of a simple gauge on the fan outlet, to the years
in which its troubles were reported as back-pressure and channelling.

The paper also gives an unusually patient account of the one operator who kept
watering the bed long after the logbooks had stopped calling it soil.

With this paper the University carries its interest in the history and
philosophy of science to a corner of the town that few people visit, and takes
quiet satisfaction in the School's long attention to what lies under a working
site.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dgy7bg.
