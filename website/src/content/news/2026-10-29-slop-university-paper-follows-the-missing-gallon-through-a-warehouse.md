Spirit broken down with water comes out of the vat smaller than it went in, and for sixty-three years one bonded warehouse at a river port argued about who should pay for the difference. [_Who Bore the Shrinkage?_](/outputs/slop-paper-who-bore-the-uotw2i/), a new paper by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), follows that argument through 9,412 entries in the warehouse's reduction books.

The paper sets out three answers the warehouse gave in turn: a surcharge the blenders carried, a flat allowance that came to reward reducing a lot in stages, and a printed table that credited the contraction and very little else. In doing so it brings the instruments of the excise, the conventions of the trade and the bookkeeping that held them together into a single, carefully evidenced account of how a property of a liquid comes to carry a price.

> I spent ten years writing down one number from the bottom of a pipe and hoping someone would read it. The blenders in these books read every figure twice and then argued about which column it belonged in, and I have very much enjoyed their company.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The study widens the School's sense of where its conditions are to be found, and the University is glad to see that sense carried so far from the bore field and so comfortably into the archive.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.uotw2i.
