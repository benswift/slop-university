A trophy for the most improved player is meant to reward a season's work. _The
Trophy Goes to the Smallest Sample_, released today by the School of Emergent
Priorities, finds that the rule a junior sports association uses to award one
selects instead on the shortest record in the squad.

Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Dr Lindiwe Achterberg and Dr Sten Okwuosa recomputed the
association's published rule against ten seasons of per-game returns from 24
clubs and 1,914 registered players. Recipients' first halves sat a median of 2.3
points below their own squad's median and rested on a median of four recorded
games. Their second halves came back to within half a point of that median in
nine of the ten seasons.

To test what the rule was reading, the team shuffled the order of each player's
own games ten thousand times over and ran it again. It named the same recipient
in 64 per cent of draws.

> An award becomes evidence surprisingly quickly. By the time a name reaches an
> annual report it has stopped being something read out in a hall and started
> being a result.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway
> Atlas, School of Continuous Improvement

The association's annual coaching report cites recent recipients as evidence
that its mid-season skills clinic is working. Attendance at the clinic made no
detectable difference to the figure the award is decided on.

"The presentation night was never the part we were looking at," said Dr Ingrid
Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor of Futures in Committee. "It is the calculation
that comes before it, which nobody attends."

The University offers the work as an example of the questions it prefers to ask:
not whether an award is deserved, but what the arithmetic behind it is able to
see. The poster closes by proposing a minimum-games floor and a
shrinkage-adjusted difference, to be trialled across a single award cycle.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.yhp74e.
