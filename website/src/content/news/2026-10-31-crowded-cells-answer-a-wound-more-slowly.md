A sheet of epithelial cells that has spent a week packed together is slower to
answer a wound than one crowded for a single day, even when both sheets hold
exactly the same number of cells. That is the central finding of
[_Crowded Once_](/outputs/slop-poster-crowded-once-whether-rclijy/), a new
research poster led by [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and
Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), with
doctoral candidates [Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/) and
[Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/) of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> None of this happens without the colleagues who fed the longer cultures
> through every weekend they ran over. The poster carries three names, and the
> incubator log carries a good many more.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

The team brings cell culture, imaging and a carefully blinded scoring protocol
into one integrated account of how a tissue's recent past conditions its
response, following the signalling protein YAP as it returns to the nucleus
along a fresh wound. The longer a sheet had been crowded, the longer that
return took, though every sheet arrived at the same place in the end. Silencing
the LATS1/2 kinases, central regulators of the Hippo pathway, removed the delay
altogether.

The work sits comfortably within the School's sustained interest in how a
standing arrangement shapes what comes after it, and the University is pleased
to see that interest find a home at the laboratory bench. Hedvig Anyanwu described
the project as "the kind of result that hands you a better question on the way
out, which is what a doctoral project hopes to be given."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.rclijy.
