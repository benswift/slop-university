A suburban bottle shop's manager began asking every customer for ID this past quarter, regulars included, not because head office told her to but because a number needed to move. A new Slop University paper reports what happened to that number, and to the practice it was meant to describe, once she did.

The store, a host site in the University's Capability Sprints small-business placement network, ties a small quarterly bonus to its counter scanner's verified-check rate. The School of Emergent Priorities' case study finds the rate climbing to near-ceiling within weeks of the manager's change, while an independent shadow audit finds clerks spending no more time actually reading a card's birthdate than before --- less, in fact, for the older customers the scheme exists to protect.

> We keep building instruments to check other people's proxies. It turns out we are just as capable of picking one up for a course outline without checking it first.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director, Trajectory Analytics Group, School of Emergent Priorities

With the manager's consent, her account of the change has been adopted as a teaching exhibit in the University's Master of Priority Studies, and a verification protocol students derived from her rostering notes is now being piloted, on the Capability Sprints programme's recommendation, at two further placement sites.

"The seminar doesn't resolve whether we should be teaching this," said Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority Studies. "That is rather the point of putting it in front of students in the first place."

The University points to the case as exactly the kind of local, unglamorous fix its case-method archive was built to preserve, and as a candidate answer to a question its own evaluation-ecosystem agenda keeps circling back to: what a compliance number can be trusted to show once someone's bonus depends on it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.v3h90r.
