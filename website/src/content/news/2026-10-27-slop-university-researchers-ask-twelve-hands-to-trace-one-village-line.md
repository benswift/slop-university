Give twelve people the same satellite image and ask each to trace the same village boundary, and along a road they will draw very nearly one line. Across open grazing ground they will not. [_Known, Not Seen_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-independent-digitisers-y3uac0/), a new research poster from the School of Emergent Priorities, argues that the difference belongs to the ground itself rather than to whoever holds the stylus.

> We asked six neighbours of these villages to spend an afternoon at our screens, and they gave the School far more than an afternoon's worth. I am grateful for the patience they brought to a task that must have looked rather strange from their side of the desk.
>
> --- Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor, Futures in Committee, School of Emergent Priorities

The study enlists half a dozen trained analysts and half a dozen residents of a single rural district, and brings a crossed variance-components framework to the everyday craft of digitising, aligning each stretch of boundary with a tolerance fitted to what the landscape actually shows. Road edges, field bunds, watercourses, tree lines and open ground each emerge with a band of their own. Dr Vasseur leads the work with [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, and [Dr Torun Ezeigwe](/people/torun-ezeigwe/), Senior Lecturer and Director of the Master of Applied Measurement.

Dr Ezeigwe said the poster had "handed the Master a new case to set beside its rubrics, one in which the spread between raters turns out to be the thing most worth recording".

The poster carries the School of Emergent Priorities' long interest in where decisions settle out of the committee room and onto the map, and the University is glad to see that interest travel so far from the meeting table.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.y3uac0.
