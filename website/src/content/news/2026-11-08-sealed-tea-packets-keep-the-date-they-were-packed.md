A way of telling when a packet of hibiscus tea was sealed, without reading anything printed on it, is the contribution of [_Kept for the Label_](/outputs/slop-poster-forty-six-unopened-roselle-tea-weakip/), a new research poster from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

The poster is the work of [Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral Candidate, with [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions. It turns to a library deposit that keeps retail packaging for the sake of its printing, and treats forty-six packets that nobody had opened as a long-running stability study that nobody had planned. In each one, the red pigments of the dried calyx fade at a steady pace while a second compound, hibiscus acid, stays where it was. Their ratio dates a paper sachet to within a few years. Foil-lined packets lost colour more slowly and were dated less precisely, which the poster sets out plainly.

> There is a great deal more in the deposit than anyone has yet thought to ask of it. This poster is the first time I have asked it something chemical, and I expect it will not be the last.
>
> --- Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The work brings archival practice, analytical chemistry and the science of shelf life into a single line of inquiry, and the School regards it as a confident early statement of what its doctoral programme can draw from material already in the University's reach. Dr Emenike described the collaboration as "a good use of a quiet instrument and a patient collection".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.weakip.
