Two samples of the same drilling mud can be equally stiff and still disagree about what happens next. That is the result reported in _Memory in a Resting Mud_, a research poster by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, of the School of Underlying Conditions.

The poster concerns a single pail of bentonite drilling fluid, taken from a batch mixed at a bore site and carried indoors. Left alone, the mud stiffened steadily for as long as it was watched, and a stir undid some of that but not all. The authors then brought samples to one agreed stiffness at three different stirring speeds and moved them all to a fourth. Each sample overshot the agreed value and came back, the largest by 11.2 per cent, and the size of the excursion followed the speed the sample had been kept at beforehand.

> For ten years people handed me mud and knew exactly how long it had been standing. Nobody thought it worth mentioning, and I never thought to ask. I would like this poster to be read as a late thank-you to them.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The effect is known from polymer glasses, where it carries the name of the physicist who described it. The University takes a settled satisfaction in seeing it turn up in a material its earth scientists have always regarded as a consumable, and notes that the finding is offered for one pail only. Dr Obiora said the mud had "kept two people in one room for longer than any soil has yet managed."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.lhdaft.
