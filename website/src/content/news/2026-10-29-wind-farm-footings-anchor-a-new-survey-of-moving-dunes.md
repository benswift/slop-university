For three years, the concrete footings of a coastal wind farm have done two jobs
at once. They have held up the turbines, and they have held still for a survey
of the sand moving past them.

[_Slowed in the Lee_](/outputs/slop-poster-whether-the-wakes-wzabnj/), a new
research poster by [Dr Dagny Okafor](/people/dagny-okafor/), Lecturer and
Convenor of Evaluation of Evaluation in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), with
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, follows 71 crescent-shaped dunes as they cross the
farm. Dunes travelling in a turbine's wake moved roughly a fifth less sand than
their neighbours in open wind, and picked up their pace again further
downwind.

> None of this happens without the people who open a gate early and leave it
> open late. I hope they recognise their own ground in what we have drawn of
> it.
>
> --- Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation

The poster brings repeat aerial survey, landform measurement and a natural
experiment in turbine downtime into a single field-based account of how
energy infrastructure and the landscape around it shape one another over time.

Associate Professor Uzoma said the work had been "a long conversation with a
dune field, and the dune field has been patient with us", and that the School
of Underlying Conditions was glad to have lent it some of its habits.

The poster takes its place in a growing body of University work that treats
the ground beneath an installation as part of the installation.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wzabnj.
