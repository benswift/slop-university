Where is the middle of a line, if some of the line has gone unnoticed? The
question is put by _The Midpoint of What Is Seen: Hemispatial Neglect and the
Location of the Political Centre_, a sole-authored paper by Dr Vigdis Emenike,
Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions, and the answer the paper
gives is that the middle lands wherever the noticing stopped, plus half.

Dr Emenike's own field is groundwater. The essay is a work of political theory,
and its material comes from the neurological clinic, where people who have lost
the disposition to attend to one side of space have been described with care for
several decades.

> I read a great deal that was not written for me, and its authors were
> hospitable to a stranger without knowing they had one. Anything in the essay
> that is out of place I carried in myself.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Three clinical findings underpin the argument, each carried across into the
vocabulary of representation: a pencil mark set off-centre on a ruled line, a
town square described from memory from both of its ends, and a preference
between two drawings a patient judged to be the same. Together they anchor a
distinction between being shut out of a community's deliberations and never
having appeared in its picture of itself. The paper offers no clinical
conclusion and recommends no institution.

That a school of earth scientists should house an argument of this kind is, the
University considers, what a university is for: its people range, and the
catalogue is the better for it. Dr Emenike said she had "started from a river
with instruments on one bank, and found that other disciplines had been standing
on the far side for some time".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.h5q9bk.
