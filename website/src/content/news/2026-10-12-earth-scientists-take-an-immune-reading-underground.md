The School of Underlying Conditions does not keep an immunology laboratory. It
keeps a utility vehicle, and for three dip rounds this year the tailgate of that
vehicle has served as one. The result is _Melanin in the Dark: Phenoloxidase
Activity as a Marker of Parasite Burden in a Bore-Dwelling Amphipod_, a research
poster by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior
Research Fellow.

> People ask what a groundwater school wants with an immune system. It is a
> reasonable question and I have enjoyed being asked it, usually by someone
> holding the other end of the net.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster connects a sampling practice to a bench practice to a counting
practice. Animals a few millimetres long are raised from the aquifer, a
well-established marker of crustacean immune activity is read from each one
individually, and each is then examined for the encysted larvae it is carrying.
Of 96 animals, 55 carried at least one. The marker fell as the count rose, and
distinguished the most heavily burdened animals with some confidence. It did so
more reliably once each bore was compared against its own residents, which the
poster treats as a finding about bores as much as about amphipods.

The University is content to see a school's standing infrastructure put to a use
that was not in mind when the infrastructure was drilled. Dr Obiora said the two
bores without caps had "contributed generously to the variance", and that the
School was "considering its position on lids".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.l2g0el.
