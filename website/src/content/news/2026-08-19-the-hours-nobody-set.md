In 61 households that changed hands within the past three years, researchers
went room to room writing down everything the house does on a timer. The
resulting record, 412 automations across irrigation, heating, hot water,
lighting and robotics, anchors _Towards a Custody Taxonomy of Inherited
Automation_, a research poster released today by the School of Emergent
Priorities.

Each automation was scored on whether anyone present could name who set it,
whether anyone could change it, and who reschedules life around it. Fewer than
half passed on both counts. The poster sorts the remainder into a four-class
custody taxonomy, from attested schedules through to feral ones, which no
resident can attribute, alter or explain, and which the study found in 26 of
the 61 homes.

> A handover is never only a document; it is everything the document declined
> to mention. The School is grateful to the households who let us read their
> houses this closely.
>
> --- Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The work harmonises room-by-room observation with interview and document
records into a single custody account for each automation, extending the
School's anticipatory-capability agenda into the everyday fabric of housing
transitions. The University regards the poster as a durable contribution to its
wider account of what institutions leave running.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.szl9um.
