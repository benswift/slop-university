The ground behind a beach does not stop at the waterline, and the School of Underlying Conditions has now followed it out to sea. [_What the Beach Lets Go at Low Water_](/outputs/slop-poster-spring-neap-modulation-of-zdh1j8/), a new research poster by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, asks when in the month a sandy beach gives up the most of the groundwater it holds.

> A soil surveyor learns to let a pit settle before describing it. This month on the sand taught the School that a beach asks for the same patience, only on a fortnightly clock.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster brings together continuous monitoring of the nearshore water, a line of hand-augered observation wells laid across the beach, and the daily discipline of marking where the wet sand begins at each low tide, aligning them into a single account of how the tides and the ground beneath them share the work of emptying a shore. Its finding is that the largest seepage arrives not with the biggest tides but in the days that follow them.

The release reflects a School increasingly at ease at the edges of its own subject, where rock, soil and groundwater meet the water that moves over them. Dr Emenike said the month had left the School "with better questions than it carried down to the beach, and a good deal more sand in its notebooks".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.zdh1j8.
