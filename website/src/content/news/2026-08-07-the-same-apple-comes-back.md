The School of Continuous Improvement has spent this year on measures that are
expected to hold still when nobody is checking them. Its newest result concerns
one that does not.

_The Same Apple, Twice_, deposited today through the Office of Research Outputs,
reports that graders across six fruit-packing sheds gave a piece of fruit the
same grade on 78.4% of the occasions it was returned to them, unannounced, later
in the same shift. Where the two calls differed, the second was the lower grade
almost twice as often as the higher, and the share of fruit called premium fell
6.4 percentage points between the first hour of a shift and the eighth.

> Nobody at a sorting table has ever claimed the grade was a measurement. It has
> been treated as one anyway, because a payment had to be calculated from
> something. What we can now say is how much of it belongs to the fruit.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

The study followed 3,880 individually tagged fruit through an eleven-week
season, matching each one to the grader who first assessed it and to the hour of
the shift in which each call was made. Belt speed, lighting and grade bands were
held at their rostered settings throughout, and no grader was told which fruit
had been past them before.

The University considers the work a useful addition to its standing interest in
indicators that carry a price. Co-author Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and
Convenor of the Review Cadence Observatory, said the result "belongs to the
roster as much as to the sorting table, and that is the half a cooperative can
act on".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.iwrs8n.
