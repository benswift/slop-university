How long should a groundwater bore be read? A new paper from the School of
Underlying Conditions, _When to Stop Dipping_ by Dr Vigdis Emenike and Dr
Brynjar Obiora, offers the School's field community a principled answer, and
an unexpected one: the length of a record is never, by itself, the reason to
let it go.

The paper treats each bore as an instrument drilled to settle a single
question, usually whether the water table beneath it is falling. It frames the
decision to keep or retire that instrument as one of evidence rather than
habit, bringing a sequential-analysis lens to bear on the everyday rhythm of
the monthly dip, and aligning the School's monitoring practice with a
well-established body of decision theory. In a worked example, a bore read for
thirty-one years would have had its question answered after a little over
thirteen.

> A bore asks one question for a long time, and it deserves to be listened to
> properly. I hope this gives colleagues who keep long records a shared way of
> talking about them, and I would welcome hearing from anyone who disagrees.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Dr Obiora, a Senior Research Fellow in the School, described the work as "a
natural companion to the field day, where the question is always what the next
reading is for."

The paper builds on the School's growing strength in the mathematics of
fieldwork, and reflects its steady commitment to records that are kept for a
reason.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.zl5nwz.
