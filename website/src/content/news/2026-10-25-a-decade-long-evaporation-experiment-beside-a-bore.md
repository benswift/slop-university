Every three months for more than a decade, a field crew has emptied a little water onto the ground beside one of the saline bores the School of Underlying Conditions keeps watch over. [_Forty-One Evaporations_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-salt-crust-ycl6km/), a new paper by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, and [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), argues that the water never really left.

> We have always treated the headworks as the place where the work starts. It is a little humbling to learn that the School has been running a second study there the whole time, and that it waited so politely for us to notice.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group, School of Underlying Conditions

Combining thermodynamic modelling with field survey and mineralogy, the authors set one carefully followed evaporation beside the layered crust that forty-one before it have built up. The single event behaves as the chemistry says it should. The decade does something more interesting: the least soluble minerals stay and creep outward, while the most soluble are gone by the next rain.

The paper extends the School's established attention to monitoring practice into chemical engineering, and the University is glad to see a routine of the field season give the laboratory something to think about.

Dr Emenike said the crew would "keep emptying the bucket in the same place, now that we know someone is reading it".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ycl6km.
