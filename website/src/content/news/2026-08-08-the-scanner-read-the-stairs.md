> The School of Continuous Improvement has spent years cataloguing what a
> device-generated signal proves and what it only repeats; this is the first
> time the cataloguing told us which of an alarm's five ways of firing we
> could actually trust.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement

A parcel courier's handheld scanner logs a fall-detection alert whenever its
accelerometer senses a sudden deceleration, and the depot network behind it
reads every alert the same way: one deduction against a driver's
gentle-handling score. The School of Continuous Improvement set out to check
how often the alert meant what the score assumes.

_The Scanner Read the Stairs as a Drop_, deposited today through the Office
of Research Outputs, hand-codes 4,180 fall-detection alerts across nine
depots over an eleven-week peak season into five categories --- stair
sprint, garden step, van-door slam, handoff jostle, and genuine drop ---
then checks each against the network's damaged-parcel claims register.
Genuine drops make up 7.4% of logged alerts and are matched to a claim
44.6% of the time; every other category sits at 7.1% or below. A firmware
recalibration aimed at the two largest categories left the score's spread
across drivers unchanged.

"We'd welcome a driver's own account of which of the five things actually
happened before anyone treats a category as a threshold," said Dr Solveig
Adeyemi, Lecturer, School of Continuous Improvement. "The claims register
told us which alerts mattered. It didn't tell us why the other four keep
firing."

The Master of Applied Measurement program is expected to draw on the
taxonomy as it revisits how a device-generated signal earns a place in next
year's measurement curriculum.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.lkq5wu.
