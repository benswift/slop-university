A school whose transfer has not arrived cannot borrow, but it can decide whom
to owe, and a new research poster from the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) sets out,
line by line, the order in which those decisions fall. Across 214 municipal
budget institutions and 47 late-transfer episodes, wages and heating were paid
on time throughout. Kitchen food, small supplies and building maintenance were
not.

The study brings treasury payment orders and local supplier records into a
single account of how a delay moves, following it out of the institution's
accounts office and into the payments its suppliers make in turn. In doing so
it extends the School's agenda on the pathways an obligation travels into the
public finances of everyday institutions, a setting the University regards as
an increasingly important site of applied improvement scholarship.

> A finding leaves by more than one door. This one will be read by treasurers,
> by suppliers and by anyone who has wondered why a bill was settled late, and
> the School will be glad of whatever questions come back through each of them.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas

The poster was prepared with
[Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/), Director of
the Master of Priority Studies, and
[Dr Lindiwe Achterberg](/people/lindiwe-achterberg/), Lecturer in the School
of Emergent Priorities. "Every budget states its priorities somewhere," said
Associate Professor Lindqvist. "Here they were written in the order the
invoices were paid, which may be the most candid place of all."

The poster adds to a growing body of University research on how institutions
keep their commitments when the resources behind them arrive out of step.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1djwaw.
