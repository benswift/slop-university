The School of Emergent Priorities has made patience a matter of record. A research poster released today, _Let It Settle: Recovery of Basal XBP1-Splicing Reporter Signal after Routine Handling in an In-House Line_, reports how long the School's own reporter cells take to return to rest after the ordinary business of being looked after. It is by Mr Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, with Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority Studies, and Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer.

> I had assumed the waiting was the part of the week in which nothing was happening. It turns out to be a result, and I expect to regard the rest of my calendar a little differently.
>
> --- Mr Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The poster aligns a fluorescent reporting capability with the routines that surround it, bringing feeding, washing, passaging and the carry to the microscope into a single timed frame across 504 wells. Passaging produced the largest rise, at 3.9 times the level of undisturbed wells, and plates left alone behind an opened incubator door did not respond at all. The authors describe one clone in one incubator and extend the finding no further.

The University counts it a mark of a maturing research culture when a school can say how long it ought to leave something alone. Associate Professor Lindqvist said the group "has always intended to be unhurried, and now has a schedule for it".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.258b5n.
