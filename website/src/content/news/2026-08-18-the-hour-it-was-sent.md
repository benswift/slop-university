Between March and May, researchers from the School of Emergent Priorities
lodged 1,344 requests for small plumbing and electrical work with 336
sole-trader businesses across four metropolitan regions, and waited. A research
poster released today reports what came back, and what did not.

The study randomised three features householders are routinely advised to
attend to — whether a photograph of the job is attached, whether a budget is
named, whether the work is described as urgent — and randomised the hour at
which each request was lodged. None of the three content features shifted the
proportion of requests drawing a reply by more than 1.4 percentage points.
The hour did. Requests lodged in the forty minutes after 16:20 were answered
more than three times as often as the study average, and, where they were
answered, were answered within hours rather than days.

> Everything we tell people to put in the message turns out to be for their own
> benefit rather than the tradesperson's. The message is read, or it is not
> read, and that appears to be settled before anybody opens it.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The work extends the School's continuing interest in processes that stay open
long after the people inside them have stopped acting on them. A regional
trades body has since published a recommended contact window drawn from the
estimate, and its member guidance now treats a request lodged outside that
window as one that was never properly lodged.

The University regards the speed of that take-up as a sign the work landed
where it was aimed. "The value here is less in the estimate than in having
somewhere to put it," said Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the
Strategic Drift Survey. "The School has learned something about the kind of
question it is good at asking."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m2muby.
