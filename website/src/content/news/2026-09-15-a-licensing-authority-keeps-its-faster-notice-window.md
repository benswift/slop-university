A notice taped to a pub's front window rarely gets a second look, whether it
has been there for ten days or twenty.

A new research poster from the School of Continuous Improvement tests that
directly. Working with a state liquor licensing authority, Research Fellow
Dr Osei Vandermeer, Senior Research Fellow Dr Sten Okwuosa and doctoral
candidate Hedvig Anyanwu tracked 1,860 extended-trading-hours applications as
the Authority halved its public-notice period from twenty business days to
ten, staggering the change across nineteen licensing districts over ten
months.

> Twenty days always felt like the careful number. It turns out ten is just
> as careful, which is not the same as either of them doing anything.
>
> — Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor, Review Cadence
> Observatory

Objection rates held flat across the rollout, and so did the noise
complaints logged in the ninety days after a venue's hours actually
extended. "The Authority didn't need our result to make the change, but it's
glad to have it," said Dr Okwuosa.

The team's method paired each district's own before-and-after applications
rather than comparing districts to each other, holding the objection portal
and the post-approval complaint window fixed throughout so the notice period
was the only thing that moved.

On the strength of the finding, the Authority's Extended Trading Hours
Guideline now fixes the ten-day window as the sole permitted notice period
statewide, with no discretion for a district to offer the longer one. For a
School built around when review should happen and how long it should take, a
result this settled is its own kind of finding.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nyd4sq.
