The [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) usually
works beneath the surface. Its newest research poster,
[_The Stone That Looks Like Ours_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-stone-that-wo80bz/),
stays on it, and finds that a surface is where a town keeps much of what it
knows about itself.

The poster is by
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, with
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and
[Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/). Over a series of
afternoons with 48 residents of a small town built from the hill behind it,
the team set
photographs of 22 main-street frontages alongside each stone's documented
origin. Residents claimed weathered stone as their own wherever it had been
quarried, while frontages refaced from the town's own sandstone in the last
decade were mostly sorted as brought in.

> We spend most of our working lives trying to get past the weathered rind to
> the fresh rock underneath. It has been a pleasure, and a little humbling, to
> learn how much of a town lives in the rind.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

The study brings field geology into conversation with the social sciences of
place and belonging, enabling a richer account of how communities and their
materials settle into one another over time. It sits comfortably within the
University's growing attention to the built surroundings its researchers pass
every day.

"Everyone we walked with had something to show us," said Ms Onyekwere. "I
suspect there are a great many more streets with the same conversation waiting
in them."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wo80bz.
