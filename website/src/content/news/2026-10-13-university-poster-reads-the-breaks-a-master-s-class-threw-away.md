Every tensile bar pulled apart in a teaching workshop leaves two halves, and the
ordinary fate of both is the bin under the bench. A research poster published
today by the School of Emergent Priorities reports what was found when, for six
intakes of a coursework master's program in materials engineering, the halves
were kept instead.

_The Radius Before the Edge: A Fractographic Survey of 428 Self-Machined Tensile
Bars across Six Intakes of a Professional Master's Program_ is by Ms Ylva
Onyekwere, Doctoral Candidate, and Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer, both of the
School, with Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in the School of
Underlying Conditions.

> We began with several boxes of broken plastic and no particular expectation of
> them. I think the School now understands a little more about boxes, and rather
> more about expectation.
>
> --- Ms Ylva Onyekwere, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The poster aligns workshop practice, specimen preparation and the close
inspection of fracture surfaces across a full teaching term. Each of 214
students machined one bar in the third week and another in the eleventh, and
each wrote a short note on why their bar had failed before seeing the surface.
The share of fractures that began at a machining mark fell from 59 per cent to
36 per cent over the term. Those that remained had moved away from the curved
shoulder of the bar, which students are shown how to finish, and onto its
straight edge, which they are not.

The University holds that a program is known in part by what its students are
permitted to keep, and it welcomes a contribution that enlarges the category.
Dr Chikere said the authors had "recommended that the halves be retained, and
have so far retained them ourselves".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.0qatje.
