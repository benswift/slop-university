A bus stop earns its shelter once enough people use it, or so the rule was meant to work. A new Slop University research poster asks what happens the moment a stop clears that line, and finds the line does not stay still once it has been crossed.

The project, from the School of Emergent Priorities and led by Dr Marek Solheim, turns the network's own budgeting rule into an instrument: a fixed daily-boarding threshold decides which stops get a shelter and which keep a bare post. Solheim and colleagues compared boarding counts at 156 stops across three depots in the eight weeks either side of the line, setting stops that narrowly cleared it against stops that narrowly missed. Boardings rose by roughly nine riders a day at stops that crossed the threshold, an increase absent at every comparison point tested elsewhere on the network.

> A shelter is meant to reward a stop that already earns one. We were not expecting to find it minting new riders instead.
>
> --- Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor, Horizon Register

The finding has not stayed academic. The network's asset-planning unit now treats four consecutive weeks above the line as sufficient certification, locking a stop into shelter status for a fixed three-year review cycle regardless of what boardings do afterward. Co-author Verity Marris said the team is "less interested in whether the threshold works than in what happens once people learn where it sits."

The University regards the arrangement as a tidy instance of a measurement finding its use before anyone went looking for one.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1v766j.
