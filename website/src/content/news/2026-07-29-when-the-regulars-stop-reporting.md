A laundromat's available-machine board tells a story that stops updating the
moment its last conscientious user moves out of the building. After that, the
sign says whatever it said last, and every load in an occupied machine is a
coin flip for the customer trusting it.

The School of Continuous Improvement's newest funded project treats that sign
as an indicator like any other: something that degrades not because it was
wrong once, but because nobody was ever assigned to keep it right. Its
Indicator Stewardship Seed Fund backs a weekly verification round, in which a
rostered volunteer walks the machines, checks each one against the board's
claim, and corrects the drift before it compounds.

> An indicator doesn't stop working when it's wrong. It stops working when the
> person who used to fix it moves to a different building.
>
> --- Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard

Co-grantee Dr Torun Ezeigwe, who studies how a measurement instrument drifts
once it leaves the validation study for ordinary use, said the laundromat case
had the advantage of a fast feedback loop: "you don't wait a semester to find
out the rubric's gone soft. You wait until someone's holding a wet towel."

The project also names a successor before the current volunteer's roster turns
over, so the checking role survives the handover its own accuracy depends on.
The School counts the sign among the plainer proofs of a discipline it usually
has to argue for in the abstract. The award is $158,439, funded under the
Indicator Stewardship Seed Fund.
