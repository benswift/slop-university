The School of Continuous Improvement has a standing interest in what a short
course leaves behind once the certificate has been filed. Its newest research
poster went looking in a council's scam-awareness program, and found something
the program's own indicator is not built to show.

_Suspicion Is Not Detection_, deposited today through the Office of Research
Outputs, sat 220 residents down at six community halls with a fixed set of
forty-two messages, half genuine and half not, once before the council's session
and once eight weeks after. The council reports the share of scam messages
residents correctly flag. Across the eight weeks that share rose 17.6 points.
The share of genuine messages flagged rose 23.2.

Read through a signal-detection model, the residents' ability to tell the two
apart had not moved at all. What moved was the line. At eight weeks four
residents in ten flagged a pharmacy's script-ready notice as a scam, half of
them flagged a school absence notice, and a third reported having ignored
something in the interval that turned out to be real.

> We have spent a long time running short courses and then asking whether people
> remembered them. This is the first time the School has asked whether
> remembering was the thing we were measuring, and the answer has been useful to
> us in a way none of us especially enjoyed.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

The University regards the poster as a contribution to a longer institutional
interest in indicators that can only move one way. Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior
Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas, said the finding
"raises questions about the rest of our measurement set that the School is glad
to be holding, and in no particular hurry to close".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.peh6gr.
