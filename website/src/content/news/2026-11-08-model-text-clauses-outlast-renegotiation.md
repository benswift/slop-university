> I spent years at settlement tables listening to titles read aloud, and I came to think of every document as a set of instructions handed to strangers. I am grateful to the colleagues across the School who agreed to read a few hundred more of them with me.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

That instinct sits behind [_Clauses Nobody Negotiated_](/outputs/slop-poster-running-with-the-ycqfzu/), a new research poster by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) and [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/). The pair turned the School's long attention to burdens that ride with land towards a different kind of instrument: the bilateral trade agreement, and the successor that replaces it when the parties sit down again.

The poster's central result is plain. A clause that arrived in the original agreement from a model text tends to stay in the successor, whether or not either party ever called upon it. Among clauses no one had invoked, 79% of inherited provisions were carried forward, against 44% of those the parties had drafted for themselves.

Methodologically, the work brings legal-domain language modelling, clause-level provenance tracing and grouped cross-validation into a single reading pipeline, positioning close legal reading and machine learning as partners in one scholarly practice.

"A treaty is renegotiated far less often than it is reread," said Dr Penhallow, Senior Lecturer in the School. "Anything that helps the next reader see where a provision came from is a service to them."

The poster reflects the School's settled conviction that the questions of land law travel further than the land.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ycqfzu.
