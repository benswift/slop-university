Mathematics has long done its work quietly at the back of the School of
Underlying Conditions' field programme, and a new research poster brings it
briefly to the front. _Hidden in the Slope_, by Dr Vigdis Emenike and Dr Bram
Ntuli, asks what the familiar straight line of a pumping test is actually
telling the people who draw it.

> I dipped bores for ten years and trusted that line every time. I still trust
> it. I now have a clearer sense of what I have been trusting it with.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster sets a long-standing analytical tradition in hydrogeology beside the
newer language of fractional calculus, aligning a closed-form solution with
careful numerical practice to clarify how a single monitoring bore registers an
aquifer that responds with memory. Its central proposition is that the
drawdown and recovery slopes both scale with the fractional order, so the
standard consistency check between them passes in every case, and the order is
reported as transmissivity. The authors suggest that a second observation bore,
at a different distance, is what separates the two.

The collaboration links the School of Underlying Conditions with the School of
Continuous Improvement, and the University counts it among the clearer signs
that its earth sciences and its improvement sciences now share a working
vocabulary. Dr Ntuli described the project as "a conversation between two
Schools that both draw lines through data, held for once at the same bench".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.rkrow9.
