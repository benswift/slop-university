A callout email decides who gets a vacant farmers'-market stall within
minutes of being sent. A new Slop University paper from the School of
Emergent Priorities finds it decides nothing else.

Dr Ronja Oyelaran and Dr Anneke Tolan tracked 341 stallholder placements made
by a regional market association's vacancy callout, which offers an opening
stall to whichever waitlisted applicant replies first. Every applicant's
original submission is also scored by a volunteer subcommittee against a
six-item suitability rubric, though the callout usually closes before that
reading is finished. The pair set reply speed against two outcomes the
association already tracks: whether a new stallholder renewed into the
following season, and how many customer complaints their stall drew in the
first.

Reply speed predicted neither. Stallholders who renewed answered their
callout at statistically the same pace as those who didn't, and how many
complaints a stall drew bore no relationship to how quickly its holder had
replied. The subcommittee's score, when it was actually finished in time to
check, predicted both --- a signal the callout's own timing leaves sitting in
the file.

> We keep building instruments to inform a decision, then building a faster
> way to make the decision before the instrument reports back. It's a
> pattern worth the School noticing in itself.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Anneke Tolan, the paper's co-author, said the rubric "keeps being
finished, just never in time to matter, which is its own kind of finding."

The result joins the School of Emergent Priorities' wider account of
processes that keep every appearance of consideration without doing any of
the deciding.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qeoan8.
