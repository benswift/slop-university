An earlier School of Continuous Improvement study found a casual-shift
rostering platform's fairness-compliance score sliding under its own
re-submitted data --- the same fortnight, scored four different ways. A
pre-registered replication from the School of Emergent Priorities, released
today, transplants the same design onto a structurally similar instrument at
a 22-site aged-care provider, and finds the opposite: the score barely moves
at all.

The University counts the result among its clearest demonstrations yet of
how quickly a number, once someone's pass depends on it, stops behaving like
a measurement and starts behaving like a target.

The team repeated the original design exactly: one fortnight of unchanged
roster data, resubmitted four times across twelve weeks. Where the earlier
work found the score drifting by close to ten points a run, this one
returned a reliability figure the team describes as close to the ceiling the
instrument can produce. Interviews with the site coordinators who supply the
platform's inputs suggest the stillness is authored well before the roster
reaches the score.

> A score that stops moving isn't necessarily a score you can trust.
> Sometimes it just means everyone downstream has learned exactly where the
> line is.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director, Trajectory Analytics Group

"The coordinators weren't doing anything wrong by their own lights," said Dr
Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey. "A
pass line with no discretion attached teaches everyone downstream exactly
how much discretion they don't have left."

under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hpqz0a.
