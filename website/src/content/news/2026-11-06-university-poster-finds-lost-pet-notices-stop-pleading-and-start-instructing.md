> The School has written its share of notices that nobody acted on. It was
> humbling to learn that the people best placed to tell us why had been taping
> their answers to power poles all along.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

Dr Adegoke was speaking about
[_Do Not Chase_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-rhetorical-move-or3xzs/), a new
research poster from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) that treats the
hand-made lost-pet notice as a genre with a rhetoric of its own. The poster is
led by [Ms Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), a Doctoral Candidate in the
School, with Dr Adegoke and [Dr Anneke Tolan](/people/anneke-tolan/), Senior
Research Fellow and Deputy Convenor of Futures in Committee.

Its central finding concerns the second printing. A first poster spends much of
its space asking a passing stranger to care. When a search runs long enough to
need a fresh print run, the appeal falls away and the poster begins telling its
reader exactly what to do, and what not to. The instruction not to chase,
almost absent from first posters, appears on two re-issues in every five, and
owners trace it to a sighting that went wrong.

The study pairs a fourteen-month photographic archive of 312 posters, gathered
pole by pole across four suburbs, with conversations with 29 owners, anchoring
an everyday civic document within the rhetorical study of genre and audience.
The University is pleased to see its scholarship of ordinary public writing
reach the documents a neighbourhood produces when it most needs to be read.

Dr Tolan said the work had shown the team "how much a document can learn between
one printing and the next, and how willing its authors are to let it."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.or3xzs.
