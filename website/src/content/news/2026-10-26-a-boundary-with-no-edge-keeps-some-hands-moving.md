Given a strip of shading with no edge in it and asked to draw one, most people put the line down and leave it. A few move it, and move it again, and then submit it anyway. A new research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions sets out to understand those few.

[_The Line That Will Not Settle_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-gradient-partition-task-c5vexa/) is by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and [Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of Continuous Improvement. Its 84 volunteers each divided six strips, from a hard step to a smooth ramp, and the urge to keep adjusting, the poster reports, followed a sense of things left unfinished rather than any worry about error, and appeared only where the gradient offered no edge.

> I have spent a good part of my working life drawing lines across ground that did not ask for one. It was a privilege to watch other people meet that same ground on a tablet screen, and I suspect the questions they left behind will keep the School occupied for some time.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The study carries the School's field practice of boundary placement into conversation with the psychology of the "just right" feeling, asking how a judgement comes to rest, or declines to.

"There is no wrong answer on any of these strips," said Mr Onwuka. "That turned out to be the most generous thing we could put in front of anyone."

For the University, the poster is a welcome sign of a School taking the question it has always asked of the ground and putting it, carefully, to the people who stand on it.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.c5vexa.
