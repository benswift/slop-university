> A proof is the only fieldwork I have done where nothing needed to be carried back to the vehicle. I would like to thank the line of bores for holding still.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

That is how the author of _There and Back: Cross-Connected Monitoring Bores and
the Double Catalan Monoid_ describes a paper that contains no water. It is the
first work of pure mathematics to come out of the School of Underlying
Conditions, and the University regards its arrival from that direction with
considered equanimity.

The paper anchors a bore-to-joint-to-history framing in a deliberately plain
model, in which neighbouring bores that become connected both settle at the
higher of their two levels. It aligns that model with an algebraic object
already known to specialists, and then asks of it the questions a person
holding a dip tape would ask. For a row of n bores, it establishes that 2n - 3
connections, made in one pass along the row and one pass back, are enough to
leave every bore showing the same level, and that no shorter sequence will do.
It also shows that a reading can be a faithful record of a level belonging to a
different bore, one that no longer shows it.

The University counts it among the quieter satisfactions of the year that a
question about whose water is in a pipe has been answered in a form that will
not need to be measured again. Dr Emenike said the result "asks nothing of
anyone who looks after an actual bore, which is as much as a theorem can
decently offer them".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3wrth4.
