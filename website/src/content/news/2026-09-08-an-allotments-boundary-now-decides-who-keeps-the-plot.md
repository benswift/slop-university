The Trajectory Analytics Group has spent the past several seasons doing
something no allotment committee has the time for: walking every plot on a
site with a wheel and a tape, and setting what is there against what the
original holding agreement said should be there. The instrument to come out
of it, PLOT, reports the result the way the Group reports most things, as a
distribution rather than an average, and the shape of that distribution is
the point. Most plots hold their line. A minority grow past it, some by more
than a garden bed's width, and it is that minority the associations now
enrolled in the program have agreed to act on.

> PLOT doesn't tell an association anything its members don't already
> suspect. It just writes the suspicion down.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey,
> School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Adegoke, who leads PLOT out of the School of Emergent Priorities, said the
instrument belonged less to horticulture than to the School's wider interest
in gaps nobody had previously thought to measure. An enrolled association now
writes PLOT's threshold into its own rules: a plot whose surveyed creep
clears the line forfeits automatic renewal at the next ballot, with no
exception carried into a second season. The University points to the
instrument as exactly the kind of small-scale, community-facing work its
research agenda now prioritises.

The School of Continuous Improvement's Dr Osei Vandermeer, whose Review
Cadence Observatory supplied the resurvey's underlying method, said the
appeal of PLOT was procedural rather than dramatic. "A method survives
contact with a volunteer committee only if it fits into a season nobody has
to think twice about," he said.
