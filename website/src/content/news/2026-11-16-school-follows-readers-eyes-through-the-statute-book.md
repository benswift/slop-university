> I drafted provisions for a long time without giving much thought to the brackets that would one day be put around them. The School has been reminded that the page is read as well as the words, and we are glad of the reminder.
>
> --- Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld, School of Settled Matters

That reminder is the subject of [_Read Around the Repeal_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-a-reader-s-juc15o/), a new research poster by [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) and [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), with [Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) of the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

Forty-eight readers, half of them law students and half drawn from a community reading panel, worked through six sections of a single consolidated Act, some as a printed reprint and some as the equivalent web page, while an eye tracker recorded where they looked. Situating gaze measurement within a reception-studies account of the legal page, the team positions the square bracket, the "[Repealed]" line and the historical note as part of how a statute communicates, not as clutter around it. On the printed page, the authors report, those marks held about a quarter of readers' attention, and readers were far better at recognising which words had arrived by later amendment.

Dr Tredinnick said the study had "left the School with more questions about the page than it began with, which is how the School prefers to leave a study".

The University counts the poster among the clearer statements yet of a conviction it has long held, that the way a text looks is part of what it says.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.juc15o.
