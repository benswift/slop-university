Over a single growing season, a fieldworker carrying a fresh sprig of purslane asked 168 growers and stallholders along one river road what they called it. Their answers, gathered in [_Where Purslane Stops Being a Weed_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-purslane-stops-3azdnr/), a new research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions, trace a boundary in the district's speech that crosses the market gardens as plainly as any line on a soil map.

The work is led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, with [Ms Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of Emergent Priorities. Upstream, the plant is pulled from between the lettuces; downstream, it is sold by the bunch. Among the district's youngest speakers, the point where one name yields to the other sits some fifteen kilometres further up the road than it does among their elders.

> Every name a district keeps for a plant is a small decision about what the plant is for. We were fortunate to arrive while the district was still deciding.
>
> --- [Ms Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Carrying the instruments of dialect geography from the archive to the trestle table, the poster aligns speaker age, place and vocabulary into a single account of how a working community's relationship with a familiar plant is renegotiated across generations.

The University counts the study among a widening family of inquiries in which the School of Underlying Conditions turns its survey methods towards the words people use for the ground they work. Dr Obiora expects the conversation to continue. "We will be back at the same stalls before long," he said, "and we fully expect the plant to be there before us."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3azdnr.
