The School of Underlying Conditions has spent much of the year asking what the
ground gives back to the water that moves through it. Its newest paper answers
in electrons.

Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma,
Lead of the Basement Relations Group, report a laboratory method in which the
tracer sent through a cracked length of rock also serves as the measuring
instrument, its chemical state read continuously by an electrode as it emerges.
Across six intact core sections drawn from either side of one buried land
surface, the most weathered rock changed the tracer most.

> For ten years I took a reading down a pipe and was asked to believe it. Now
> the reading comes back out of the rock itself, and I think the School is
> still working out what it has learned about its own habits from that.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The work brings together organometallic electrochemistry, fractured-rock
hydrogeology and the School's long custodianship of recovered core, aligning
surface-scale measurement with an emerging understanding of how buried
weathering shapes the water around it.

Associate Professor Uzoma said the paper "gives the core store a reason to keep
every awkward, cracked section we were once tempted to set aside."

The University counts the paper among the clearest expressions yet of its
commitment to research conducted at the scale of a single surface, and to the
quiet conviction that rock, given time, will answer a well-posed question.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ja8jgw.
