The School of Emergent Priorities' Horizon Register exists to catch a
priority before it arrives; a new research poster turns that same instinct
on the building that houses it, tracing what happens when an institution's
own clock starts counting against someone the notification hasn't reached
yet.

A public library's hold-shelf system starts its three-day countdown to
expiry the moment a reserved book is scanned onto the shelf, not when the
"ready for collection" email actually leaves the branch's outbound mail
queue. Across nine metropolitan branches audited over sixteen weeks, that
difference ran from under half a day to nearly a day and a half, tracking
each branch's own send lag rather than anything a patron did.

> A clock that starts before anyone's told doesn't tell time so much as it
> tells on the institution running it.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register, School
> of Emergent Priorities

The work extends the School's standing interest in what an institution's own
instruments quietly assume about the people on the other end of them.
Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group, said
the finding "sits comfortably alongside" the Group's broader account of how
a commitment's timeline hardens well before anyone downstream has had a fair
look at it.

The audit found more than two in five holds logged as "expired, uncollected"
would have fallen inside a patron's true response window had the countdown
started at the email instead of the scan --- a gap the researchers argue the
library's own records have never been positioned to see.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6ubzk4.
