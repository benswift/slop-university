What can a beaker of soapy water work out for itself? The [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) has put the question formally, and the answer is a new paper.

[_The Micelle Computes a ReLU_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-micelle-computes-ygtevh/), by [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group, and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, takes the colloid chemist's most familiar curve, the one that bends at the critical micelle concentration, and reads it as a calculation. Depending on how the micelles are allowed to behave, the authors show, the same solution divides, computes nothing at all, or closely approximates the threshold function at the heart of modern neural networks.

> Our School has always been interested in what a sample does when nobody is directing it. It was a pleasure to find that the answer, in this case, already had a name in another field.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

The study brings the formal machinery of theoretical computer science into conversation with the long-standing physical picture of self-assembly, treating the solution as a reaction network and asking what it can be relied upon to calculate under three different assumptions about how its aggregates form and dissolve. For Dr Obiora, the result is "a reminder that the laboratory's oldest curve still has questions left in it."

The University takes particular satisfaction in work that lets a neighbouring discipline see a routine laboratory reading afresh, and in a School whose interests now run from drill core to the theorem.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ygtevh.
