Thirty days was the period after which the tablets at a Slop University core shed were permitted to forget that a record had been deleted. A paper released today reports what happened on the thirty-first, and on a number of days after it.

_The Block Stays in the Tray: Tombstone Retention under Heavy-Tailed Peer Absence in an Infrastructure-Free Replicated Store_ is the work of Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, both of the School of Underlying Conditions. It concerns the 23 devices on which the shed keeps its tray records, which hold copies of one another's work and compare notes whenever two of them are in the same place.

> We are grateful to every device that came back. Each of them was welcomed, and each of them was listened to, and that turned out to be the difficulty.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

Working from the devices' own logs of when they last met, the authors establish how long a tablet is typically out of contact, connect that pattern to the likelihood of a returning device restoring records its colleagues had already retired, and position the question within a wider account of what a shared system owes its absent members. Over the study period 412 records were restored in this way. The paper weighs several approaches to the matter and finds that each involves remembering something.

Its recommendation is that the system keep its deletion notes for good, which the authors cost at 362 kB per device. Dr Obiora said the School "now has a clear picture of how long its own equipment stays away, which is a thing it had not previously thought to ask of itself."

The University has always held that a field programme is entitled to be out of range, and it is pleased to see one of its schools make the arrangements that follow from that.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.et3oig.
