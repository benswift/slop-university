How many times must a remedy be taken apart before anyone can say which of its
ingredients it depends on? A new paper from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/),
[_Dismantling a Decoction_](/outputs/slop-paper-dismantling-a-decoction-8sxi7m/),
puts a number on the question, and then puts a second number underneath it.

Written by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), the paper
reframes a familiar bench routine, preparing a formula with ingredients withheld
and checking whether its effect survives, as a structured inquiry pathway, and
aligns it with established frameworks from the theory of computation to fix
upper and lower limits on the effort involved. One of its worked cases, a
fifteen-ingredient formula built from seven pairs, cannot be fully understood in
fewer than 135 tests.

> Most of my working life has been spent taking one thing away at a time and
> waiting to see whether anything changes. It is a comfort to have it shown, in
> writing, that this was very nearly the best anyone could do, and a useful
> discomfort to learn what it was never going to tell me.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Associate Professor Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group, said the
School valued short proofs "in the same way it values a light core tray: there
is less to carry, and more of it arrives intact."

The University sees in the paper a research culture prepared to count its own
steps, and to treat the count as a finding in its own right.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8sxi7m.
