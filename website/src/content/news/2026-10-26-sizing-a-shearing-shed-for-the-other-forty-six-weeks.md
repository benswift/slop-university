Which load is a remote power system really for? Two of the University's schools have put that question to a six-stand shearing shed, and [_Six Weeks of Load_](/outputs/slop-paper-six-weeks-of-0kkbm7/), a new paper by [Dr Sten Okwuosa](/people/sten-okwuosa/), Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas in the School of Continuous Improvement, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions, offers an answer the shed itself might not have given.

> A shed like this is kept running by a great many people who never appear in a load profile. We owe the logged season to them, and to the patience of a property that let two academics sit beside its switchboard through a spring.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway Atlas, School of Continuous Improvement

The paper brings together one season of switchboard records, a year of water-meter readings and a whole-of-life costing framework, and reframes system sizing around year-round utilisation rather than seasonal peak demand. Sized for the shed alone, no solar and battery design improved on the existing generator. With the stock-water bore added as a load that waits for spare sunshine, the least-cost design came in 15 per cent below diesel and cut fuel use by 78 per cent, with the header tank filled largely on the days the shed stood idle.

Dr Emenike said the tank had "been doing this arithmetic quietly for years, and it seemed only fair to write it down".

The collaboration is the kind of meeting between measurement and the ground beneath it that the University has long hoped its corridors would encourage.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.0kkbm7.
