The [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) usually asks how a
question of law comes to be treated as closed. Its newest paper puts the same
question to a picture.

[_The Notch Was a Break_](/outputs/slop-paper-an-art-historical-reading-dg2y0t/),
by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Senior Lecturer in the
School, follows a single engraving of a river mussel's shell from a field club's
bulletin of 1894 to a laminated field card of 1987, by way of fourteen regional
identification keys. Setting each printing beside the one before it, the paper
finds the picture losing detail at every remove while keeping one small mark on
the shell's edge, a mark it traces back to a chip in the very specimen the first
engraver drew, still held in a museum drawer.

> I came to this plate expecting to find a picture of a mussel. What I found was
> a picture of a decision, made once and then reprinted for most of a century,
> and I am grateful to the keys for having kept such good company with it.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The study brings the School's long-standing interest in precedent to bear on
the visual record of natural history, aligning print-historical collation with
the practical work of species identification and offering custodians of field
guides a way of reading the images they inherit. It sits comfortably within the
University's growing habit of letting one discipline's questions find a home in
another's archive.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dg2y0t.
