A roster drawn up to check a screening program has been read back as something
closer to an experiment.

_The Quiet Room Was Available_, deposited today through the Office of Research
Outputs, sets the school hearing screening sessions a quality-assurance officer
attended against the sessions nobody attended. Researchers in the School of
Emergent Priorities took the officers' availability roster, fixed before each
term's school list was known, as the thing deciding which sessions were watched.

Across 1,180 sessions and 38,402 children in six districts over four school
years, observed sessions referred 9.4 per cent of the children screened and
unobserved sessions 6.1 per cent. Adjusted for venue type, screener, term and
school size, the gap held in every district and did not narrow across the four
years.

> We have not shown that any child was screened badly, and the design cannot
> show that. What it can show is that the indicator carries two practices at
> once, and that nobody had been asked which of them the districts are being
> compared on.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift
> Survey, School of Emergent Priorities

Sessions run in whichever room a school can spare, and the service publishes
district referral rates as a child-health indicator. Observation coverage itself
ranged from 2.1 to 7.8 per cent of sessions between districts.

"Every assurance program produces a second dataset it did not set out to
collect," said Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow in the School of Emergent
Priorities. "The roster is the part of this one nobody thought of as
measurement."

The School of Emergent Priorities has made a programme of reading an
institution's routine paperwork as evidence about the institution, and the
University counts this among the more transferable results that programme has
produced.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ca6ntd.
