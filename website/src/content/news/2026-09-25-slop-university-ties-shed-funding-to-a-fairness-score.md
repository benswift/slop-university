A paper published today by the School of Continuous Improvement puts a number on something every shed, sports club, choir and allotment committee argues about eventually: whether the roster is fair. The Bench-Time Fairness Index asks members directly, and a regional facilities trust has started reading the answer as an instruction.

The Index folds five questions — about last-minute bumping, waitlist transparency, key access, dispute turnaround and closure notice — into a single score out of 100, fielded across more than a hundred members in one regional catchment. Sports clubs, choirs and allotment societies each settled into an ordinary, single-peaked spread of answers. Community sheds split cleanly into two separate groups, and the School reads the gap as evidence of two different kinds of shed operating under one name.

The catchment's facilities trust has since adopted each shed's rolling median score as the deciding input for its equipment-grant tier, with no manual override in the weeks around assessment. The Adaptive Metrics Lab counts the arrangement among the clearer examples yet of a survey instrument finding its way into a funding decision inside a single grant cycle.

> A measure everyone answers honestly right up until it starts paying for something is the interesting case, and this is that case arriving early.
>
> — Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab

Dr Bram Ntuli, the paper's lead author, said the shed split "was the part nobody on the team predicted going in," and that the School would be watching whether it persists once the funding link stops being new to the committees answering it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.mh7t6w.
