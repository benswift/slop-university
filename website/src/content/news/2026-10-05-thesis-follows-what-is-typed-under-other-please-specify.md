The string "n/a" was typed 6,402 times into boxes that had asked respondents to
specify something else. It is one of the smaller findings of _Other (Please
Specify)_, the doctoral thesis Halvard Nnamani has submitted for examination in
the School of Emergent Priorities.

Mr Nnamani, a doctoral candidate in the School, took the last item of the
drop-down list as his subject and stayed with it through four linked studies
across 38 community organisations. A census sorted three years of entries into
six kinds. A trace then sat with each organisation's registrar or bookings
officer and established how far each box's contents had travelled: for 310 of
417 boxes, no one had seen them. Recorded sessions asked the people typing what
they expected, and a three-arm trial across 14,406 form sessions discarded the
entries for some respondents and removed the box for others. Discarding changed
nothing that was measured. Removing the box did.

> Every school has a question it assumed somebody else had already settled. This
> one was sitting at the bottom of a list, and it took a candidate with a
> registrar's patience to scroll that far.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority Studies

Associate Professor Lindqvist supervised the thesis with Professor Verity
Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group. Its recommendation has been
adopted as the University's Specify Field Standard: every list on a University
form will end with the box, and what is typed there will be neither transmitted
nor stored. The School counts the submission among the plainer demonstrations of
what its doctoral program is for, and the thesis is now with examiners.

The full thesis is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fr8eys.
