The water table a seismic survey sees is not the one a monitoring bore records, according to [_The Seismic Water Table Is the Top of the Capillary Fringe_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-seismic-water-ecq3zq/), a paper released today by the School of Underlying Conditions. Across eight bores on one alluvial terrace, the seismic surface sat between 0.14 and 0.81 m above the dipped level, and highest where the sand was siltiest.

The paper is the work of [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School, and [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group. It is the School's first paper to put a hammer to the ground, and the University welcomes geophysics to the School's growing repertoire of ways to ask the subsurface a question.

> I have spent most of my working life trusting the dipper. It seems the ground has been keeping a second opinion a little higher up, and I am in no hurry to tell either of them which one is right.
>
> --- [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The study brings together seismic imaging, inversion and the described texture of stored drill core to build a shared picture of where saturation begins, and it pays careful attention to which parts of the ground its instruments can genuinely speak for. Associate Professor Uzoma said the School would "go on describing the sand first, so that whoever measures it next knows what they are standing on".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ecq3zq.
