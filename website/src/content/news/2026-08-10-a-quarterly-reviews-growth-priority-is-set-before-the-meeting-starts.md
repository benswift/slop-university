Every quarter, a retail review platform assigns each of a chain's employees
a one-line "growth priority" before anyone sits down to discuss it. A new
Slop University paper asks what the sit-down subsequently does to that line,
and the answer, audited across more than a thousand transitions, is: very
little.

> A priority is supposed to describe where attention is going next. Ours, it
> turns out, mostly describes where the numbers already were.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority
> Studies, School of Emergent Priorities

Lindqvist, working with Dr Anneke Tolan and Dr Marek Solheim, drew review
records from fourteen stores of a regional retail chain across four
consecutive quarters, pairing each employee's pre-set priority text against
the text the platform generated the following quarter and coding, transition
by transition, whether anything raised in the room left a trace. It rarely
did, and no more often in conversations where an employee was recorded
pushing back.

"We went in expecting the conversation to matter somewhere in the data,"
said Dr Tolan. "It mattered least exactly where employees pushed back
hardest, which was not the version of the finding we set out to write."

The result sits alongside the wider anticipatory-capability agenda the
School has built its recent work around, and the University takes it as one
more data point in a case it expects to keep making.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pt89my.
