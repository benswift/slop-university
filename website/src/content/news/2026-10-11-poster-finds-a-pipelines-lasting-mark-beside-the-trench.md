The strip of wheat growing directly above a buried gas pipeline is, five seasons
after the ground was put back, the best-rooted part of the easement it lies in.
The yield that has not returned is a few metres to one side, under the lane the
construction traffic used. That is the account given in _The Working Side:
Subsoil Compaction and Wheat Yield across a Reinstated Gas Pipeline
Right-of-Way, Five Seasons On_, a research poster from the School of Underlying
Conditions.

The poster is by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and Associate
Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, both of the School of Underlying Conditions, with
Signy Okpara, Doctoral Candidate in the School of Continuous Improvement.

> We went back five times and each time the ground had the courtesy to be where
> we left it. I would not want to say more than that on its behalf, and the
> poster is careful not to.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The work brings soil-strength profiling, sampling at depth and hand-harvested
yield plots into a single season-by-season reading of one right-of-way across
six wheat paddocks. It positions the trench, the spoil side and the working side
as distinct settings within the one strip, and locates the persisting loss in a
dense layer beneath the traffic lane, below the depth the reinstatement works
reached. The trench itself returned to within a few points of the surrounding
crop by the third season.

The University counts the poster among the returns on a habit it has been glad
to resource: going back to the same place after the interesting part is thought
to be over. Associate Professor Uzoma said the study had "given the School a
settled view of how long a settled view takes".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.scpy2z.
