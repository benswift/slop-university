Does a title that forbids "pigs and poultry" stop a later owner keeping a few hens? Asked a question of that shape, present-day readers split, and how they split depends less on the word "and" than on what it joins.

That is one of the findings of [_Neither the Shop nor the Factory_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-the-scope-otlym4/), a new paper by Dr [Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) and Associate Professor [Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/). The authors transcribed 611 covenants that prohibit several things at once from a century of superseded certificates of title, and asked 212 readers whether a described act broke each one. A prohibition joined by "or" was taken to forbid each thing it named on 94 per cent of trials. One joined by "and" was not, particularly when it joined two acts rather than two things.

> The people who wrote these sentences had a reader in mind, and it was never quite us. I find that rather moving, and I think the covenants have been patient with us for long enough that we owe them a careful reading in return.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The study carries the School's established attention to the burdens that travel with land into the semantics of coordination and negation, connecting corpus-based variation analysis with experimental judgement methods across a broad drafting timeframe, and the School counts it a natural companion to its teaching in land law. Associate Professor Threlkeld, who drafted legislation before joining the University, described the collaboration as "a conversation between the person who reads the instrument and the person who once wrote one, which is not a conversation the instrument usually gets to hear".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.otlym4.
