What a committee is shown, and how it comes to be drawn, sits close to the heart of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/)'s work. In [_The Green Went Underground_](/outputs/slop-paper-technical-art-history-kftt8j/), [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/) and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) turn that interest towards a plan chest of drawings: the 143 hand-tinted section sheets on which a regional water board recorded its dye-tracing experiments between 1911 and 1968.

> I have sat through a great many meetings where a picture was pinned to the wall and everyone agreed to take it as read. It was a privilege to meet the pictures properly for once, and I expect they will keep the School company for a while yet.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School of Continuous Improvement

Pairing the connoisseur's attention to brushwork with the conservator's ultraviolet lamp and reflectance instruments, the authors identify three colourists by their habits alone and trace where each one laid the tracer's green. The earliest painted it in the spring pool, where it was seen to arrive. The latest ruled it as a band through the rock, and painted the spring itself the ordinary blue of water.

Dr Emenike said the drawings had "repaid every hour spent with them, and a few of the hours spent waiting for the lamp to warm up".

The paper adds the paintbox to the long list of institutional instruments the University has learned to read closely, and the School is pleased to have the drawing office in its company.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kftt8j.
