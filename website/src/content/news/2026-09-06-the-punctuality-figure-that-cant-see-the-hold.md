A held bus, Slop University's newest research paper reports, can repair a
punctuality figure without repairing what the figure was meant to describe.

The School of Emergent Priorities and the School of Continuous Improvement
have piloted Pacemaker, a headway-holding controller installed at a single
control point on an outer-suburban bus route. Led by Senior Lecturer Ronja
Oyelaran with Research Fellow Osei Vandermeer, the sixteen-week deployment
reads each bus's live position against the one ahead and tells an
early-running driver exactly how long to hold before departing, so the gap
behind it has time to close. Weekly headway variation falls by roughly half
once holding begins, and on-time running at the seven timing points
downstream of the control stop climbs by a mean 26 percentage points --- a
figure the operator has now adopted as the route's headline punctuality
statistic for its next service contract.

> An early bus and a late one are the same problem, seen from two different
> stops. We only ever built an instrument for the one everyone was already
> looking at.
>
> --- Senior Lecturer Ronja Oyelaran, School of Emergent Priorities

The adopted figure starts timing a service only once it leaves the control
stop, so the minutes a bus spends held there never enter the statistic at
all. "It's an odd kind of proof," said Research Fellow Osei Vandermeer.
"The display now tells a driver exactly how long to wait, and the network's
own report was never built to notice that anyone did."

The work extends the University's wider account of what a queue-adjacent
instrument actually reports, alongside its long-running study of the
routes, permits, and pick-up zones that hold everyday travel together. The
full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ktpz2s.
