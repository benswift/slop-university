Half an hour into a tandem language exchange, the two partners swap languages,
and with them the right to correct each other. A research poster from the
School of Underlying Conditions, released today, shows that something else
crosses the half-hour too: the corrections a partner gave in the first half
come back to them, very nearly one for one, in the second.

> Nobody at those tables was asked to keep score, and nobody needed to be. We
> are grateful to every pair who let us sit in on an evening that belonged to
> them, and we mean to go on listening.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

Working across 96 recorded sessions, 24 pairs and six language combinations,
the team brought conversation-analytic transcription into dialogue with an
established framework for learner feedback, building a fine-grained picture of
how expertise is negotiated, shared and handed back in peer-to-peer language
learning. Corrections given in the second half tracked those received in the
first (r = 0.71), and around two sessions in three closed within a single
correction of even. Quieter, embedded corrections were not returned at all.

The poster sits within the School's widening engagement with the ordinary
talk through which people teach one another, and the University counts it a
welcome addition to that conversation.

"Every exchange evening has its own customs, and it has been a real
education to learn a few of them," said
[Ms Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), Doctoral Candidate.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7zateu.
