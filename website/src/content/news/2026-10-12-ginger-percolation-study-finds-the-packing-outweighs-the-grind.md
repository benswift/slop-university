> The instruction to moisten the powder before packing it is older than anyone in the School. It is pleasant, now and then, to be handed an instruction and to find nothing wrong with it.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Dr Emenike's remark accompanies _Where the Menstruum Went: Tracer breakthrough in bench percolators packed with comminuted ginger rhizome_, a research poster she has published with Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow. The poster carries the School of Underlying Conditions, whose ordinary business is rock, soil and groundwater, onto a pharmacy bench, where a glass cone of ground rhizome is asked the question the School asks of any ground: which parts of it the liquid passes through, and which parts it leaves alone.

The work aligns an extraction practice with a subsurface vocabulary. Thirty-six beds were prepared across three powders and two ways of packing, a tracer was introduced to each, and what emerged at the outlet was interpreted for what it disclosed about the passage. The beds packed dry let the tracer through early; the beds moistened and packed in layers kept more of themselves in the flow. In these beds, the poster reports, the packing counted for more than the grind.

A school willing to borrow another discipline's glassware and hand it back with a result is, on the University's reckoning, a school in good order. Dr Obiora described the bench as "the first field site of my career that could be emptied and packed again before lunch", adding that he intended to mention it less often than he had been.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m7iobm.
