The School of Emergent Priorities keeps a standing interest in registers
that outlive the thing they were built to track. Its newest case study
turns that interest on a strata scheme's noise-complaint log, and finds the
log's own naming habit has been quietly wrong for twelve years.

Dr Fenna Okoro and Dr Solveig Adeyemi, of the School of Emergent Priorities
and the School of Continuous Improvement, re-coded 308 noise-complaint
tickets from one 44-unit apartment block, spanning 2014 to 2025, against the
block's as-built structural plan rather than against the resident each
ticket named. Ticket density clustered overwhelmingly in a single
structural bay, accounting for 144 of the 308 tickets logged, across five
separate resident turnovers recorded in the block's own committee minutes.
A 2025 renovation opened a ceiling cavity in the implicated bay and exposed
a mis-poured slab joint, confirming what the register's per-resident field
had never been built to see.

> A register is only as good as the field it was given. Ours gave every
> ticket a name and never asked whether the name was the point.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register, School
> of Emergent Priorities

"Five people were told the noise was a neighbour they'd never met," said Dr
Solveig Adeyemi. "The one thing the file never told any of them was that
four other people had already heard the same thing, from four other
neighbours, in the same spot."

The case study extends the School of Emergent Priorities' running interest
in what an institutional record keeps, and what it quietly retires, once a
category has already been chosen for it.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.c4aizu.
