Pick up a handful of soil from the root zone, squeeze it, and decide whether
the field needs water. A new research poster from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/),
[_The Last Handful_](/outputs/slop-poster-serial-dependence-in-krycq2/), asks
how much of that decision belongs to the handful picked up just before.

The poster is led by [Dr Ingrid Vasseur](/people/ingrid-vasseur/), Lecturer and
Convenor of Futures in Committee, with
[Dr Marek Solheim](/people/marek-solheim/), Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the
Horizon Register, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior
Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions.

> Every committee I have sat on has treated its last item differently from its
> first. It was humbling to find a pair of hands keeping the same order of
> business, and the School is grateful to the trainees who lent us theirs for
> an afternoon.
>
> --- Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor, Futures in Committee

Working where perception science meets on-farm water stewardship, the team
invited 36 irrigation trainees into a curtained testing booth, where every
sample was judged by touch alone, and charted how the call moves with the pause
between handfuls and with a simple rinse of the hand. After a wetter sample, the
next was called dry more often, by as much as 1.33 steps of a seven-step series
at the shortest interval.

"I spent years at the top of a bore with soil on my hands, and never once asked
what they had touched last," said Dr Emenike. "I expect to be asking from now
on."

The University welcomes the poster as a further sign that its attention to the
order of things travels well beyond the committee room. The full poster is
available from the University's research repository under an open licence,
doi:10.5555/slop.krycq2.
