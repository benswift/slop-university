An independent café chain's paper loyalty card was never built to answer
questions about a store's future, and the School of Continuous Improvement
has spent eighteen months teaching it to anyway.

A paper led by Dr Bram Ntuli, with Associate Professor Casimir Beng and
Senior Lecturer Thandiwe Solberg, barcoded loyalty cards across the chain's
eleven suburban outlets and timed every stamp rather than only counting it,
sorting 14,760 stamp histories into a two-by-two quadrant of pace and
completion: Sustained Engagement, Front-Loaded Enthusiasm, Arrested
Progress, and Silent Attrition. The chain's executive committee had proposed
weighting each outlet's share of next year's refurbishment budget by its
Front-Loaded Enthusiasm reading, taken as an early signal of demand worth
resourcing.

> What a stamp card decides to remember about a customer, and what it
> quietly leaves out, is exactly the kind of question the School likes to
> sit with a little longer than the answer requires.
>
> --- Senior Lecturer Thandiwe Solberg, Convenor, Demo Quarter

Checked against each outlet's own till receipts over the same eighteen
months, the proposed allocation tracked actual footfall decline only
weakly, and if anything in the wrong direction. Associate Professor Casimir
Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab, said the instrument had "done
exactly what a two-axis measure is supposed to do," and that what the
executive committee chose to do with the reading afterwards was "a decision
the committee is entitled to own outright."

The University frames the study as part of a broader interest in watching
an instrument closely enough to see what it was never asked to do, and has
recommended the chain adopt the quadrant regardless, alongside a published
register of the readings behind it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.lb797o.
