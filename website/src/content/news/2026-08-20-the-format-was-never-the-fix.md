A wedding coordinator hears the same complaint whichever format she chooses:
guests change their mind after the seating chart is locked. A research
poster released today from the School of Continuous Improvement tested
whether the format was ever the problem.

Dr Bram Ntuli and Dr Solveig Adeyemi tracked 214 weddings over a 14-month
window, comparing coordinators who kept RSVPs on paper reply cards against
those using a shared spreadsheet. Late-arriving changes, logged after each
seating chart locked and reconciled against the caterer's own headcount,
moved no differently by format --- the spreadsheet weddings recorded a
slightly higher average, not a lower one. Coordinator-reported confidence in
the record was the one measure that moved, and it moved toward the
spreadsheet regardless of what the log actually showed.

> The instructive result here isn't the null result --- it's how confident
> everyone stayed regardless. That's the question we're carrying into the
> standard's review.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School
> of Continuous Improvement

"We thank the twelve coordinating venues that let us reconcile a log nobody
else was reading," said Dr Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer, School of Continuous
Improvement. The team pre-registered its late-change threshold before the
first venue came online, and treated coordinator confidence as a separate,
secondary measure --- the one where format did, unmistakably, show up.

The Office of Research Outputs' recommendation proceeds on that confidence
finding alone: a national RSVP reconciliation template, standardising the
spreadsheet format across affiliated coordinators, with a published review
once uptake clears half the register.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fres85.
