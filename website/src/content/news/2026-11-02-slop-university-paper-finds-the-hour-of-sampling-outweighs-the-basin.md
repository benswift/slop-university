Storms seldom keep a building site's hours, and the sample bottle at the edge of
a sediment basin usually does.

[_Next Morning, After the Basin Settles_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-the-timing-ttm0tb/),
a new paper by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer,
and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, both
of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), asks
what a single bottle of basin water can say about the discharge it was taken
from, depending on when it was filled.

> A bottle on a pole is an old instrument and an honest one. All we did was ask
> it what time it was dipped, and it turned out to have a good deal to say on
> the subject.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Pairing a wet season of continuous outlet monitoring with a design-based
resampling framework, the authors treat each site's sampling routine as a
sampling design in its own right. Across 74 discharge events, a bottle filled
in the first hour read at roughly twice the event's average, a bottle filled at
the next morning's inspection read lower, and a two-reading approach came
closest of all.

The paper widens the School's long attention to monitoring records from the
aquifer to the surface water that runs off a site before it gets there, and the
University is glad to see that attention travel. Dr Obiora said the basins had
"asked the question a soil surveyor learns to ask of every boundary, which is
who drew it, and at what hour".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ttm0tb.
