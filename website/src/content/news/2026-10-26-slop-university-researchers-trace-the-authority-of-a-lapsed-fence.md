Slop University's newest research poster leaves the campus for the city footpath, and asks a question that planners and philosophers have tended to ask separately: whose instruction is a pedestrian following when they walk around a fence nobody has renewed?

[_The Fence Outlived Its Permit_](/outputs/slop-poster-orphaned-barriers-why-ffoj5b/) is by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions, with [Dr Anneke Tolan](/people/anneke-tolan/), Senior Research Fellow and Deputy Convenor of Futures in Committee, and [Dr Ronja Oyelaran](/people/ronja-oyelaran/), Senior Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey. Across 64 barriers still standing past their permits and 3,410 coded passages, walkers detoured at lapsed fencing almost as often as at permitted fencing, however long ago the permit had run out. What changed their minds was a single moved panel.

> Every one of those fences was put up by someone with a good reason. We did not go looking for who. The more courteous question, I think, is what the rest of us owe a decision once it has been left in our keeping.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Aligning a council works register with a structured kerbside audit, the team sets planning practice and the theory of authority side by side, positioning the everyday temporary barrier as a place where a decision goes on being honoured well after anyone is available to make it.

The University takes particular satisfaction in a School of the subsurface turning its patience to the surface of the street, and finding there the same respect for what is left in place.

Dr Tolan said the team hoped the poster would find its way "onto the agenda of any works committee that has ever approved a fortnight".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ffoj5b.
