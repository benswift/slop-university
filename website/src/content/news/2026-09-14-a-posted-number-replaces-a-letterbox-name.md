A rural property's address used to be a name everyone nearby already knew; under a state addressing scheme rolled out road by road, it is now a number set by distance from the highway. A new brochure from the School of Continuous Improvement collects what residents say changed once the number took over the job the name used to do.

Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor of the Living Dashboard, spent the past year sitting with residents at their own gates, asking not whether the new number was accurate but what it now settled on their behalf: which delivery arrives, which visitor keeps circling the same stretch of road, and what a triple-zero call-taker repeats back under pressure. The University treats the interviews as confirmation that a civic register earns the same scrutiny it already turns on its own dashboards.

> The number doesn't ask to be understood. It only has to be read the same way twice, by two different people, at speed.
>
> --- Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Living Dashboard, School of Continuous Improvement

Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Horizon Register, said the finding matched what her own register work keeps turning up: "an entry outlives the reasoning that opened it," and that the School of Emergent Priorities was watching the rollout for the same reason.

The brochure sets out the commitment behind the interviews: every rural-numbering rollout the University partners with now receives a published legibility audit before residents are asked to adopt the new address, its findings standing with no discretionary override.

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qdquid.
