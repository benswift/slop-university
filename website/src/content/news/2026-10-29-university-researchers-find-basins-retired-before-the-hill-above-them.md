The [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) has made
a habit of asking where one thing is judged to end and the next to begin. Its
latest research poster,
[_The Basin Handed Over Too Soon_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-basin-handed-uedfjj/),
carries that question out of the paddock and into the planning office, and
finds that on one growing estate the answer was set by the staging plan rather
than by the slope.

The poster is led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow, with [Dr Ronja Oyelaran](/people/ronja-oyelaran/), Senior
Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey, and
[Dr Marek Solheim](/people/marek-solheim/), Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the
Horizon Register. Reading the estate's consent record alongside its changing
ground cover, the team found that five of its six construction sediment basins
were handed over as parkland while land above them was still stripped, for a
mean of 30 months each.

> Every condition of consent is a small promise about how long something will
> be needed. We would be glad to hear from anyone who has drafted one and
> wondered, some years later, whether it was kept.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer and Deputy Convenor, Strategic Drift
> Survey

Working from the estate's own paperwork, the study aligns the lifecycle of temporary drainage
infrastructure with the evolving footprint of upslope activity, and offers
planning authorities a catchment-anchored settings change to underpin it.

The University regards the poster as a welcome case of its earth scientists
lending an eye for boundaries to a neighbouring profession. Dr Obiora said the
project had taught the School "to look twice at the lines we draw for our own
convenience, and we found a few".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.uedfjj.
