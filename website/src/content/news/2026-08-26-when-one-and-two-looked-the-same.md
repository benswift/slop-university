Once the two lenses look the same, the clinically available answer is to say
so. A suburban optometry chain recoded that answer as an incomplete
refraction, and withheld the next continuing-education day from any practice
that still logged it above a published threshold.

A paper published today by Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Dr Ingrid
Vasseur and Dr Iben Chikere of the School of Emergent Priorities follows
4,218 lens-flip exchanges across 36 practices, fourteen weeks before that
rule and twenty-six weeks after it. Patients' spoken turns are sorted into a
five-class taxonomy: a discriminated preference, a first-offered default, an
alternation, an unprompted "same", and a guess produced only after a re-ask.

The same-declared share fell from 14.2 per cent of flips to 2.9 per cent. The
forced-guess share rose from 9.1 per cent to 21.4 per cent. Discriminated
preference did not move, and nor did the chain's own spectacle remake rate.
Eleven practices sat above the eight-per-cent threshold in the first quarter
after the rule and had a continuing-education day withheld; by the fourth
quarter all eleven had fallen below it.

The paper belongs to a line of University work that takes a logged
completeness rate seriously enough to ask what it is complete of.

> The log got cleaner. The 1-or-2 question did not get easier; the safer
> turn was to pick a number.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority
> Studies

Dr Ingrid Vasseur, who convenes Futures in Committee, said a rule that
closes an available answer "does not retire the moment the two lenses look
alike; it only changes what gets written down about it."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.02j1em.
