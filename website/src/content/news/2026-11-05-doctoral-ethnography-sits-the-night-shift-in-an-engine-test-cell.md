The night shift called it the Lodger. The engine at the centre of
[_Sitting with the Lodger_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-night-shift-h7znf8/), a new
paper by [Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), spent its
thousand-hour durability test running through the night in a combustion
laboratory's test cell, with two technicians awake beside it for every one of
those hours.

> I went in expecting to study how a test is kept in order, and came out having
> spent most of a year in good company. Both are in the paper, and I am not
> certain the second is the smaller part.
>
> --- Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Across sixty-three night shifts and two engine campaigns, the study brings the
attentive traditions of cultural anthropology into conversation with the
working life of advanced combustion research. It situates the hourly
walk-round, the logbook's remarks column and the naming of an engine within a
wider practice of presence, and positions the technicians' care as part of
what allows a test's results to be read with confidence at its end.

The paper is a welcome sign of the range the School's doctoral cohort now
brings to its research agenda, carrying a candidate's attention well beyond
the seminar room and into the long hours in which the University's partner
disciplines quietly do much of their work.

"The School keeps late hours of its own," said Associate Professor Kwame
Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority Studies, "and it is good to see
one of our candidates spend them so generously in someone else's."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.h7znf8.
