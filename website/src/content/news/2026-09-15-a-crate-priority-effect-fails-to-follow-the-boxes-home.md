A box that goes into the garage after a house move often does not come out
again until a council truck takes it away.

A new paper from the School of Emergent Priorities tests whether that
ordinary fact undermines one of the School's own instruments. Doctoral
candidate Hedvig Anyanwu's earlier study of four office restacks found that
the order staff opened their allocated crates tracked which projects a team
already treated as urgent. With Senior Lecturer Dr Ronja Oyelaran and
Lecturer Dr Iben Chikere, she has now run the same design on 58 households
moving house, tracking 612 boxes from packing tape to spare-room shelf.

> An office crate has colleagues watching it. A garage shelf has nobody
> watching it at all, which turns out to matter more than what's inside the
> box.
>
> — Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Self-rated priority predicts almost nothing about how soon a household box
gets opened; where it physically sits does. Boxes shelved at eye level are
opened within a fortnight about half the time; boxes left on the garage floor
mostly are not, regardless of what a household said mattered when it packed
them. Boxes still sealed after nine months are mostly cleared unopened at a
single kerbside hard-rubbish collection.

"A finding that only works in front of an audience is still a finding," said
Ms Anyanwu. "We just now know whose audience it needs."

The School's Trajectory Analytics Group treats the result as a boundary
condition rather than a defeat, and is extending its packing-day protocol
into a Household Priority Audit requirement for any move booked through the
University's staff-relocation scheme, waived for no household.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.90amlj.
