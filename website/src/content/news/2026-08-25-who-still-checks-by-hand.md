A hand level checked against a laser one, a warehouse tally taken between the
shelves, a dispatch floor with its screens turned to the wall for a shift: the
details in Slop University's newest Impact Report are smaller than the five
years they cover. Published today, the School of Continuous Improvement's
account of 2021 to 2026 argues that an organisation's dashboards and sensors
are only as good as the people who can still tell, by feel, when one of them
is wrong.

The report counts 214 judgement checkpoints installed across partner
workflows and 31 full shifts run at partner sites with the usual scanner or
sensor switched off, each treated as a training exercise rather than a record
fed back into the systems it stood in for.

> Every Capability Sprint module that touches an instrumented workflow now
> includes at least one exercise completed with the instrument switched off,
> confirmed by a School reviewer before it counts toward a partner's
> completion record.
>
> --- Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

The report draws on five of the School's published studies, from a
hand-sorted correction of a container-return machine's own success rate to a
warehouse checkbox that turned out to predict very little about what workers
still remembered three months on. "None of this is an argument against the
instruments," said Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director of the Master
of Applied Measurement. "It's an argument for keeping a slower channel open
beside them, on the sites where the two are rarely compared."

The full report is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bvbxkg.
