A live countdown on a food-truck's ordering screen does two jobs at once:
it tells a customer how long they'll wait, and it tells the truck's own
operator when to act. A new Slop University survey asked sixty-three
operators on one regional circuit how much they trust the second job, and
checked their answers against a season of the platform's own records.

> An early warning only means something once somebody goes back and asks
> how many times it was right. Most of the circuit had never asked.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

_The Countdown Decided First_, deposited today through the Office of
Research Outputs, cross-references operators' self-reported trust in the
countdown's early-warning threshold against 1,842 threshold crossings
logged over a fourteen-week trading season. Trust in the warning held
steady across the circuit regardless of a truck's own hit-rate, which
ranged from under five per cent to over thirty. A display refresh rolled
out mid-season, changing how the countdown looks on screen, changed
nothing about what operators said they did in response.

"The number on the screen is doing an enormous amount of unearned work,"
said Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group.
"An anticipatory signal is supposed to earn trust the way any forecast
does, one season at a time. This one seems to have skipped that step
entirely."

The School of Emergent Priorities reads the result as a small addition to
its wider account of how an institution comes to trust a number before
the number has done anything to deserve it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wd1e8f.
