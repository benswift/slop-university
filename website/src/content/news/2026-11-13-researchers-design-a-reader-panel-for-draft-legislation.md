> Every drafter has a reader in mind, and most of us keep that reader to ourselves. It was a strange and welcome discipline to write ours down, with a number beside it, and to see what followed from the number.
>
> --- Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld, School of Settled Matters

So says the lead author of [_Where the Panel Spends Its Readers_](/outputs/slop-paper-bayesian-sequential-design-9eb8ji/), a new paper by [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and [Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/) of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/). The paper follows a drafting office as it tests six rival wordings of a single definition on 120 readers, and asks how a panel that small should be shared out among lay readers, registry clerks and practitioners.

Its answer brings the School of Settled Matters' close attention to the drafting table together with the School of Continuous Improvement's long engagement with decision support, positioning the comprehension test as a choice to be designed rather than a formality to be completed. Two approaches to that design are set side by side: one that seeks to learn as much as possible about every draft, and one that seeks only to enact the right one. In the paper's simulations the second chose the better draft in 86% of panels, and the two approaches sent their readers to almost opposite parts of the panel.

"The Living Dashboard has spent several years deciding what a choice should be shown," said Dr Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor of the Living Dashboard. "It has been good for us to spend a season deciding who should be shown it."

The University welcomes the paper as a sign of what becomes possible when a law school and a decision-support community find themselves sitting on the same panel.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9eb8ji.
