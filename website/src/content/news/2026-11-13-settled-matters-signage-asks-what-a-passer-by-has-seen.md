Much of the law that governs a footpath rests on a quiet assumption about the
person walking along it: that a notice displayed as the rules require has been
seen. [_The Law Assumes You Saw the Sign_](/outputs/slop-ad-the-law-assumes-2qnj95/),
the newest panel from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/),
puts that assumption on the very screens a passer-by walks past, and leaves it
there to be tested.

> I drafted a good many provisions that told a reader where to stand. I never
> once stood there myself, and I am glad the School now sends its students out
> to do it.
>
> --- Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld, School of Settled Matters

The campaign, led by
[Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/), positions
visibility as an interpretive question as much as a physical one, aligning the
School's close reading of notice provisions with the ordinary experience of the
walk to the bus. Its illustration follows a single pedestrian past a blank sign,
a measuring tape unrolled along the kerb between them. Its small print anchors
the panel in the University's repository, from
[_Where the Panel Spends Its Readers_](/outputs/slop-paper-bayesian-sequential-design-9eb8ji/),
a study of how to test six rival drafts of a statutory definition on lay
readers, to
[_Waved At, Not Yours_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-self-report-survey-wsu100/), a
survey of how often people at a kerb step toward a vehicle that is not theirs.

It says something about a young School's confidence that its signage asks to be
read under the same conditions it studies, at walking pace and from across the
road. The panel joins the portrait rotation on campus and bus-stop screens.
