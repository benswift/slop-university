Thirty years of water samples from a single monitoring bore have turned out to
be saying something about the bore itself.
[_What the Casing Kept_](/outputs/slop-poster-unmixing-one-monitoring-hqf3dm/),
a research poster by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior
Lecturer, and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), finds that a
steady rise in the bore's organic-matter record came not from the groundwater
but from the standing water left in the pipe when a visit was cut short.

> The bottles were always filled with care, and it seems they kept a little more of each visit than anyone wrote down. It has been a pleasure to read the rest of it.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The team brought the School's shelved spectral holdings together with the paper
record of each sampling visit, applying an advanced resolution approach that
lets overlapping signals in a sample be told apart without assuming in advance
what they are. Of the three signals it recovered, one matched neither the
aquifer's organic matter nor its nitrate, and it faded steadily the longer the
bore had been pumped before the bottle was filled.

The poster sits within the School's sustained interest in what a single reading
taken down a pipe can be asked to say about the ground around it, an interest
the University regards as a distinctive strength of its earth sciences.

Associate Professor Uzoma described the work as "an invitation to look again at
what is already on the shelf, in our own store and in every store like it."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hqf3dm.
