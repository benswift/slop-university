At what point does a bump on the side of a signal become a second signal? A
research poster from the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) puts the
question to the people who answer it every working day.
[_When Does a Shoulder Become a Peak?_](/outputs/slop-poster-when-a-shoulder-pt2jzx/)
is the work of Mr Eivind Onwuka, Dr Torun Ezeigwe and Associate Professor
Casimir Beng.

> Every group in the School has a word it uses more confidently than it can
> define. A year spent with this one has made me a good deal more patient with
> the rest.
>
> --- Mr Eivind Onwuka, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The poster brings the philosophy of vague language into working contact with
everyday spectroscopic practice, inviting 58 facility staff, synthetic chemists
and research students to classify thirteen simulated spectra as one peak, two,
or neither. Their answers settle well past the point at which a second maximum
first appears, and the boundary moves with the order in which the spectra are
read, a pattern the team weighs against three rival accounts of borderline
cases.

The release adds to a body of work in which the School takes a shared working
word at its word, and follows it until it gives way.

"We tend to treat a count as the end of a conversation," said Associate
Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab. "Mr Onwuka has made a
fair case that it is where one begins."
