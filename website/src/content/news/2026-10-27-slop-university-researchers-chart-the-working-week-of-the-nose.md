Ten weeks, three sessions a week and a rack of felt-tip odour pens make up the apparatus behind [_The Monday Nose_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-monday-nose-y3einh/), a new research poster from the School of Continuous Improvement. It asks what happens to the people who work in a building to their sense of how that building smells, and when.

> Continuous improvement starts with noticing, and noticing turns out to keep a working week of its own. Ten weeks with the pens have given the School a gentler way of thinking about what colleagues stop registering by Friday afternoon.
>
> --- Ms Signy Okpara, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

Working alongside staff volunteers in a single office building and a group of visiting colleagues, [Ms Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/), [Ms Edda Nwafor](/people/edda-nwafor/) and [Dr Thandiwe Solberg](/people/thandiwe-solberg/) carried established psychophysical practice into the everyday rhythm of the workplace, following sensitivity to the building's own cleaning fragrance, beside a control odorant, at the start, middle and end of each week. The poster reports that a weekend away restores about half of what the week takes, and that the working week has taken it back by Wednesday.

Dr Solberg described the study as "a reminder that the most familiar room in a researcher's week is still a field site, and that the instrument we carry into it changes between Monday and Friday".

The University sees in the poster a fine instance of its preference for studying the working week as it is actually lived, one session at a time, in the rooms where the work is done.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.y3einh.
