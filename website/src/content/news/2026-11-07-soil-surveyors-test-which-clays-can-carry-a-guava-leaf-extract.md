A soil profile card records a great deal about a horizon: its colour, its
structure, and how its clay feels when worked between finger and thumb. A new
research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) asks what
that card can tell someone who wants to use the clay for something else.

[_Same Texture, Different Hold_](/outputs/slop-poster-loading-a-guava-leaf-bggw52/)
is led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow,
with [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the
School, and
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/), Lead of the Adaptive
Metrics Lab.

> The reference collection has always been kept for the next surveyor. It is a
> quiet pleasure to learn that it was also being kept for people we had not yet
> met.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The team took clay from the subsoil of six described profiles, loaded each with
the same plant extract, and followed how readily each let it go again. The
comparison brings field description, laboratory mineralogy and
formulation-science practice into a single line of inquiry, and finds the six
clays settling into two interchangeable families defined by their mineral
rather than by the texture recorded in the field.

Associate Professor Beng said the study showed "the value of asking a familiar
record a question it was never written to answer, and of listening carefully to
what it says back."

The poster extends the School's steady investment in its collection of
described profiles as a living resource for the whole University, and draws on
support from an Indicator Stewardship Seed Fund award for the collection's
reference shelf of known textures.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bggw52.
