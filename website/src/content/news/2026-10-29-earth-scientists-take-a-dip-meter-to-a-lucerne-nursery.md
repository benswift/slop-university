Plant breeding is not, on the face of it, the business of an earth sciences school. [_Where the Water Stands_](/outputs/slop-paper-partial-resistance-to-qzg9dw/), a new paper from the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), makes the case that it can be, provided the plants are growing over the right kind of ground.

The paper is by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow. Its subject is root rot in lucerne, and its instrument is one the School knows well: a pipe in the ground, read once a week.

> The School came to this with no opinion about lucerne at all. What it had was a habit of asking how wet the ground is, and it turns out that habit travels further than we expected.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer

The study aligns soil-landscape survey, shallow groundwater observation and disease assessment within a single field design, positioning the hillslope itself as a screening platform for breeding programs working under variable winter conditions. Its findings are expected to inform how resistance is evaluated wherever the water in a paddock cannot be relied upon to stay in one place.

Dr Obiora acknowledged the many early mornings behind the result, and thanked "everyone who ever carried a dip meter down a wet slope without asking what it was for."

The University welcomes the paper as a sign of its earth scientists finding common ground with the field around them. The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qzg9dw.
