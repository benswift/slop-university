A cup on the folding writing tablet is now what keeps a lecture in its room.
A new paper from the School of Emergent Priorities sorts 47,440 seat
observations across 14 theatres into five kinds of tablet-arm use, and finds
that only the service-tray kind --- a cup, bottle, or food container resting
on the arm --- predicts whether the class returns in the same slot on the
following timetable.

Doctoral Candidate Hedvig Anyanwu led the study with Lecturer Lindiwe
Achterberg and Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master
of Priority Studies. Observers walked each taught hour three times, coding
the arm before they read the next period's timetable.

> We coded what was on the arm, not what the occupant said they were doing.
> The timetable appears to have been doing the same thing already.
>
> --- Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Writing-surface was the most common deployed kind and did not predict
retention. Lectures in the top tercile of weekly tray share reappeared at 88
percent; those in the bottom tercile at 41 percent. The School reads the
result as a reminder that a held priority is the one still sitting on the
furniture.

"Once the floor is in the circular, the room is what moves," said Associate
Professor Lindqvist. The Timetabling Office has adopted an 8 percent weekly
tray-share floor: two consecutive weeks below it returns the class to the
pool, with no ordinary override.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m6h1fc.
