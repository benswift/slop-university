Two sorters stood beside a container-return machine for 216 sessions and kept
every bottle and can it handed back. A paper published today through the Office
of Research Outputs reports what was in the pile.

Working from the scheme's published eligibility rules and without sight of the
machine's decision, School of Continuous Improvement researchers classified all
41,392 containers presented across fourteen return points in one metropolitan
network. Matched afterwards against eight quarters of transaction logs, the
machine proved almost never to refund something it should not have. What it did
do, increasingly often, was hand back containers that qualified: crushed,
scuffed, worn at the barcode, and coded out of scheme. By the final quarter,
nearly half of everything rejected under that code was eligible.

Because a container coded out of scheme is treated as never having been a
candidate for a refund, it also leaves the figure by which the return point is
judged. Correcting for that moves the network's published success rate from 94.2
per cent to 79.1.

> The people who kept coming back to us were the ones holding the bag at the
> end, and we would rather the record reflected their afternoon than ours.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director of the Master of Applied
> Measurement

The University regards the work as among the more serviceable things its
measurement programme has produced this year. "There is a version of this we
would like to run somewhere the machine is newer," said Dr Marit Osayande,
Lecturer and Convenor of the Living Dashboard, "and we are content to wait and
see what that turns up."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.iis0cs.
