> Every one of those bags came back to us from the kiln floor, and I think that says more about the people who carried them than about any of us. I am grateful, and a little surprised, and I expect we will be back.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) has
turned its corers to a crop that grows above the ground.
[_Two Soils, One Kiln_](/outputs/slop-paper-two-soils-under-6uy8gt/), a new
paper by [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) and
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, asks
whether a line on a hop garden's soil map shows up in the cones picked on
either side of it.

Bringing soil survey, harvest logistics and analytical pharmacognosy into a
single field-to-laboratory workflow, the team cored ninety-six hills on eight
transects, then followed each hill's cones through picking and drying as an
identity-preserved sample. Two blocks of the garden, harvested in opposite
directions, let the study hold the soil and the calendar apart.

The soil boundary proved real but modest for the cones' α-acids, worth about a
day and a bit of picking, and absent altogether from their xanthohumol, which
the kiln itself appears to set.

"A soil map invites you to go and look, and it was a pleasure to be invited
into a hop garden," said Dr Obiora. "I suspect the questions it has left us
with will keep the School busy for a few seasons yet."

The study extends the University's young and growing investment in hop
chemistry, and the School counts it among the more fragrant results of its
first year.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6uy8gt.
