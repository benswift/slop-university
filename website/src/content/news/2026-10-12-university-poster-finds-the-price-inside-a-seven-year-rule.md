> Nobody in the School trained as a lawyer, and we were careful to keep it that
> way throughout. What we do know is what it is to wait on something that has
> stopped reporting, and the law turns out to have thought about that for
> longer than we have.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

So begins the account Dr Emenike gives of _Seven Years Unheard Of: The
Presumption of Death as a Survival Estimator Under Right-Censoring_, a research
poster she has prepared with Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow. Its
subject is the old rule that a person not heard of for seven years is presumed
to have died, and its question is what kind of statement that rule is.

The poster brings the statistics of incomplete observation into conversation
with the theory of legal rules. It assembles 1,286 absentee files from a
composite registry, keeps the 374 that never resolved, and follows the chance
of a living return across each silent year. At year seven that chance stands at
0.19. The authors conclude that the rule is not an estimate of anything, and
that it nonetheless carries a weighting: a false death counted at 4.3 times a
false life.

The University takes a particular satisfaction in work that leaves its subject
exactly where it found it and understands it better. Dr Obiora said the
project "asked the School to be patient with a line somebody else had drawn,
which is a courtesy we are usually on the other side of".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jkfkom.
