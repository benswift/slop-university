For four and a half years, households in one state signed fixed-rate home loans
under a break-fee clause whose meaning the courts were still arguing over.
[_Priced Before It Was Settled_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-mortgage-lenders-mjwlpt/),
a new paper by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and
[Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/) of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), sets out what
that interval was worth to the lenders who wrote those loans, and to the
borrowers who took them.

> I spent a long time telling people which judgments still stood. Coming at the
> same question through a stack of rate sheets has made me a slower, and I hope
> a fairer, reader of the law reports.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The study brings appellate reasoning and household finance into a single frame,
reading each court decision as a signal that lenders absorbed, weighed and
returned to borrowers through the price of credit. It finds that the market had
treated the question as closed after a second appellate bench agreed with the
first, well before the profession stopped pleading the alternative, and that the
borrowers who fixed in the meantime and later moved on carried both sides of the
argument.

Associate Professor Lindqvist, who directs the University's Master of Priority
Studies, said the paper would "find its way onto the reading list well before
anyone has decided which week it belongs to."

The work joins a growing shelf of University scholarship on what a legal
question costs while it is still a question, and the University is pleased to
see its law school and its public-finance scholars writing that shelf together.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.mjwlpt.
