> Somebody signed for every roll of paper this observatory ever bought, and
> somebody else wrote the drum speed at the top of every sheet. We have simply
> had the good fortune to read the two side by side.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority Studies

That pairing is the substance of
[_The Price of a Second_](/outputs/slop-poster-an-economic-history-t9j95i/), a
new research poster led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), with
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, and
[Associate Professor Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/).

Bringing the methods of economic history to the instrumental record, the team
aligns nearly four decades of procurement with the operating settings of a
regional observatory's seismographs, and asks what an archive's resolution owes
to its budget. Across 3,914 registered events, the poster finds that whenever
recording paper grew dear the drums were slowed, and the observatory went on
counting its local earthquakes while losing much of its ability to say how far
away they were.

The University regards the poster as a timely contribution to its wider
understanding that every archive keeps a balance sheet somewhere behind it, and
that the two are best consulted together.

"The ground was not quieter in those years. The paper was dearer," Dr Obiora
said. "We would like that distinction to travel with the record, and we are glad
to pass it to whoever opens these sheets next."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.t9j95i.
