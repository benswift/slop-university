A flat white bought from the same coffee cart for three years turns out to be
a data set. A research poster released today by the School of Continuous
Improvement puts three years of that cart's till receipts through an
event-study, the method economists use to time how fast a price reacts to a
cost shock, and finds the board reacts twice as fast to bad news as to good.

_Sticky on the Way Down_, by Dr Osei Vandermeer and Dr Marit Osayande, timed
forty-two bean-cost changes against the day each drink's own price actually
moved. A rise clears the board within a week, on average; a cut takes closer
to two months, and the pattern holds across the flat white, the latte, the
long black and the chai latte alike. The team pre-registered a 120-day
adjustment window before pulling a single receipt, ruling out the possibility
that a slower fall was simply an earlier fall nobody had finished counting.

> Every pricing committee assumes it's watching one number. We found two: how
> fast bad news gets priced in, and how slowly good news does. The board
> never runs those past each other, but we can.
>
> --- Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor, Review Cadence
> Observatory, School of Continuous Improvement

Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor of the Living Dashboard, said the
next step is a second cart chosen specifically because its supplier changes
prices more often, "to see whether the lag shrinks or just moves." The
finding extends the School's running interest in what a number does once an
institution starts trusting it, and the team's working note on a
campus-wide Beverage Pricing Integrity Protocol is now circulating for
internal comment.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.zis8a9.
