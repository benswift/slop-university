The permit form had a list of purposes, and groundwater monitoring was not on
it. [_Riding on Supply_](/outputs/slop-paper-pandemic-border-exemptions-eugxy1/), a new paper by
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and
[Ms Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), follows what
that omission meant for the crews who drill across an internal border, over
the twenty-two months it stayed closed.

> The border has opened again, but the whiteboards are still up in a few
> depots, and we expect to be reading them for some time yet.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The study brings together contractors' own permit records and conversations
with drillers, offsiders and permitting staff, situating the everyday logistics
of a working rig within a wider conversation about pandemic mobility and
essential work. Its central finding is that monitoring work kept crossing,
nearly all of it alongside a supply job whose permit carried it through, and
that the closure quietly reshaped the workforce in ways the reopening did not
undo.

The collaboration is the kind of meeting between field science and the study
of institutions that the University's schools have long hoped to make room for.
Ms Anyanwu said the project had left her "with more questions about how work
moves than I brought to it, which feels like the right way round for a
doctorate."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.eugxy1.
