A deck of plain cards, a pair of lapel microphones and an invitation to "tell us
about this one" took a Slop University research team into the living rooms of
118 couples who share a home.

[_Not Mine, Not Ours_](/outputs/slop-paper-mine-yours-ours-9jmhbr/), a new paper
by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer in the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), and
[Mr Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), sets each
partner's way of speaking about the household's furniture, shelves and kettles
against the attachment questionnaire they completed beforehand. Partners less
comfortable with closeness spoke less often of things as "ours", and, the paper
finds, no more often of them as "mine". They called them "the".

> We listened to each household for about an hour, and every home gave us more
> than an hour could hold. I suspect the recordings will be telling us things
> for some time yet.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The study brings attachment research, close attention to everyday speech and a
careful household inventory method into a single frame for understanding how a
shared home is spoken into being, one noun phrase at a time.

Mr Onwuka said the walk-throughs had been "a good reminder that a home is always
partly a home for showing", and that he was glad to have been shown round so
many.

The University regards the paper as a fine instance of two of its Schools
discovering that they had been standing in the same room.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9jmhbr.
