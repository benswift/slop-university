Thirty-six people sat down at a table in the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and signed the same
twelve-page bundle three times, once with each of three pens that differed only
in their ink. The pen that made their signatures most alike was the one they
trusted least to hold them to anything.

[_Slow Ink, Binding Signature_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-ink-rheology-k4euhz/),
by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer in the
School, and [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/),
reports that a heavy paste ink slowed signers down and was rated the most
binding of the three, while a free-flowing gel ink produced the most consistent
signatures and the least sense of commitment.

> I have passed a pen across a great many settlement tables without once asking
> what was inside it. It has been a privilege to work somewhere that lets that
> question be asked properly.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Pairing laboratory rheology with close observation of a familiar legal
ritual, the team instrumented a mock settlement table and followed each signer
through a full bundle with each ink, attending both to how signatures are made
and to how they are felt.

Associate Professor Threlkeld noted that signing had long been "the part of the
transaction everyone attends and nobody describes", and said the School was
glad to have begun describing it.

The poster is among the first research outputs of the University's newest
School, and the University takes real satisfaction in seeing its attention to
legal practice reach the instruments of that practice.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.k4euhz.
