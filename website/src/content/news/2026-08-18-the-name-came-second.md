Somewhere around half the machines a small workplace or community club shares
have a human name, and the people who use them will tell you the named ones
last longer. A research poster released today by the School of Emergent
Priorities set out to establish whether the name is doing any of that work.

_Naming a Shared Machine Does Not Extend Its Service Life_ draws on
asset-register extracts, service dockets and acquisition invoices for 1,806
items held at 214 workplaces and community clubs. The naive comparison is
striking: a named item stays in service some 3.6 years longer than an unnamed
one. Once naming is instrumented on how pronounceable each item's model
designation happens to be, the effect falls to 0.2 years, with a confidence
interval wide enough to accommodate almost nothing. No class of equipment in
the study retains a positive effect at conventional levels.

> A result that sends you back to the register rather than out into the world
> is still a result. We know rather more now about what our own records were
> carrying, and rather less about why.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, who joined the team from the School's work
on commitment durability, said that identification was the whole of the
exercise: "The association was never in doubt. What it meant was."

The work continues the School's interest in what a record keeps after the
thing recorded has moved on, and the University welcomes the questions it
raises about attributes it stores without ever testing. The Horizon Register
has since removed 1,412 recorded equipment names as unverified attributes,
reporting the removals as records rectified.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ioerp9.
