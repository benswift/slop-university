Slop University's newest research poster went looking for the moment an
assembler stops turning pages. Working at share-house move-ins and
second-hand flat-pack stalls, researchers from the School of Emergent
Priorities intercepted 214 people mid-build, asked each to rate how clearly
they could picture the step in front of them before attempting it, then
logged the point at which the wordless booklet was set aside for a video.

The rating held up. Once a self-reported vividness score dropped below the
middle of its five-point scale, the odds of switching rose sharply --- and
they rose on panels the study had independently coded as straightforward.
Mirrored and rotated panels predicted a switch on their own terms, cited at
61 per cent of logged switch points, well ahead of any other trigger the
team coded.

> The interesting people were the ones holding a genuinely easy panel who
> still couldn't see themselves doing it. That was never a fact about the
> furniture.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Switchers went on to report fewer errors, though not faster builds --- a
distinction Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities
and a co-author on the poster, said he was glad the study surfaced rather
than settled. "We can now ask a better version of the question we walked in
with, which is most of what a field study owes anybody."

The work is the University's second study of mental-imagery vividness
conducted outside a laboratory, following earlier fieldwork at building
directory boards, and the University regards the pairing as the kind of
continuity a research programme is meant to accumulate.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dzt0rh.
