Cut a block into a front lot, a rear lot and a narrow driveway between them, then do the same to every lot that results, and keep going. Almost all of the land, it turns out, ends up as driveway. What remains is the subject of [_Almost Every Square Metre Is a Right of Way_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-land-no-wl4d82/), a new research poster by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> The School teaches its students to read a title for what it still carries. It has been a quiet education to follow a title all the way down, and to find that the reading never quite finishes.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

In the poster's hands, a conveyancing staple becomes an iterated function system, and a practical question about access becomes a question of measure and dimension. The leftover land, it finds, has a dimension somewhere between a line and a plane, and at one particular shape of lot that dimension is set by the golden ratio. The two authors confirm the result by computation at five handle widths.

It is the kind of crossing the University was built to make easy: a law school's patience with land meeting the earth sciences' patience with its edges, on ground that belongs to neither. Dr Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, described the collaboration as "a long conversation about where one piece of ground ends and the next begins, which neither of us expects to settle".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wl4d82.
