Two visits to the same escape room, six to ten weeks apart, returned almost
the same verdict on how its hints felt to receive. That agreement anchors
Slop University's newest research poster, which turns 214 groups'
hint-by-hint self-reports into the first instrument built to score a room's
difficulty by what a hint costs a player, not how many the game master hands
out.

The poster extends the School of Continuous Improvement's long-running
interest in what an indicator actually tracks into a setting the School has
not previously visited. Dr Dagny Okafor and Dr Mirela Hanke developed the
Hint Fatigue Index (HFI) as a seven-point item completed immediately after
each hint, then retested it across eighteen commercial rooms at four
metropolitan venues. Mean HFI scores held within 0.3 points of themselves
between sessions on all four advertised difficulty tiers (ICC = 0.88).

> A hint count only tells you the room reached for help. It doesn't tell you
> how the help landed. We wanted the number that does.
>
> --- Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation

Item-level analysis places most of the HFI's variance in its Cadence
subscale --- how a hint is paced, not how many arrive --- ahead of Delivery
and Tone. A commercial escape-room booking platform has begun piloting
HFI-derived tiers as a difficulty-certification badge across its listed
venues, in place of operators' own self-declared ratings. Dr Hanke, a
Postdoctoral Fellow in the School, called the pilot "the part of this worth
watching longest," and said the team would want the badge checked against
independent completion-rate data before it spreads much further.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gez3ke.
