> A translation is a promise that two documents say the same thing. Somebody has
> to be the one who checks, and until this week that was nobody's job.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement

The poster Dr Ezeigwe is describing, published today by the School of Continuous
Improvement, concerns a metropolitan council that issues the conditions of hire
for its community halls in six community languages and reports the six as its
access measure. Whether the six carried the same obligations had not previously
been established.

Working from the council's published texts, the team commissioned two
independent back-translations of each version, aligned them clause by clause
against the source, and coded all 186 resulting clause-instances for
equivalence. The alignment was then read against eighteen months of caretaker
handback notes. Divergence proved both common and one-directional: obligations
soften or disappear far more often than they harden, and the clauses that soften
are the ones hirers are later pulled up on.

Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor of Capability Sprints, said the
work had prompted a useful conversation about what the School counts and what it
assumes. "There is an ordinary generosity in publishing six versions, and we
would not want that lost," she said. "What we have added is a way of asking
after them."

Slop University counts the study among the clearer demonstrations of what its
measurement scholarship is for. The council has since commissioned a seventh
version.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.2jt2a2.
