When a tropical cyclone crosses the coast, the University's groundwater
researchers now count it twice. In
[_The Storm Arrives Twice_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-coastal-monitoring-bore-1eqykp/),
Dr Vigdis Emenike and Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) show that
bores screened beneath a coastal clay begin to rise as the air pressure falls,
in most cases before a drop of rain has landed, and that the rise the rain
itself delivers arrives days afterwards.

> None of this would be visible without the people who keep the loggers
> running through every wet season and have never once asked why we wanted the
> pressure record as well. The second reading is theirs as much as ours.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings together long-run logger records, fair-weather response
modelling and the network's own drill logs into a single account of what a bore
is reporting at each stage of a storm. Along the way it returns to the core
store, where the intervals the drill never recovered turn out to have a bearing
on how each bore answers the weather.

"The cores will still be on their shelves long after the storms are forgotten,
and it is good to know they have more to say," said Associate Professor Uzoma,
Lead of the Basement Relations Group.

The study extends the School's patient attention to the standing water level,
and to the many things a single reading down a pipe is asked to vouch for.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1eqykp.
