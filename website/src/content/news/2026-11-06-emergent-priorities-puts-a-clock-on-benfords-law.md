How long does a new set of accounts take to settle? The
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), which has made a
study of how long institutions take to catch up with themselves, has now put
that question to the leading digits of a ledger.

[_Not Yet Benford_](/outputs/slop-poster-leading-digits-as-dn6694/), a new
research poster led by [Dr Runa Adegoke](/people/runa-adegoke/), Senior
Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey, with
[Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/), Director of
the Master of Priority Studies, and doctoral candidate
[Tove Ilesanmi](/people/tove-ilesanmi/), borrows the language of theoretical
physics to describe a familiar pattern in financial records as an equilibrium
that has to be reached rather than a property a ledger simply has.

> Every institution I have studied has been in a hurry to be judged. It has been
> a quiet pleasure to work on something that asks, very gently, for a little
> more time.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

The team brought a statistical-physics lens to bear on 312 cost centres in one
anonymised institutional ledger, treating each year's indexation as a small step
around a circle and tracing how quickly a newly opened account's figures arrive
at the distribution of first digits known as Benford's law. Accounts opened in
the last few budget cycles, the poster reports, carry most of the flags a
standard conformity screen raises, while a comparison that ages alongside each
account keeps its attention on the cases it was built to find.

The School counts the poster among the steadier expressions of a conviction it
has held from the start, that a system is best read at its own pace. "What
counts as settled is a question we expect to keep company with for some time,"
Associate Professor Lindqvist said.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dn6694.
