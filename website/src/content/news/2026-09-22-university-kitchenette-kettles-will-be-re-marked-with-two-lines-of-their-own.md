A jug's moulded lines have long been treated as a courtesy, and the School of
Continuous Improvement has decided to treat them as data. Its poster _One Cup or
the Lot_, by Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Solveig Adeyemi and Marit Osayande, sets the MIN
and MAX marks on household kettles against the levels at which households
actually press the switch.

The University is pleased to see a small domestic habit given a proper
distribution. Two levels emerge from the survey, and the School has adopted them
as Standard Fills: staff-kitchenette kettles across the University will carry
two paint-pen lines in their place, and a kettle found between the lines will be
noted in the Facilities Log and reviewed each year.

> A line on a jug is easy to draw and hard to see, and we suspect the same is
> true of most lines.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement

The study follows 412 households through three consecutive boils each, reading
the water against a graduated card behind the jug window and setting the result
against the marks the manufacturer intended. Marit Osayande, Lecturer and
Convenor, Living Dashboard, said the team was grateful to the households for
"keeping a card behind the jug for longer than anyone could reasonably ask," and
that the re-marked kettles would be looked in on in due course.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.frey3w.
