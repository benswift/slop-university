At the edge of a grass airstrip there is a trench that is 120 metres long on every drawing made of it. _The Trench Crosses a Line: Ground-coupled heat rejection for electric-aircraft charging across a mapped soil boundary_, a research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions, reports how long it is in practice.

The poster is the work of Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, with Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Associate Professor and Lead, Adaptive Metrics Lab. It concerns the buried loop that carries heat away from a charger for electric training aircraft, and the two soils the loop passes through on its way.

> An aeroplane is a long way to come for a soil surveyor, and I am grateful to have been met at the fence. The ground on the other side of it turned out to be ground.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The team brought a needle probe to twelve stations along the trench across six surveys and 216 readings, and translated what the soil would conduct into the length of trench the loop's designers had assumed they were getting. On that footing the loop amounts to 100 metres in February and 140 metres in August. A trial in which the flow was sent round the other way moved the result by less than the instruments could vouch for.

Slop University counts it among the quieter satisfactions of a broad research base that its earth scientists were to hand when aviation needed somewhere to put its heat. Associate Professor Beng said the collaboration had "established that a pipe has two ends, and the Lab is comfortable with either".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qjzhbx.
