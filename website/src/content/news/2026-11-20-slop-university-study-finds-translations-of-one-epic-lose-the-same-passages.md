When twenty-three translators carry one long poem into nine languages, independently and across many generations, they turn out to leave behind the same stretches of it. [_The Same Soft Beds_](/outputs/slop-poster-core-loss-in-9b2o03/), a new research poster from the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), sets that pattern out canto by canto, and places a field geologist's working habits at the centre of comparative reading.

> In the core shed you learn to look at the empty part of the tray before the full part. A translated poem has trays of its own, and it was a pleasure to finally lay them side by side.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group, School of Underlying Conditions

The team treated each translation as a borehole through a shared original, aligning every line against the source and logging what each version brought up, what it left behind and where a summary or a translator's note had been set in the gap. Read together, the twenty-three logs show nine cantos holding 61% of all the lines that did not come across, among them the poem's catalogues of local places, a genealogy and a refrain in dialect.

The poster brings stratigraphic correlation, translation studies and archival reading practice into one shared frame for comparison, and it forms part of the School's wider programme on the intervals a record does not recover. It also enlarges the Basement Relations Group's interest in what a log is entitled to say about material nobody has seen.

[Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the School of Settled Matters, who co-authored the work with doctoral candidate [Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/), described it as "a reminder that every reading inherits someone else's choices about what to bring home".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9b2o03.
