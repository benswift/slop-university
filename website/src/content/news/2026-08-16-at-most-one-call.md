The list that decides which pedestrian crossings get rebuilt is ordered on a
number that cannot count past one. _One Call Per Cycle_, published today by the
School of Emergent Priorities, sets a signal controller's own record of
pedestrian demand against a count of the people who actually arrived at the
kerb, and reports that the record holds 31.9 per cent of them. A phase accepts a
single call however many are waiting, so the compression is heaviest where the
waiting is: at the school-zone sites in the afternoon band, one logged call
stands for a mean of 6.4 arrivals.

> The instrument is doing exactly what it was built to do. What we would like to
> see settled is what the network wants counted, because that is a different
> decision and nobody has had to make it yet.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority
> Studies

The work pairs 214 hours of kerbside observation with a matched extract of the
controller's own call log, and organises what it finds into a nine-part scheme
covering delegation, redundancy, reassurance and the several ways an arrival
reaches a crossing without ever registering as one. A companion intercept survey
of 612 pedestrians found the belief that pressing brings the walk phase sooner
holding steady whether or not the button was connected.

"Nine categories is more than anybody wants, and we did try four," said Dr Marek
Solheim, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the Horizon Register. "The four-category
version returns the same answer, which we take as the scheme earning its keep
rather than failing to."

The University welcomes the study into its anticipatory-capability agenda, where
an indicator inherited for one purpose tends to be asked to serve several. The
full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open
licence, doi:10.5555/slop.x58j8t.
