The notification that a driver has arrived is, for most operators in a new Slop University study, a report from somewhere else. A research poster from the School of Continuous Improvement, led by Lecturer Dr Solveig Adeyemi with Senior Lecturer Dr Torun Ezeigwe and Lecturer Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, scores fifty-four operators against five named Arrival-Ping Maturity levels, and records the reimbursement rule the University has attached to the result.

Thirty-three of the fifty-four operators sit at Pinned. At that level the median ping-to-pin distance is forty-nine metres; at Sighted it is twelve. Fifty-eight per cent of the 12,408 observed pings fire more than forty metres from the booked pin.

> The interesting case is the operator that publishes a protocol and still pings from two doors down.
>
> --- Lecturer Dr Solveig Adeyemi, School of Continuous Improvement

Kerbside observers with a laser rangefinder recorded each rider-facing arrival ping against the booked pin over eighteen weeks. Levels were assigned from each operator's published pickup protocol before the first observation and were not adjusted afterwards.

The release extends the School's work on whether a named level can be asked to carry an allocation. Staff and HDR fieldwork travel is now reimbursable only where the operator sits at Sighted or Attested. A ping more than forty metres from the booked pin is an ineligible fare, with no override. The School will publish a quarterly Arrival Maturity Register; an operator that drops a level mid-semester has remaining booked jobs cancelled.

Dr Ezeigwe, who directs the Master of Applied Measurement, said "named levels give an organisation a way to say what it thinks it is doing, and a way for a later register to disagree."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.opoj9y.
