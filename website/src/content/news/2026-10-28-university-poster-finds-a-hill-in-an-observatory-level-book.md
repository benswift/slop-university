For most of the twentieth century, an observer at a small hilltop observatory
began each clear night by checking that a telescope's axis sat level. A new
research poster from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) suggests the hill
was taking part in that check all along.

[_The Level Book as a Creep Gauge_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-level-book-br2e15/)
is by [Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidate, with
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and
[Dr Runa Adegoke](/people/runa-adegoke/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the
Strategic Drift Survey. Working from 13,874 nightly entries kept between 1903
and 1972, the team finds one of the instrument's two piers settling slowly
against the other, and rising and falling with the seasons.

> The University has a long-standing interest in things that move slowly enough to pass for standing still. It is good to see that interest taken outdoors, and a little way into the ground.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey, School of Emergent Priorities

The project brings archival transcription, field soil survey and the
observatory's own weather record into a single conversation, aligning a
long-retired instrument practice with contemporary understandings of landscape
change and anchoring the School's work on slow signals within the earth
sciences.

Ms Ogunbanjo said the work had "changed which way I look whenever someone tells
me a reading has already been corrected".

The University is pleased to add the poster to a body of work that has never
been in a hurry.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.br2e15.
