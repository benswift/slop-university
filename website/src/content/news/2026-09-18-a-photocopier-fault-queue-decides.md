A shared office photocopier does not know who is asking it to be fixed. The
complexity-scored ticket queue that now decides how fast it gets fixed,
Slop University's Strategic Plan 2026-2031 reports, does --- or something
close enough to matter. Published today, the plan commits the University to
a five-year program auditing every service queue it runs, after an interview
study of the photocopier's own users found that how they worded a fault
report shaped whether it moved.

The finding, from the School of Emergent Priorities, holds no surprises:
staff who had learned the queue's preferred phrasing saw their faults
resolved within a day, and staff who described the problem plainly, more
often, did not. What the plan does with it is the news. Dr Lindiwe
Achterberg, the plan's lead author, and Dr Solveig Adeyemi of the School of
Continuous Improvement have carried the finding into a University-wide
Plain-Language Parity Protocol: no service queue will be permitted to route
a request by algorithm until an independent human reviewer has cleared it of
the same bias, on a published quarterly cycle.

> We didn't set out to audit a photocopier. We set out to find out whether
> the University hears itself the way it thinks it does.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

"A queue this ordinary tends to be the one nobody thinks to check," said Dr
Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer, School of Continuous Improvement. "That's exactly
why we checked it." The plan's authors describe the five years ahead as the
University holding its own routine infrastructure to the scrutiny it already
expects of an external partner.

The full plan is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.r1eq1x.
