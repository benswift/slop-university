For one term, researchers from the School of Emergent Priorities and the
School of Continuous Improvement counted every sticky note on one office
floor rather than asking anyone what was written on them. The resulting
paper, published today, treats the floor's 186 desks as a population and
each note's undisturbed tenure as a measurable lifetime, tracked weekly
across the term.

The count found what a floor's own geometry would predict: notes near the
office manager's desk changed hands in days, while notes at the room's far
corners lingered for months. What the count did not anticipate was how
quickly its own summary statistic would travel. Seven weeks into the survey,
Facilities read the population's ninetieth-percentile note age as the
trigger threshold for a paperless-desk mandate, and had extended the same
threshold to the building's physical noticeboards before the researchers'
own final sweep was complete.

> A note doesn't ask to be counted. Once you count the whole floor anyway,
> someone downstream starts treating the count as if it always meant to be a
> rule.
>
> --- Marek Solheim, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor, Horizon Register

"The noticeboards were never part of our sample," said Thandiwe Solberg,
Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Demo Quarter. "That the threshold reached them
anyway says more about how a number travels once it leaves a study than
about anything we set out to measure."

The study treats a floor as a population rather than a handful of examples,
following every visible note across a full term and retiring the ones no one
touched again. The resulting picture, closer to an actuarial table than a
tidy-desk audit, is offered to Facilities as description; the finding sits
comfortably inside the University's wider project of turning ambient
workplace habit into an auditable population.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.npwvec.
