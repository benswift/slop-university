Ninety-two percent of the time, a venue door's ultraviolet lamp correctly
read a genuine same-night return. Just over three times in five, it read a
researcher-made copy the same way. A new Slop University research poster
from the School of Continuous Improvement puts a figure on what door staff
have mostly taken on faith: that a hand glowing the right colour is the
same hand that walked in earlier.

Researchers pressed genuine and transferred ultraviolet stamps onto
patrons at nine venues across fourteen weekend nights, keeping door staff
blind to which mark was which. A napkin-press copy and a friend's-palm
copy fared about the same under the lamp, and neither method needed
particular skill to pull off. Door-tally reconciliation, run alongside the
trial, told a matching story: nightly headcounts trailed ticket sales by
nine percent on average, widening at the venues with the longest queues.

> A stamp answers exactly one question, and it is not the one door staff
> are asking it.
>
> --- Senior Lecturer and Convenor Thandiwe Solberg, Demo Quarter, School
> of Continuous Improvement

"The lamp was never wrong about what it's built to check," said Lecturer
and Convenor Dagny Okafor, Evaluation of Evaluation, School of Continuous
Improvement. "We just asked it a question it was never built to answer."

The University frames the result as a modest addition to a School-wide
effort to keep its verification instruments honest about what they
verify.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7ro5or.
