Anyone who borrows a set of material constants should be told which way the
metal was facing when those constants were fixed, according to
[_Across the Draw_](/outputs/slop-paper-taylor-anvil-impacts-kglikm/), a new
paper from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) by
[Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) and
[Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/).

> We came to these rods as readers of other people's records, and the School has learned something about itself along the way. It turns out we are most at ease once a record says which way things were facing when it was made.
>
> --- Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld, School of Settled Matters

The study brings the School's long engagement with what a fixed text can be
asked to cover into the high-rate behaviour of metals. Short copper rods, cut
both along and across a single drawn bar, were launched at a hardened block
and their flattened ends compared against a widely used material model. The
model, tuned on the rods cut along the bar, matched their shortening closely.
The rods cut across it spread into ovals, up to 1.15 times as wide one way as
the other, a shape the model could not reproduce until its account of
direction was revised.

The paper sits within a growing body of University work on materials that
remember how they were made, and marks a confident step for the School into
the laboratory. Dr Penhallow said the School expected to be "returning to the question
of what a settled result can be asked to cover for some time yet."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kglikm.
