How many rays should a simulation spend, and where should it spend them? [_Allocating the Ray Budget_](/outputs/slop-paper-spending-the-ray-rkjtnu/), a paper released today by the School of Emergent Priorities, brings a question familiar from public budgeting to the modelling of heat in furnaces and chambers, and returns with an answer the field may not have expected to hear from a school of foresight.

The paper is the work of [Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/), Director of the Master of Priority Studies, and [Dr Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/), Postdoctoral Fellow in the School.

> The School has always held that a budget says what an institution values. We are grateful to the colleagues who let us test that conviction against something as unforgiving as heat, and we come back from the exercise a little more careful with our own spreadsheets.
>
> --- [Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/), Director, Master of Priority Studies

Working across three simulated enclosures, the study joins sampling theory to a close accounting of where each unit of computational effort lands. Its central finding is that once the simulated estimates are corrected after the fact, the most valuable rays are the ones traced from the small surface under study rather than towards it. In one case the redesigned budget proved more than three hundred times as efficient as customary practice.

The University regards the paper as a natural extension of its standing interest in how finite resources reach the places that need them. Dr Mensah said the work had "given the School a new vocabulary for an old habit of mind", and that the next question was the one the School asks of every budget: who decided where the first share went.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.rkjtnu.
