Most of what a streaming service decides about its own catalogue, a new paper
argues, it can safely be trusted to decide. The difficulty lies in a narrow
strip of titles at the edge of each rating band, and that strip is the subject
of
[_Judge in Its Own Catalogue_](/outputs/slop-paper-judge-in-its-l1tdg9/), by
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> A university sorts things all day long, and it is easy to forget that someone is standing at every one of those lines. I would be glad if the paper helped a few more of us notice where we are standing.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Situated where political theory, regulatory scholarship and the governance of
digital platforms meet, the paper brings a long-standing principle of
impartial judgment to bear on schemes in which providers classify their own
titles. It positions the borderline case, rather than the catalogue as a
whole, as the place where institutional design must concentrate its
attention, and it sets out a declared-margin arrangement through which
providers and reviewers might share that work.

For Dr Obiora, whose earlier work asks how a line drawn through gradual ground
comes to be read as a fact about it, the rating band is new ground to survey.
The University is pleased to watch one of political theory's oldest questions
settle so comfortably into its earth sciences school.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.l1tdg9.
