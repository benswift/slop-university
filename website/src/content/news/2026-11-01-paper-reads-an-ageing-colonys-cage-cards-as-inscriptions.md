> A soil profile card and a cage card are both forms that someone filled in standing up. Reading one teaches you a good deal about reading the other, and we were glad of the chance to find out how much.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

With that, the School of Underlying Conditions turns from the soil pit to the
animal house. [_Vixit Dies_](/outputs/slop-paper-an-epigraphic-edition-ulv046/)
is by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and
[Ms Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/). It takes one
archive box of retired cage cards from a mouse colony kept to study long life,
and treats the box the way a scholar of ancient inscriptions treats a
collection of stones.

The paper situates the humble cage card within a long tradition of
commemorative writing, mobilising the conventions of the critical edition to
surface the printings, hands and abbreviations through which a colony
recorded its deaths. Its closing pages offer an edition of five cards, set out
in full with apparatus and commentary.

At its centre are the sixty-three cards on which a technician drew or stuck a
small star. The authors find that the star follows the hand that wrote it and
the last animal left in a cage, more closely than it follows the animal's age.

Ms Balogun said the project had shown the School "how much an institution keeps
by accident, and how patiently it waits to be read." The paper is the
University's newest contribution to the study of the laboratory's own records,
and it reflects a growing readiness across both schools to let a document
answer questions it was never asked.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ulv046.
