Why should a bottle that leaves the still perfectly clear arrive at the shop the colour of milk? That question, put to the University by a small bitters maker, is the starting point of [_Where the Tonic Louches_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-the-tonic-0favvn/), a new research poster from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

The work is led by doctoral candidate Ms [Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), with Ms [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) and Dr [Petra Umbile](/people/petra-umbile/). Across fourteen winter batches, the team finds that ten have a cloud point above the coldest morning their delivery van recorded, and that these ten are precisely the batches the maker's customers sent back.

> We came to the tonic expecting to find a fault and are leaving with a map, which is the more useful thing to have. I suspect the map will be asked to do other work before the winter is out.
>
> --- Ms Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

At the poster's centre sits a phase map of oil, alcohol and water, assembled from careful bench titration and set against the lived temperature profile of a regional delivery round. It connects the thermodynamics of mixing with the everyday logistics of a craft producer, and positions formulation as a shared question between the bench and the road.

Dr Umbile said the team had been "fortunate in a maker who keeps better notes than most laboratories, and in a van that was willing to be cold on schedule."

The University regards the poster as an early harvest from its deepening relationships with the makers in its own neighbourhood, and as evidence that a careful afternoon at the burette can travel a long way.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.0favvn.
