At twenty to ten every morning for half a year, the same four suburban shopping
strips were photographed clock by clock. A paper released today by the School
of Emergent Priorities reports what those 33,742 face-readings add up to: on
the first day after the autumn changeover, 83 of the strips' 187 public-facing
clock faces showed the old hour, and by the following spring nearly a third of
them still did --- corrected in the end not by any hand, but by the changeover
coming back around.

_Time to Correction_, by Dr Fenna Okoro, Dr Ingrid Vasseur and Dr Petra Umbile,
harmonises daily photography with competing-risks modelling into an account of
who corrects a clock, who removes one, and who waits. Analogue faces were
reset at nearly twice the rate of digital ones, and the strips' quarterly
presentation audits --- which keep a defect category for a stopped clock but
none for a wrong one --- were found to move only the stopped ones. The paper
extends the School's long project on the readings a street takes on trust.

> The changeover arrives on schedule whether or not anyone is ready for it,
> and there is something in that the School is still learning from. We are
> grateful to the four strips for leaving their clocks exactly as they were.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, noted that the panel "asked of the high
street nothing it was not already displaying".
