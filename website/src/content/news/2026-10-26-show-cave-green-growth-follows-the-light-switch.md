The School of Underlying Conditions has made its name on what lies beneath the
surface. Its newest paper turns to what grows on top of it.

[_What the Switching Log Grows_](/outputs/slop-paper-lampenflora-on-the-z31c2z/)
is by [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, with [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/),
Senior Research Fellow, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior
Lecturer. It follows the green films that gather on stalagmites and flowstone
under a show cave's tour lighting, in the eighteen months after every lamp on
the circuit was replaced with an LED.

> We went underground expecting the rock to have the last word. It turns out the clock has a good deal to say as well, and the School is still getting used to listening to it.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

By integrating quadrat-scale photometry, repeat imaging and the cave
controller's own switching records into a single dose-informed framework, the
team offers a whole-of-circuit perspective on light, moisture and time at the
rock face. Their analysis suggests the films are better explained by how long
each zone of lights stays on than by how bright the new lamps are, and that a
change to the controller's timer made at commissioning carried much of the
weight.

For the University, the paper extends an underground research agenda that now
reaches from the air moving through a cave to the light falling inside one.

"Every lit surface is, in its way, a record," said Dr Obiora. "There are a great
many more of them waiting to be read."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.z31c2z.
