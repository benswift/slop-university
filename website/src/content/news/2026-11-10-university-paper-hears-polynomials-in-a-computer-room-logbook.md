"Count the clicks to the stop and you have the order." The instruction, pasted inside the cover of a computing laboratory's sixth logbook, is the starting point for [_Counting Down by Ear_](/outputs/slop-paper-operators-of-an-d432ng/), a new paper by [Mr Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), Doctoral Candidate in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

The paper brings the methods of musicology to an archive no one had thought to listen to: fourteen operator logbooks and nine reels of tape from a valve-era machine whose console loudspeaker clicked through every calculation it ran. Transcribing the library routine that found the roots of polynomials, Mr Nnamani identifies a regular metre in the machine's working, and shows that the operators learnt to hear the shape of each problem in front of them, naming what they heard in a vocabulary of ladders, hangs and double steps. In nineteen recorded runs with a written remark beside them, the remark and the transcription agree in seventeen.

> Most of what these operators knew went home with them at the end of a shift. The remarks column is the part that stayed behind, and I have tried to read it as carefully as they listened.
>
> --- Mr Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The work extends the School's interest in what gets written into the spaces a form leaves open, and brings a new instrument, the transcription, to that agenda. It sits alongside recent University studies of listening at the test-cell glass and of what survives in a departed colleague's notes, and adds a historical voice to that growing conversation across the University's Schools.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.d432ng.
