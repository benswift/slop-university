A time written in marker pen on a seed bag has become the deciding document in
one cropping district's disputes over failed pulse crops, and a new paper sets
out how it got there.
[_Whose Hours Are These?_](/outputs/slop-paper-who-holds-the-a95oq9/) is by
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), with
[Dr Anneke Tolan](/people/anneke-tolan/), Senior Research Fellow and Deputy
Convenor of Futures in Committee in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> I have dug a great many pits to find out what happened underground. This time the more useful digging was in the yard, in the few hours before anything went into the ground at all.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Two sowing seasons in the yards, sheds and cabs of a pulse-growing district
allow the authors to situate a small handwritten practice within the wider
scholarship on coordination, handover and shared responsibility across
organisational boundaries. The paper traces how the tag moved from a
contractor's courtesy to a grower's deadline, and how the goodwill that once
settled most complaints came to occupy the cases the tag could not reach.

For Dr Tolan, the study is one the School of Emergent Priorities will carry
into its own work on how plans are made. "We spend a good deal of time on
documents that set out who does what," she said. "It is salutary to meet one
that fits on a luggage tag."

The paper marks a further step in the University's growing attention to the
working arrangements of rural industry, and to the patient fieldwork they
reward.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.a95oq9.
