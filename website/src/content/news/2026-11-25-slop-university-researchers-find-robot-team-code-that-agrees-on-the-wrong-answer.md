A team of robots can settle on the wrong answer and still pass every test its programmers wrote for it, because those tests mostly ask whether the robots agree.

That is the central finding of [_It Still Converges_](/outputs/slop-poster-it-still-converges-5j4cjr/), a new research poster by Dr [Sten Okwuosa](/people/sten-okwuosa/), Associate Professor [Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/) and Dr [Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/) of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/). Of 312 repaired defects in software that keeps groups of robots in step, 146 still brought every machine to a common value, just not the one the underlying mathematics promises. The laboratories' own test suites flagged nine of them.

> I came to this School from control rooms, where a steady reading was what everyone hoped for at the end of a shift. It is good to be reminded that steady and right are two different words, and to work among colleagues patient enough to tell them apart.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab

The team's approach pairs repository mining with structured defect classification and proof-informed testing, following coordination algorithms on their journey from the published page into everyday laboratory practice across twenty-three research groups.

The study carries the School's long-standing interest in measurement systems into the code that keeps machines moving together, and the University is pleased to see that interest travel so far from home.

"We are grateful to every group that opened its history to us," said Dr Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas, "and to the many contributors whose careful repairs made the study possible."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.5j4cjr.
