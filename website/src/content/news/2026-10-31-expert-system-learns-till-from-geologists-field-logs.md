Telling a glacial till from the debris flow that has reworked it is one of the
oldest judgement calls in field geology, and a new paper asks where that
judgement is kept: in what geologists say about it, or in what they have
written down at the outcrop.

[_What the Section Logs Knew_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-rule-based-expert-jgky9c/)
is by [Dr Fenna Okoro](/people/fenna-okoro/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the
Horizon Register in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> We owe this paper to nine colleagues who were generous with their afternoons
> and more generous still with their filing cabinets. The School is grateful to
> everyone who has ever taken the trouble to fill in the remarks line.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

The study situates knowledge engineering within the everyday practice of
Quaternary mapping, setting an interview-led rule base beside one learned from
the field record and testing both against exposures neither had seen. Across 86
blind sections the log-based system came within two calls of the geologists
themselves, while the interview-based system found the debris flows hardest of
all.

The work sits comfortably within the University's growing interest in what its
records know before its people are ready to say it. Associate Professor Uzoma
said the paper had left the School of Underlying Conditions "with some very good
questions about its own log sheets, which is exactly where a field season ought
to leave a school".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jgky9c.
