A hand auger brings up a little more soil on some turns and a little less on
others, and surveyors are usually trained to even that out. [_The Uneven
Lift_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-uneven-lift-znmmki/), a research poster from the
School of Underlying Conditions, suggests the unevenness is worth keeping.

The poster is by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research
Fellow, with [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and Convenor of
Improvement Grand Rounds, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/),
Senior Lecturer.

> Every record the University keeps began as somebody's habit. It is good to be part of a School that stops, now and then, to ask which of its habits have been quietly doing the work.
>
> --- [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

Working from 212 holes logged in a single map unit, and reading them twice over,
once as written in the field and once as the standard field sheet would have
recorded them, the authors set the School's survey practice alongside the
mathematics of incomplete observation. Read as written, the holes locate the
middle of the clay's depth to within a few centimetres; read from the sheet,
they can place it only somewhere in a ten-centimetre band.

It is the kind of patient, close-to-the-ground inquiry the University has always
counted among its quieter strengths. Dr Emenike described the work as "a
reminder that the field book and the method have always been in conversation,
whether or not anyone was listening to both at once."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.znmmki.
