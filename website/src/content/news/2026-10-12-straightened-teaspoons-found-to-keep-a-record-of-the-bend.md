What does a teaspoon look like on the inside once somebody has bent it and
pressed it straight again? The School of Emergent Priorities has taken the
question to the bench in _Where the Spoon Remembers: Strain-hardening gradients
at the neck of bent-and-straightened teaspoons_, a research poster by Ms Hedvig
Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate, with Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in
the School of Underlying Conditions, and Tove Ilesanmi, Doctoral Candidate.

The team took the full contents of one tearoom drawer, 52 spoons of a single
pattern, replaced them like-for-like, and cut each one lengthwise. Of the 21
spoons that had been bent and straightened, all carried a hardened zone within
12 mm of the neck, measuring 3.18 GPa against 2.41 GPa in the 31 that had never
been bent. Grain size was the same in both groups. Four spoons carried two
hardened zones, side by side.

> You spend a career being told that a thing put back the way it was is the
> same thing. I would not want to generalise from a drawer, but the drawer did
> not seem to think so.
>
> --- Ms Hedvig Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The poster brings sectioning, indentation and diffraction mapping into a single
characterisation pathway, aligning what a specimen looks like with what it has
been through. It is the kind of patient, close-range work the University likes
to be found doing when nobody has asked.

Dr Obiora described the collaboration as "a useful reminder that the ground is
not the only material with a past".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.a3vfzu.
