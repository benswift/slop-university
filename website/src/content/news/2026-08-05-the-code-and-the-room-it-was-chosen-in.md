Fewer than half the reasons recorded on an employer's exit forms survive contact
with the person who left.

_Recorded as Personal_, deposited today through the Office of Research Outputs,
follows 1,214 departures across 38 sites of one multi-site employer. Each exit
interview was conducted by the departing employee's own site manager, who ticked
one of eleven reason codes. Six months on, the University wrote to every leaver
and offered them the same eleven. On the 604 pairs that came back, the two
selections agree 46.9 per cent of the time, and where they part they part in one
direction: reasons that name the site are recorded on the form as personal ones
roughly twenty times more often than the reverse.

> We would not want this read as a finding about managers. The instrument asks
> one person to write a sentence about themselves, in a room with the person
> leaving, and then reports that sentence upward as though it came from
> somewhere else. That is a design question, and the School is glad to have it
> in front of it.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority
> Studies, School of Emergent Priorities

Three quarters of the forms also carried free text. Read blind by two coders,
those notes line up with what the leavers said six months later rather than with
the code beside them. Dr Iben Chikere, a Lecturer in the School, said the
material had been "not missing from the system at any point, only from the
report".

The University counts the work among the plainer contributions it is pleased to
have on the record, and part of a longer interest in what an institution learns
about itself, and from whom. The full poster is available from the University's
research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ih1b2w.
