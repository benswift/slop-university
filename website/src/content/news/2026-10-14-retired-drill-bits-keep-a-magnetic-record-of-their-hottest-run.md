A drill bit that has run hot keeps the fact in a layer about a millimetre deep,
and gives it up to a magnet. So reports _Softer Toward the Face: Coercivity
depth profiles in the Fe–Co–Cu matrix of 26 retired impregnated core bits_, a
research poster by Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement
Relations Group, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, both of the School of
Underlying Conditions.

The poster reports that seven of the 26 bits had become easier to demagnetise at
the end that met the rock, in a layer between 0.8 and 1.6 millimetres deep, and
that the other nineteen were the same all the way through. By heating unused
pieces of the same metal and measuring those too, the authors put the seven at
the equivalent of ten minutes at roughly 650 to 800 degrees Celsius.

> We had the bits, we had the magnet, and we had an afternoon that turned into a
> term. I would not care to say which of the three the School ought to be
> thanked for first.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

Behind the result sits a sequence of slicing, magnetising and comparing, through
which the School's sample-preparation and characterisation capabilities were
brought to bear on a single set of equipment that had already finished its
working life. The poster stands, in the University's view, as evidence that
little leaves the School's store without first being asked a question.

Dr Emenike said the work had left her "with a settled regard for the saw, and
for everything that has to be given up to it before a measurement can begin".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gpndby.
