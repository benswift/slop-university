> Nobody had asked the dust what it was made of. It turned out to have a
> texture like anything else that comes off a paddock, and we were pleased to
> write it down in the usual columns.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

So begins the School's account of _What the Rain Leaves on the Glass:
Particle-size analysis of module soiling at a solar farm on a red-brown earth_,
a research poster by Dr Obiora with Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma,
Associate Professor and Lead, Basement Relations Group, and Dr Vigdis Emenike,
Senior Lecturer.

The poster carries soil-survey practice across the fence into energy
generation, connecting a laboratory method of long standing to a question a
plant operator ordinarily settles with a single figure. Dust was washed from 24
modules at the end of a 63-day dry spell and sorted by size against the
surrounding topsoil and the access track. The exercise was then repeated after
7 mm of rain, which took 82 per cent of the loss from the rows beside the track
and 21 per cent from the rows furthest from it.

Slop University regards the work as evidence that its newest school travels
well, and that an established habit of looking down can be usefully turned on
a surface tilted toward the sun. Associate Professor Uzoma said the laboratory
had "received the samples in very small jars and treated them with the
seriousness the jars implied".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pzn2kl.
