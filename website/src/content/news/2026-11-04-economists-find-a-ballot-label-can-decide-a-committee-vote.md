Thirty small committees sat down in a University laboratory to settle how much
water a shared aquifer could give up. Each was handed three options and a fixed
running order. Not every committee was handed the same names for those options,
and that, a new research poster finds, was enough to change what they decided.

[_Holding Out for the Low Cap_](/outputs/slop-poster-agenda-order-and-hpmk0x/),
by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), with
Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist and Dr Ingrid Vasseur of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), shows members
ready to think several votes ahead while the options carried letters. Once the
options were named as pumping caps, few would cast a vote against the lowest of
them, even where that vote was the only way to keep the highest one from
passing.

> Every committee I have sat on believed it was deciding the question in front
> of it. This work invites the University to wonder, gently, how much of that
> deciding the running order had already done, and we are content to leave the
> question with the next committee that meets.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority Studies

The poster connects a long scholarly conversation about collective choice with
the everyday stewardship of shared resources, translating the formal grammar of
agendas into a setting any catchment board or standing committee would
recognise. It marks a further step in the steady traffic of ideas between the
two Schools, which the University counts among its quieter strengths.

Dr Vasseur said she expected the findings to be "read with interest in any room
where a motion has been moved and then amended".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hpmk0x.
