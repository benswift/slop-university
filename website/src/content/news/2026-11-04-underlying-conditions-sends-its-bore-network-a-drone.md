Not every bore needs a visit every month, and the one that most needs it may
be the one nobody can quite account for. That is the conclusion of
[_Visit the Bore Its Neighbours Cannot Predict_](/outputs/slop-paper-routing-a-data-mule-pjev3z/),
a new paper by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer
in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), with
[Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/) and
[Dr Osei Vandermeer](/people/osei-vandermeer/) of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

> We set out to plan a flight and ended up with a fairly candid account of
> our own network. It turns out the School has been keeping a bore for
> decades on instinct, and the instinct was sound.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings the School's groundwater fieldwork together with operations
research and the logistics of the aerial round, aligning a monitoring
programme's long memory with a monthly flight budget. Rather than asking how
many bores a drone can read, it asks which readings the rest of the network
cannot supply on its own, and builds each month's campaign around those. Across
eighteen replayed campaigns on a network of 212 bores, the approach brought the
error in the network's monthly picture down to 3.1 centimetres within a budget
of 480 flight-minutes, set against 6.0 for a round that simply reads as many
bores as it can.

The collaboration places two Schools' long-standing interests in the same
airspace. Dr Vandermeer described it as "the kind of routing problem where the
map keeps asking questions back", and said the team would be glad to hear from
colleagues whose instruments have outlived their paperwork.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pjev3z.
