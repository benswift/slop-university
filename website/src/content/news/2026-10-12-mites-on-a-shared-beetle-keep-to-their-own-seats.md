Where does a mite hold on when the beetle is crowded? The question is put, and
for one paddock answered, in _Where the Passengers Sit: Attachment-site
partitioning among phoretic mites on dung beetles in a single grazed paddock_, a
research poster by Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor, Futures in
Committee, with Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in the School of
Underlying Conditions, and Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow.

> I convene a seminar on how an open question becomes an agenda item. It has
> been instructive to spend a season with animals that settle the same matter
> without minutes, and I have brought nothing back that the committee could use.
>
> --- Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor, Futures in Committee

The poster connects a fortnightly trapping program to a body-region scoring
framework, bringing each of 412 beetles to the bench in a bag of its own and
recording every passenger in place before it was removed. Of the 268 beetles
that carried mites, 171 carried more than one kind, and on those the five kinds
sat apart to a degree that reshuffling the same loads never reproduced. On the
97 beetles where a kind rode alone, it spread over most of the host.

The Office of Research Outputs has received the poster as a welcome widening of
the School's field of view, and notes that the paddock was approached with the
same seriousness the School brings to a standing committee. Dr Obiora said he
had "dug a great many holes in paddocks of this sort and not once turned a
beetle over", and that he expected to go on doing the first.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gtb4m0.
