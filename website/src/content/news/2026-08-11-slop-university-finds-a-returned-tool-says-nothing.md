An apprentice who reliably returns a borrowed tool on time is, several
workshop supervisors report, someone they've come to expect is coming along
well --- a read they say they lean on ahead of, and sometimes instead of,
the formal competency sign-off that's supposed to settle the question. A new
Slop University paper from the School of Continuous Improvement puts that
expectation to the test, and finds the tool crib's return log is telling
supervisors less than they think.

Researchers compared 63,482 tool-checkout records against blinded formal
competency scores for 189 apprentices across four workshop sites and three
intake cohorts, building a return-timeliness proxy for each apprentice. The
proxy barely tracked the formal outcome it was standing in for, and an
automated return reminder rolled out at two of the four sites moved it no
more than ordinary drift moved the sites without one.

> A crib log was built to keep track of tools, not people, and it turns out
> to be very good at that.
>
> --- Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

The School frames the result as one more entry in its long-running interest
in what a convenient signal quietly comes to stand in for. "A workshop
already has a formal process for judging competency," said Associate
Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab. "This paper is
about why supervisors keep reaching for a faster one anyway."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.eth1lp.
