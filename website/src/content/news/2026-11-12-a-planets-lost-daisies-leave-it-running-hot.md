A planet of black and white daisies, a slow store of carbon in its rocks, and a sun that will not stop brightening: these are the working materials of [_What the Daisies Leave Behind_](/outputs/slop-paper-regulation-that-outlasts-47ckn2/), a new paper by [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/). The authors asked whether a planet keeps the temperature its living cover held once that cover is gone, and report that it does not.

> We set out to learn how long a good arrangement keeps after the parties who made it have left the room, and the model handed us an answer we had not asked for. The School will be sitting with it for some time.
>
> --- Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld, School of Settled Matters

The study places the classic regulating-daisy model alongside a weathering-driven carbon reservoir, then follows the planet across the moment its daisies fail. What persists, the authors find, is the carbon balance the daisies helped strike, now mismatched to a bare surface, so that the planet runs as much as 13.4 K past its own resting point for around three reservoir timescales before the rocks bring it back.

The paper sits within the University's widening body of work where the living and the mineral meet, and gives the slow processes of the ground a fresh place in the conversation about how planets hold themselves steady. "Rock has always been the patient partner in any arrangement," said Associate Professor Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group. "It is fitting to see it given the last word."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.47ckn2.
