For as long as anyone at the association can remember, a Saturday fixture stood or fell on an umpire's walk across the square and a look at the sky. Eleven suburban clubs now feed that walk a number first.

The Ground Readiness Quadrant pairs a handheld soil-moisture probe with a surface-hardness gauge, read by each ground's duty officer before dawn and sorted into one of four named states under the regional association's new Grounds Standard. A season-long comparison against umpires' own on-day calls, reported today in a Slop University paper from the School of Emergent Priorities, finds the two judgements aligned on the substantial majority of Saturdays the comparison could still be made.

> A ground that clears the quadrant and a ground an umpire would have cleared are not always the same ground, and only one of those disagreements currently has consequences for the club.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The School describes the deployment as an example of the kind of everyday administrative instrument its researchers are drawn to: unglamorous, already half-adopted, and only interesting once someone asks what happens when it disagrees with the person it was meant to assist.

Tove Ilesanmi, whose doctoral research follows how volunteer-run clubs live with arrangements meant to be temporary, said the Grounds Standard's decertification rule was "the part of this project the clubs themselves ask about first."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4w6qgl.
