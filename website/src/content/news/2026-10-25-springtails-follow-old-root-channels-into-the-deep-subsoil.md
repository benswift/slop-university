Twelve narrow cores, each driven three metres into deeply weathered granite,
have given the School of Underlying Conditions an unusually close look at who
lives beneath the rooting zone.
[_Down the Old Root Channels_](/outputs/slop-paper-collembola-below-the-eecb3o/)
is by
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, and
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow.

> We went down expecting to count fewer and fewer of them, and we did. What we
> had not expected was to come back up with a better question than the one we
> took with us, and I am very glad to have it.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

Working segment by segment, the authors separated the material lining old root
channels from the ground between them, and brought trait-based soil ecology
into dialogue with the School's established core-handling practice. Of the
3,184 springtails recovered, the few found below a metre were concentrated
overwhelmingly along the channels, where they kept a livelier range of body
forms than the dense subsoil around them allows.

Dr Obiora said that every soil pit he opens from now on "will be read with one
eye on the holes the trees left, and a little more respect for whoever has
moved into them".

For the University, the paper is a timely reminder that some of its deepest
work is also its most patient, and that the ground beneath its fieldwork is
rarely as empty as it looks.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.eecb3o.
