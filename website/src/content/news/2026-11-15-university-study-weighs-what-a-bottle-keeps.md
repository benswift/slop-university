Two hundred and sixteen containers, each turned upside down and left until nothing more would leave it, are the evidence base for [_Where the Last Spoonful Stops_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-much-of-exo8f6/), a new research poster from the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/). Its authors, [Mr Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/), [Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), set out to establish how much of a thick fluid a container quietly holds back, and where inside it that remainder chooses to settle.

> I spent years deciding what a household could leave out for a visitor to see. It has been a quiet pleasure to give the same attention to what a household cannot get out of the jar, and I am grateful to everyone who lent the project their patience while the drains finished.
>
> --- Mr Eivind Onwuka, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The poster brings laboratory rheology, purpose-built container geometries and careful weighing into a single framework for understanding residual volume, positioning the container's shoulder as a design lever with a clear limit. Past a certain steepness, the authors report, what remains clings to the walls, and no amount of reshaping reaches it.

Dr Obiora described the drains as "a lesson in waiting, which is most of what a field season is", and said the School of Underlying Conditions had been glad to lend a rheometer and a little of its experience with resting gels.

For the University, the study is a measured addition to the long and honourable tradition of taking ordinary household problems as seriously as the instruments allow.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.exo8f6.
