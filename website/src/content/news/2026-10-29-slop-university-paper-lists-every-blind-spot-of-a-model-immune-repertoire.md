> The School of Emergent Priorities spends much of its time asking what an
> organisation is prepared to see. A twelve-letter alphabet of noughts and ones
> turned out to have a good deal to say on the subject, and we have tried to let
> it finish its sentences.
>
> --- Ms Ylva Ogunbanjo, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

With those words, [Ms Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/) introduces
[_Every Hole in a Twelve-Bit Repertoire_](/outputs/slop-paper-exhaustive-enumeration-of-fhaqhu/), a new paper
written with [Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/), Postdoctoral Fellow and
Deputy Convenor of the Living Dashboard. It takes up one of immunology's oldest
balancing acts, the thymus's task of removing receptors that react to the body
itself while keeping enough of the repertoire to recognise everything else, and
it does so at a scale small enough to be read end to end.

Rather than estimate, the team adopted a whole-of-universe approach, mapping
every pairing of 4,096 receptors and 4,096 peptides across the full spectrum of
match settings, self-set scales and presentation scenarios, and surfacing
precisely where recognition capability is retained and where it falls away. The
gaps, the paper finds, gather close to self, and the arrangement that protects
what the thymus never sees is the same one that leaves those gaps open.

The paper adds a rare note of completeness to the University's research
portfolio, and extends the School's interest in anticipation into territory it
had not previously charted. Dr Hanke said the work "gives the next, longer
string something solid to stand on", and that the authors expected to return to
it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fhaqhu.
