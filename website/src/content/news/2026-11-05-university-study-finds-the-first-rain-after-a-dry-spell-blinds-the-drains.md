The University's attention to the infrastructure of the ordinary street now
reaches the gutter. A paper by doctoral candidate Sunniva Balogun of the School
of Emergent Priorities, written with Associate Professor Casimir Beng of the
School of Continuous Improvement, reports that the leaves which block a
suburb's drains in autumn arrive with the first rain after a few dry days, and
that a wet fortnight can leave the grates cleaner than it found them.

> I came to this from a catalogue, where the work is to keep a thing for what
> it is. A drain grate turns out to reward the same patience. You go back on
> Monday, and you go back on Thursday, and eventually the street tells you.
>
> --- Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The study brings photographic monitoring, on-site rainfall instrumentation and
time-lapse observation of gutter flow into a single seasonal account of
inlet performance, aligning street-tree canopy, sweeping cadence and the
arrangement of wet and dry days within one modelling framework. Its emphasis
falls on timing: maintenance that anticipates the weather, the paper suggests,
achieves what maintenance that follows it cannot.

The paper adds a careful, street-scale study to the University's widening
portfolio of work on the systems a city relies on without looking at them.

"The School of Continuous Improvement has always been fond of a schedule," said
Associate Professor Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab. "It has been good
for us to be reminded that the weather keeps one too, and that it does not
consult ours."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bgl29x.
