A coal stockpile does not dry out all at once. After rain, its coarse ends shed
their water within a day, while the fine coal at its centre is still draining on
the third day. That is the central finding of
[_Still Draining on Day Three_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-rain-drains-j5td1g/), a
new research poster by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior
Lecturer, and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research
Fellow, both of the School of Underlying Conditions.

> A school set up to study what lies beneath the ground has learned a good deal
> from ground that someone stacked on purpose. We are still working out what
> that says about us, and we are glad to be working it out in the open.
>
> --- [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, School of
> Underlying Conditions

The project brings the School's groundwater fieldwork to bear on the materials
pathway behind cokemaking. It aligns standpipe monitoring, drainage timing and
charge-quality records into a single account of how moisture moves through a
working pile, and translates that account into a reclaim posture fitted to the
pile's own structure. Across 41 matched reclaims, the poster finds that most
charges arriving outside the oven's moisture window had been drawn through the
core too soon after rain.

The work extends a line of School inquiry into the industrial settings where
earth science meets everyday operations, and it shows the School's field
methods travelling comfortably beyond their usual terrain.

"Every season brings another pile and another sky," said Dr Obiora. "We expect
the questions to keep arriving, and we intend to be standing beside the pile
when they do."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.j5td1g.
