Somewhere in the middle of last winter, a ridge of broken sea ice that had spent seven weeks freezing solid was pushed again, and a drill that had met hard ice at a metre's depth a fortnight earlier fell straight through. _Where the Drill Fell Through_, a new paper from the School of Underlying Conditions, is the record of that winter and of the ridge that changed its mind.

The paper is by Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer.

> A winter on the ice is mostly waiting, and we were grateful to spend it in such patient company. The ridge will be gone by the summer. We hope the paper keeps a little of it.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

Across seven field campaigns and 103 days, the team returned to the same line across the ridge, drilling and logging each hole by hand and by feel, and dipping the water in every one. The resulting picture brings together ice growth, snow cover and the operator's own sense of the auger into a single account of how a ridge consolidates, and of how quickly that consolidation can be set back.

The work extends the School's long attention to what a drill recovers and what it leaves behind, now carried onto a surface that refreezes between visits. It reflects the University's steady commitment to research conducted slowly, in the open, and in the cold.

"There is a particular quiet when the water rises in a fresh hole," said Dr Emenike. "We have tried to let the paper keep that, too."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.xeqbmf.
