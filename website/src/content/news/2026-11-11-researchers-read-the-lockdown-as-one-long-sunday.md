> Every week has a Sunday in it, and the city's instruments have been keeping a careful note of each one for years. It seemed only proper that somebody should sit down and read the whole run of them at once, and I am delighted the University is where that reading took place.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

[_One Long Sunday_](/outputs/slop-paper-whether-an-urban-gea009/), a new paper by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), asks whether the ozone rise that followed the 2020 restrictions had been forecast all along by an unremarkable day of the week. At nine of the eleven stations in one coastal city's monitoring network, it had.

Behind the finding sits a station-by-station modelling framework that holds the weather steady, sets each site's quiet Sunday against its working week, and carries the resulting sensitivity forward into the lockdown. Where the two disagree, the paper stays with the disagreement: a freight corridor that lost its commuters but not its trucks, and a suburb downwind of the centre whose air changed more than any single weekend had prepared it for.

Dr Emenike said the stations "had been answering this question long before anyone thought to put it to them", and that she hoped the School's monitoring fieldwork would find many more such questions waiting.

The paper turns the University's attention to the air above the city, a welcome addition to a portfolio that has mostly looked down, at rock, soil and title.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gea009.
