Close one door on a molecule, and it will usually find another. That
observation, familiar to anyone who has prepared a candidate compound and then
watched what the body makes of it, is now the opening line of a portrait
campaign from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/),
prepared by [Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/), Doctoral Candidate in the
School.

The poster sets a bench of paired flasks against an evening laboratory, one
outlet of each pair sealed and the vapour stepping sideways out of its
neighbour. It invites the passer-by to the School's study _Blocked but Not
Slowed_, and lists further work from the University's record on tannin chemistry and on
an archived genotoxicity screen of coded compounds.

> I came to the bench as an outsider, and what I kept noticing was how much
> care goes into the version of a compound that is meant to fail in one
> particular way. I wanted the poster to give that care a little room.
>
> --- Hedvig Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The campaign translates the School's synthesis-and-evaluation practice into the
signage register, positioning paired preparation as an anticipatory capability:
a way of surfacing a compound's alternatives before they surface on their own.
Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate and a contributor to the featured study,
welcomed the poster as "a fair picture of a long afternoon at the bench."

The University counts the campaign among its quieter efforts to let a
laboratory's patience be seen from the corridor, and it joins the portrait
rotation this teaching period.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dzgv12.
