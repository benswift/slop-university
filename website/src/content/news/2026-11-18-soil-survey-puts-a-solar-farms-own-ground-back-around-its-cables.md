Every kilometre of cable trench on a solar farm leaves a heap of soil beside it, and the customary destination for that heap is somewhere else. A new research poster from the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) asks how much of it could simply go back where it came from.

In [_Back Into the Trench It Came From_](/outputs/slop-poster-reusing-excavated-native-nz6cwk/), [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, works with [Dr Marit Osayande](/people/marit-osayande/), Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard, and [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, both of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), to bring the soil survey's pre-construction mapping into the cable-rating conversation. The team aligned each trench section's mapped soil unit with two summers of in-ground thermal monitoring, building a section-by-section picture of where a farm's own spoil can carry its own load. Seven of the nine sections backfilled with native soil held within the design value through both summers.

> Most of what we learn on a construction site we learn from the heap we were going to throw away. I am glad this one stayed long enough to teach us something.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

The poster takes its place in a growing line of work in which the School's survey practice finds a second career below the energy transition's infrastructure. "Two of the sections behaved differently from what the map led us to expect, and those are the two I will be returning to," said Dr Obiora. "A soil keeps its own counsel until the second summer."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nz6cwk.
