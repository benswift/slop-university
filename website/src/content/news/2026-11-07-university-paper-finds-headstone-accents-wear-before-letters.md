The University's interest in the ground its researchers stand on has always
extended to what stands upon it, and a new paper from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) takes that
interest into a country cemetery.
[_The Stone Keeps the Letters_](/outputs/slop-paper-which-names-a-gig9yj/), by
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, follows the small marks above and below the letters
of old inscriptions, and finds that on most of the stones where they were cut,
they have gone.

> A surveyor learns early that the line on the map and the ground beneath it
> part company over time. We are glad to have found the same parting so close
> to home, and glad of the questions it leaves the School to sit with.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper aligns the School's field and laboratory practice with a question
more often left to family historians, bringing structured-light scanning,
petrography and a simple recession model to bear on 2,874 names across three
kinds of stone. It locates the loss not in the weather but in the original
work of the letter-cutter, and in the choice of stone each family made.

Associate Professor Uzoma said the study "sits comfortably with everything
the Basement Relations Group has learned about reading a record for what it no
longer holds."

The University regards the paper as a fine instance of its schools turning
familiar instruments towards unfamiliar ground.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gig9yj.
