Most rural shires own a gravel pit, and most of them sell from it. A new research poster from the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) asks what becomes of the money. [_Do Rural Councils Spend Their Gravel?_](/outputs/slop-poster-do-rural-councils-k4xiiv/) is the work of [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) and [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) of that School, with [Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/) of the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

The team set two decades of shire financial statements against the weighbridge records of 96 council pits, and used the state road program's demand for gravel to separate the money a council chose to earn from the money that arrived on its own. Of each extra dollar from the pit, the poster finds, 54 cents went to day-to-day spending. Shires that carry their pit in the asset register, with its remaining tonnes written down as they are sold, kept 66 cents of the dollar for capital works, reserves and debt; shires without such a schedule kept 25.

The poster brings the University's long acquaintance with the ground into contact with the questions of public finance, and the School counts it among the clearer demonstrations yet of what the earth sciences can lend a budget.

> A pit is the plainest thing a shire owns, and the School has always found that the plainest ground repays the closest survey. We would be pleased to see the poster find its way onto a works committee's agenda.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Associate Professor Lindqvist said the collaboration had given him "a new respect for anyone who has to reconcile a tonne with a dollar at the end of a financial year."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.k4xiiv.
