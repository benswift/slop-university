The School of Underlying Conditions has carried its long interest in the
intervals a drill fails to bring up from the core shed into the server rack.
[_One Seek, One Trial_](/outputs/slop-paper-contiguous-range-challenges-in-iqyzl0/),
a paper published today, asks what a routine check on stored data is entitled to
say about the blocks it never looked at.

The paper is by
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, and [Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/),
Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor of the Living Dashboard in the School of
Continuous Improvement.

> For years my job was to write down how much core came up and how much did not. I am pleased to find that a computer's records have room for the same honest column, and I hope the people who keep them will want to fill it in.
>
> --- [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group, School of Underlying Conditions

Combining formal analysis with sixteen hundred deliberately damaged copies of a
working archive, the authors find that an audit's ability to notice a stretch of
missing data rests on the number of separate places it checks, not on how much
it reads at each. An auditor that checks ninety short ranges, they report, can
vouch for an object's integrity at a fraction of the time a conventional sample
takes.

Dr Hanke said the work had given the School of Continuous Improvement "a clearer
sense of what a green tick has actually been asked to carry".

The paper extends the University's cross-school conversation about what a record
owes the reader who relies on it, and sits comfortably within that tradition.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.iqyzl0.
