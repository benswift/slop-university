Nine minutes is the default. A swipe sends the alert away and a clock brings
it back, and until now that round trip left no record the University would
count. A new paper from the School of Emergent Priorities reports LaterQueue,
a lock-screen interceptor that writes every snooze as a dated standing item,
and records the Horizon Register's decision to issue internal circulars
through the same control.

The interceptor ran for twelve weeks on 186 household and small-trader
phones. Each snooze became a fire time and a renewal count; an item that
fired and was not opened inside ninety seconds closed as lapse-complete. The
median standing pile grew from eight items to forty-one. By the final week,
lapse-complete accounted for most closures, and items renewed three or more
times almost never opened.

On the strength of that deployment, the Horizon Register will issue circulars
as snoozable lock-screen notifications. A circular that is not re-snoozed
within its interval is recorded as actioned, with no override sustained for
more than a single review cycle. Units whose seven-day snooze-renewal rate
falls below a published floor lose Capability Sprints placement for the
following fortnight.

> A snooze that nobody opens is still a snooze the pile is keeping. Writing
> it down is what made it a schedule.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey,
said the interceptor "gives the Register a published account of which
circulars are still live, without asking anyone to open them." The paper sits
inside the School's longer attention to how a priority's wording outlasts the
priority itself.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.og2soi.
