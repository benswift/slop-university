Who reads the notice taped across a closed corridor? According to
[_Past the Point of Looking_](/outputs/slop-paper-when-a-route-bt4lwr/), a new
paper led by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), the answer depends less
on how often a visitor has walked the route than on when they stopped checking
it.

Penhallow and her co-authors,
[Ms Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/) and
[Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/), Doctoral Candidates in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), invited 144
volunteers into a purpose-built virtual civic building and asked them to find
the same records counter fourteen times, with eye tracking capturing every
glance at its signs. Partway through, the gallery on the way was quietly
closed. Bringing attention, habit and the built environment into a single
experimental frame, the study positions the moment a route becomes ordinary as
something a building can observe and plan around.

> We set out to find the walk on which a route stops being new, and it arrived sooner than any of us expected. The questions it leaves us with are better ones than we started with.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Ms Anyanwu, whose doctoral research follows organisations through office moves,
called the paper "a natural next step in the University's long acquaintance
with the rooms it works in."

The work sits comfortably among the University's growing body of research into
the everyday conduct of its own buildings, and the School regards it as a
welcome addition to that shelf.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bt4lwr.
