Every map has to decide where one thing stops and the next begins, and
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) has spent a career making that
decision in the field. His newest research poster,
[_A Band, Not a Line_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-a-mosaic-wket7b/), made with
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), carries the
question from the soil pit to the microscope slide.

The work turns to a group of rare skin conditions in which a patch of skin
carries a genetic change the rest of the body does not. Across 31 archived
specimens, the team sampled thin columns of tissue at regular steps across each
lesion's visible edge and sequenced every one. The clone, they find, does not
end where the eye says it does. It thins out over a band around a millimetre
wide, high and low columns interleaved, and where its margin is placed depends
far more on the threshold chosen than on how closely the samples are spaced.

> The School came to this work expecting to bring its own habits to unfamiliar
> ground. It has come away with a clearer sense of what those habits were.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster brings the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/)'s long
attention to transects, sampling intervals and gradational boundaries into
conversation with molecular genetics, connecting field survey practice and
sequencing workflows within a single account of how edges are recorded. It is
the kind of crossing the University's structure is designed to make possible.

"We are glad to have been asked a question this far from the drill floor," said
Associate Professor Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group. "It will be
interesting to hear what the people who know this tissue best make of it."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wket7b.
