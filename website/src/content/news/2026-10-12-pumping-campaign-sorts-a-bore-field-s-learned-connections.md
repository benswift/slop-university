> People ask whether we minded pumping bores a computer had already drawn lines
> between. We did not mind. A line is a fine thing to be handed, and a pump is a
> fine thing to bring to it.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

So begins the School of Underlying Conditions' account of _A Pumping Test Is a
Do-Operator: Telling connection from shared weather in a network learned over
sixteen bores_, a research poster by Dr Emenike and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior
Research Fellow.

The poster brings statistical learning, field hydrology and a long-standing
problem in the theory of computation into a single working arrangement. A model
built from three years of logged water levels proposed 27 links between the
bores of one riverside field. The authors then pumped the bores in turn and
watched which neighbours answered. Seventeen links held; ten turned out to
join bores that had only been listening to the same river. A rule for choosing
the next bore finished the campaign in seven tests, where the field crew's
customary circuit would have taken thirteen.

The University counts it a mark of a settled research culture when a school
checks a result by going outside and running a hose. Dr Obiora said the two
authors had "kept to the order the arithmetic gave us, including on the morning
it sent us to the far corner first".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.xd29pb.
