For six late summers, two researchers from the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) went back to the same hollow in a coastal dune field, plotted every stone tool the wind had uncovered since their last visit, and left each one exactly where they found it.

[_Lowered, Not Moved_](/outputs/slop-paper-lowered-not-moved-7yddrk/) is by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow. As the floor of the blowout sank through two buried surfaces of past occupation, the pair recorded 1,186 artefacts at the moment each came into the light, and then watched what the wind did with them next.

> We kept expecting the ground to surprise us, and mostly it told us the same thing again a little further down. I have come to think that is its own kind of generosity.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The study brings together landform monitoring, groundwater observation and the patient refitting of broken stone into a single account of how a surface is assembled over time. It also puts a long-running set of monitoring pipes, inherited from earlier work in the dune, to a use their installers could not have foreseen.

The paper extends the School's continuing attention to what the ground holds on to and what it lets go. Dr Obiora described the fieldwork as "a slow conversation with a very consistent correspondent", and said the School was pleased to have kept up its end.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7yddrk.
