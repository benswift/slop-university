Where does a laboratory's software go wrong? For one unnamed laboratory that
studies how bilingual speakers move between their languages, a new research
poster answers: wherever the software does the same. _Where the Code Switches
Language: Defects at programming-language hand-offs in fourteen cued-naming
experiment repositories_ is by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, Dr Brynjar
Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow in the
School of Emergent Priorities.

> The School was founded to study what lies underneath things. A good deal of
> what lies underneath an experiment, it turns out, is a file that two programs
> each believe the other one understands.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The team's reading connects nine years of version history to the 412 occasions
on which someone at the laboratory repaired a fault, and follows each repair
back to the change that made it necessary. Files in which one programming
language exchanges material with another make up 19 per cent of the code and
hold 36 per cent of the faults, and in thirteen of the fourteen codebases they
were the more fault-prone kind. The poster makes no claim about the
laboratory's published results, none of which were re-examined.

It is the School's first excursion into software, and the University counts it
in the School's favour that it went in at the joins. Dr Obiora said the
fourteenth codebase "has been a great comfort to everyone, and in particular to
whoever wrote it".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.33km6h.
