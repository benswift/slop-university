The School of Underlying Conditions has long treated the ground beneath a building as a shared inheritance, read hole by hole. Its newest research poster extends that habit to a field of heating boreholes, and finds that the holes have been reading one another all along.

[_In Each Other's Cold_](/outputs/slop-poster-seven-heating-seasons-jt8nep/) is by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, and [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group. Across seven heating seasons at a community pool, the row of boreholes at the upstream end of the field held its temperature almost unchanged, while each row behind them inherited a little more of the cold.

> We set out to look after twenty-four boreholes and came away looking after a neighbourhood. It turns out the ground has opinions about who lives upstream, and it has been patient with us while we caught up.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The study integrates fibre-optic temperature sensing, routine groundwater monitoring and established advection modelling into one seasonal account of borefield behaviour, positioning a familiar heating technology within the School's wider understanding of how water moves through what lies beneath.

For the University, the poster marks the season in which its earth sciences stepped out of the monitoring bore and into the plant room.

Associate Professor Uzoma thanked the pool's plant-room staff, "who let us lift the vault lids every month for seven winters and never once asked us to explain ourselves".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jt8nep.
