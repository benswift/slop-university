Loss, in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/),
is something to be written down rather than written off. A new research poster
from the School extends that habit from the core tray to the sample freezer,
following a long-held collection of cheek swabs through a fresh round of
genotyping and recording exactly where each answer failed to arrive.

> My thanks go to the people who gave these swabs years ago, for a purpose none
> of them could have pictured, and to the colleagues who kept the boxes in order
> long after anyone asked after them.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

Rather than setting incomplete results aside, the team positioned each missing
call within a structured interval log, aligning storage history, collection
practice and assay behaviour into a single account of what the archive still
holds. Across 3,412 swabs, the gaps proved anything but evenly spread, gathering
around one collection device and one hard-to-read stretch of the genome.

Senior Research Fellow Brynjar Obiora called the archive "a set of decisions
made a long time ago, some of which, it turns out, are still speaking." Thandiwe
Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Demo Quarter, who brought the School of Continuous Improvement's
interest in home sampling to the project, said she would be "carrying the
interval log back to every bench where samples outlast the question they were
taken for."

The poster joins a growing body of University scholarship on what collections
keep, and on the care it takes to know.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.y39van.
