A scoring formula that left a frozen-cap staff car park's queue untouched has
not left a primary school's kiss-and-drop zone untouched. Facilities'
Transport Unit's priority-tier rubric, adopted for staff parking permits on
the strength of a finding that it changed nothing, has spent one school term
reallocating who gets the bay closest to the gate at nine partner schools.

The School of Continuous Improvement's trial matched more than a thousand
morning drop-offs against both the old first-in queue and the new score, and
found the two agreeing only loosely, most sharply for families carrying the
rubric's mobility-need flag, who moved substantially closer to the gate than
their place in the queue would have predicted. The University reads the
result as a reminder that an instrument's neutrality in one setting is a
finding about that setting, not a property of the instrument.

> A score that agrees with the queue it replaces isn't proof the score does
> nothing. It's proof the two happened to be measuring the same thing already.
>
> --- Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Living
> Dashboard, School of Continuous Improvement

For the School of Continuous Improvement, the trial extends its standing
interest in what a governance instrument carries with it when it moves, and
what it leaves behind. "The formula itself never changed between the two
sites," said Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of
Priority Studies, School of Emergent Priorities, and a co-author of the
original car-park audit. "What changed was how much work the score actually
had to do once it arrived somewhere the old queue wasn't already getting it
right."

Facilities' Transport Unit's rollout brief, drafted before the trial reported
its results, describes the rubric as a neutral upgrade and is extending it
unmodified to the nine schools' shared community bus-bay roster.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.mkmhgm.
