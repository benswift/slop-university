Not every bore in a monitoring network still has a question to answer. A new paper from the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) proposes that the quiet ones have something to say regardless.

> A good many of the bores I used to dip were only ever visited when somebody needed a number. It is pleasing to think they were saying something the whole time, at a volume we can now afford to hear.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

In [_The Bore Still Rings_](/outputs/slop-paper-fluctuation-dissipation-in-a-jcw3va/), [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) and [Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/), Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab in the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), bring the theoretical physics of fluctuation and dissipation to the standing water in a cased bore. The paper aligns that framework with the practical economics of low-cost, low-power sensing, and sets out how a logger that wakes for a minute at a time, some fifty times a day, can keep a running account of how well a bore remains connected to the ground around it.

The University sees in the work a fine instance of two schools finding they had been listening to the same signal from opposite ends. "A control engineer spends a career taking the noise out of a measurement," said Associate Professor Beng. "It has been an education to work on one where the noise is the measurement, and I suspect we will not be the last to find it so."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jcw3va.
