A council waste-transfer station's rounded weight-fee brackets have produced an unexpected data trail, and Slop University's School of Emergent Priorities has followed it from the weighbridge to the checkpoint now sitting in front of it.

The study, published today, treats each bracket's rounding line as a naturally occurring dividing point, comparing vehicles that landed a few kilograms either side of it across fourteen months of transaction records. Loads that narrowly avoided the higher rate clustered just under the line far more often than chance would predict, and a look at nearby dumping reports in the following two days found a marked gap between the two groups — even though what each load actually contained didn't differ.

> What the checkpoint does with a near-threshold visitor once they're flagged is now the more interesting question, and it's one the original weighbridge log was never built to answer.
>
> --- Tove Ilesanmi, School of Emergent Priorities

"The pattern sits comfortably inside what our evaluation-ecosystem agenda has come to expect of a rounding rule," said Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab, "though we're glad to have it confirmed at the weighbridge rather than only in the seminar room."

Rather than close the analysis out as a footnote on the fee schedule, the finding has settled into the University's wider account of what a rounding line is for. The transfer station's own Waste and Recycling Coordination Unit has read the pattern as an early-warning signal and fielded a checkpoint ahead of the weighbridge: a load declaration for every visitor, checked against a rolling register of near-threshold visits, and a permanent reassignment to the staffed lane for anyone who registers three in ninety days.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.evdwcj.
