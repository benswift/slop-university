Three of the University's Schools have pooled their attention on a stretch of
open water. [_How Many Matchups Settle an Altimeter Wave-Height Calibration?_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-many-altimeter-buoy-ryk0z5/),
a new paper by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/),
[Mr Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/) of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) and
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), follows a
decade of satellite passes over nine moored wave buoys and asks a deceptively
simple question: at what point did the calibration stop changing?

The paper brings together long-run collocation records, errors-in-both-variables
modelling and a careful reading of an operator's maintenance log, positioning
the reference record itself as an active participant in the calibration rather
than a fixed backdrop. Its central finding is that the order in which
observations arrive shapes how quickly an estimate settles, with the winter
storms carrying much of the weight.

> None of this would have been possible without the people who went out to the
> buoys in all weathers and wrote down what they changed. A record is only as
> good as its log, and this one was kept with real care.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Mr Nnamani described the collaboration as "a conversation between Schools that
had not had much reason to talk about the sea, and now have several".

The paper takes its place in a cross-School portfolio the University regards as
increasingly central to how it brings its disciplines into contact with the
physical world.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ryk0z5.
