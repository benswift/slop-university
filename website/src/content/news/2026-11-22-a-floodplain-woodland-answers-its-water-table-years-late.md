How long does a tree take to notice that the water beneath it has gone? For one floodplain woodland, [_Behind the Water Table_](/outputs/slop-paper-repeat-airborne-lidar-a9vre0/), a new paper from the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) and the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), puts the answer at about four and a half years, and only once the water table has fallen past a depth of roughly 9.4 metres.

> A bore and a tree keep different hours. It has been a privilege to sit with both records long enough to hear them agree, and the School means to go on listening to each of them at its own pace.
>
> --- [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Integrating five airborne surveys of the woodland's canopy with a monitoring-bore network kept in service well beyond its original purpose, Dr Emenike and [Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/) position repeat remote sensing, groundwater stewardship and floodplain ecology within a single long-horizon evidence base. The study finds the canopy steady across the shallower half of the woodland and thinning by close to four percentage points for every further metre of depth beyond the threshold.

Dr Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor of the Living Dashboard, said the collaboration had given the School of Continuous Improvement "a fresh appreciation of an indicator that arrives when it is ready, and not a moment before".

The paper extends the University's patient regard for evidence that accumulates slowly, and for the colleagues across its schools who keep such records in good order.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.a9vre0.
