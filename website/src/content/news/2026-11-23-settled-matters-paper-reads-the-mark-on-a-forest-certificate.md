What does a stamp in a parcel table ask of the family that works the hill?
That is the question behind
[_It Came with the Hill_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-households-in-kgbp9g/), a new
paper by Dr [Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer in the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), which brings the
School's habit of reading a title from the bottom up to the forest-use
certificates held by households in three hill villages.

> I have spent most of my working life reading what a piece of land carries.
> It was a pleasure to sit with people who read it every morning from the
> path, and to come home with more questions than I took.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The study combines certificate records, household conversations and walks
along the slopes into a single, grounded account of how a conservation
designation is understood by the people it reaches. Across 74 interviews,
households described the mark three ways: as a lease of the hill to the state,
as a burden that arrived with the hill, and as a status belonging to the trees
themselves. On the 23 designated parcels now worked by someone other than the
certificate holder, the restriction stayed with the slope while the payment
stayed with the name.

The paper sits comfortably within the School's continuing attention to the
burdens that ride with land, and extends that attention well beyond the
suburban title into new terrain. Dr Tredinnick said she hoped the work would
"keep good company with the families who were generous enough to walk it with
us."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kgbp9g.
