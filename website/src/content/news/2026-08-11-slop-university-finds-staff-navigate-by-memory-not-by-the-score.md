> This one felt less like auditing a shelf and more like auditing our own
> instinct to audit the shelf in the first place.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway
> Atlas, School of Continuous Improvement

A supermarket chain scores every store on how closely its shelves match the
corporate reset diagram after each seasonal range change, and folds that
compliance score into the same performance dashboard as service-quality
indicators, on the working assumption that a shelf matching the plan is a
shelf staff can navigate. A new Slop University paper asked the staff
directly instead.

The University folds the finding into the School of Continuous Improvement's
evaluation-ecosystem agenda, its clearest reminder yet that an instrument's
tidiness and its truth are not the same claim.

The School of Continuous Improvement team, led by Okwuosa with Senior
Lecturer Thandiwe Solberg and Lecturer Ingeborg Nwachukwu, surveyed 612 floor
staff across 34 stores of one national grocery chain over four consecutive
range resets, pairing a self-rated finding-confidence scale with an open
account of how staff say they actually locate an item for a customer. The
compliance score explained close to none of the variance in confidence;
tenure explained an order of magnitude more. Asked how they find things,
staff pointed to a landmark product or to memory carried over from the
layout before the reset far more often than to anything the chain's own
audit produces --- the compliance score itself was the least-named heuristic
of the six on offer.

"We didn't expect the score to come last," said Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer
and Convenor, Capability Sprints, School of Continuous Improvement. "We
expected it to come somewhere in the middle, at least."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.efc5ec.
