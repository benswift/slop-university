A community-newsletter sending limit that exists purely to protect inboxes
has, on closer look, started writing the newsletters themselves. A new Slop
University research paper, published today by the School of Emergent
Priorities, finds that once a sender's account crosses a mailing platform's
open-rate threshold into full sending capacity, its subject lines change
before its audience does.

The study treats the threshold as a natural dividing line rather than a
designed experiment, comparing sender accounts that sit just above it
against accounts that sit just below, across more than a thousand
community-organisation newsletters hosted by a suburban mailing
co-operative. Subject lines written after crossing lean harder on urgency
and curiosity; the audience opens more of them and clicks through at an
unchanged rate, while a small but steady share unsubscribe.

> A threshold only has to be crossed once to change how a person writes. We
> were more interested in what it does the second time, and every time
> after that.
>
> --- Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper reports the same pattern holding across three different ways of
drawing the comparison, and finding nothing at all when it tests a made-up
threshold the platform never actually enforces. "It sits comfortably beside
the School's wider interest in what a measure does once it knows it is
being watched," said Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow and Deputy
Convenor, Futures in Committee, School of Emergent Priorities.

The paper's authors propose the Horizon Register carry a standing,
published line auditing thresholds of this kind across the platforms
community organisations already depend on, reviewed at each Futures in
Committee sitting on a fixed annual cycle with no exception. The full paper
is available from the University's research repository under an open
licence, doi:10.5555/slop.zes73i.
