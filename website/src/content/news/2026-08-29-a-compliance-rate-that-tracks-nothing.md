A junior sport association's minimum-game-time by-law has, since its
introduction, done exactly what a by-law is meant to do: every club now
complies. A new Slop University study asks what that compliance actually
buys the players it was written to protect, and the answer complicates the
association's own scorecard.

Released today, the study draws on two seasons of team-sheet audits across
24 clubs, matching each club's by-law compliance rate against the following
season's registration rolls. The two move independently of each other — a
club complying at 76 per cent keeps its players at roughly the same rate as
a club complying at 98 per cent.

> A compliance rate earns its keep by tracking something. This one tracks a
> team sheet.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

It is the kind of gap the School of Emergent Priorities exists to go
looking for, and one its researchers are in no hurry to smooth over. "The
study asks a question the association's own facility-allocation formula was
never built to answer, and we're glad it's finally sitting next to the
numbers," says co-author Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of
the Strategic Drift Survey.

The association's facility-allocation formula continues to weight by-law
compliance as its junior-development indicator; the study changes what is
known about that weighting, not the weighting itself. The full research
poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.f0qf5u.
