A blood donation appointment produces two records of what happened in the
chair, and only one of them tells the donor when they can come back. A new
Slop University paper puts those two records side by side across an entire
network, and finds they agree only moderately.

> A reminder clock doesn't know it's reading a field built for something
> else. It just keeps counting, exactly as configured, long after the thing
> it was counting stopped meaning what everyone assumed.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director, Trajectory Analytics Group, School
> of Emergent Priorities

The School of Emergent Priorities team linked the booking system's
"attended" flag, the sole trigger for the next-eligible-to-donate text,
against a separate phlebotomy outcome log across 46 centres and seven
months of appointments. A fifth of the visits logged as attended were
early-stopped or self-withdrawn draws that never reached full volume, and
every one still received the same standard-interval reminder as a
completed donation; incomplete-draw donors went on to return well past the
interval the reminder had assumed for them. A supplementary "volume
confirmed" toggle, already piloted at fourteen centres, left the
underlying mismatch statistically unchanged.

"What surprised us wasn't the mismatch itself," said Dr Anneke Tolan,
Senior Research Fellow. "It was how ordinary the toggle's non-effect turned
out to be --- an optional field, added in good faith, absorbed by exactly
the practice it was meant to interrupt."

The University reads the result as one more instance of a question its
measurement schools keep returning to: a field logged as evidence something
happened isn't always evidence of what it is later trusted to mean.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dwyux3.
