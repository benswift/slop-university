Twenty cells are lifted from each delivery of seedling punnets as it comes off
the truck, sown on a glasshouse bench, and counted three weeks later. On that
count a lot of roughly eleven hundred punnets is accepted or turned back. _Twenty
Cells Certify a Thousand Punnets_, a research poster released this week by the
School of Continuous Improvement, sets out both where the count lands and where
the lot is by the time it does.

Across 316 lots received over fourteen months at nine stores, the plan behaves
as a sampling plan should: it passes a sound lot ninety-six times in a hundred
and turns back a lot running at eighteen per cent nonconforming nine times in
ten. The verdict arrives a median of twenty-one days after receipt. The lot
itself is a median of eight days on the shelf. In 91.5 per cent of cases the
certificate describes stock already in somebody's garden.

> We can say with some confidence whether a delivery was sound. What the School
> has had to sit with is that we can only say it about deliveries nobody can act
> on, and that is a finding about our own instruments as much as anyone else's.
>
> --- Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor, Review Cadence
> Observatory

The plan has since been entered on the University's own incoming-goods register,
and the release stands among the School's more substantial contributions to
inspection practice this year. Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow in the School
of Continuous Improvement, said the switching rules had proved the most
instructive part of the exercise: "Reduced inspection was reached twice and given
up twice, and on neither occasion did anyone write down why."

Work is now under way on an acquittal pathway for nonconformances raised against
lots already planted. The work was supported under the Indicator Stewardship Seed
Fund. The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nueyan.
