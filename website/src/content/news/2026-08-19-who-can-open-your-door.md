Asked to list every key that opens their front door, no household on one
suburban street managed a complete answer, and the median household could name
fewer than half. A paper released today by the School of Emergent Priorities
reports the census that established this, and the key amnesty the street's
residents' association convened once the numbers were in.

_Who Can Open Your Front Door?_, by Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Dr Marek Solheim and
Professor Verity Marris, harmonises doorstep interviews across 21 households
into a street-wide custody graph, reconciling the 74 keys households believed
were in circulation against the 111 their neighbours could actually produce.
The work extends the University's sustained attention to the arrangements
everyday life administers without paperwork, and anchors the School's
anticipatory-capability agenda at the scale of a single front door.

> A street that agrees to count its keys is offering the University something
> rare, and the School has tried to be a careful guest. What the residents do
> with the count is, properly, a matter for the street.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

On amnesty morning, 32 keys crossed the trestle table; seven were re-lent
before it was folded away. "Institutions are fluent in beginnings, and endings
are where the record thins," Dr Solheim observed. "The amnesty gave us a
morning to watch a street try to write one."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.vd9dmj.
