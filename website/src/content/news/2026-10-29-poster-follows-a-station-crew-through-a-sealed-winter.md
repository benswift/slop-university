Once the last aircraft of the summer leaves a polar research station, the people
on it are the only people on it until spring. A new research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) treats that
long closure as a study design in its own right.

The poster is led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow, with [Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), Lecturer, and
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer. Eleven crew
members collected a swab of their own each week for forty weeks, and the team
followed how often each person's vaginal community state type changed before,
during and after the sealed winter. Switches were rarer across the closed months
than either side of them, and most of the winter's few changes kept time with
participants' own cycle diaries.

> The School has always been good at long waits. This is the first time we have
> waited out a whole station winter alongside the people living it, and I think
> we have come back a little better at noticing the week when something turns.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The work brings remote-site logistics, self-directed sampling and
community-level sequencing into one season-scale account of stability under
closure, positioning the station as a setting where the usual drivers of change
can be held apart. It is the kind of patient, whole-season attention the
University counts among its quieter strengths.

Dr Chikere described the protocol as "something we hope the next winter crew
finds already packed and waiting for them."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kshc4p.
