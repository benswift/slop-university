Leave a class of eight-year-olds waiting three seconds longer and the sums come
out right about as often as before. What changes is the account of how they
were done. That is the report of _What the Silence Is Used For_, a research
poster by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior
Lecturer, and Eivind Onwuka, Doctoral Candidate.

> We sat at the back of four rooms for two terms and tried to take up as little
> of the quiet as we could. It was not ours to use, and it was being used.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The team recorded the oral warm-up of 64 mathematics lessons at one primary
school, where a sum is put to the whole class, a pupil is named, and the
teacher asks how the answer was reached. Across 1,312 such exchanges, a pupil
named within a second mostly said they just knew. After longer waits the same
pupils, on the same sums, more often described doubling, adjusting or counting
on. Of 61 pupils heard both ways, 38 reported a slower method after the longer
wait. Answers were correct 88 per cent of the time after short waits and 87
after long ones.

The poster also records where its two raters disagreed, on answers of the form
"I just knew it, because seven and seven is fourteen", and treats that
disagreement as a finding of its own.

For the University, the poster is a small demonstration that patience is a
research capability and can be lent from one discipline to another. Dr Emenike
said the School had been "made welcome by people half its height, and has
tried to be a good guest."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6oytbm.
