The [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) has long
taken an interest in what persists once the reason for it has gone. Its newest
research poster carries that interest into the hippocampus.

[_After the Wheel Goes_](/outputs/slop-poster-what-happens-to-oqufpf/), by
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) and
[Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), follows a single labelled cohort
of new neurons in fifty mice given a running wheel, then takes the wheel away
when those cells are one, three or five weeks old. Removed early, the wheel
takes most of its benefit with it. Left in place to the fifth week, it can
leave without taking anything at all.

> We went in with a narrow question about timing and came out with a narrow
> answer, which is the best kind. Plenty remains open, and we would be glad of
> colleagues who want to keep it that way for a while.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

In the language of the poster's methods, the team aligned exercise exposure,
cohort labelling and masked stereological assessment into a single pre-registered
pipeline, then extended it into a measure of how the surviving cells respond to
something new. The University regards the poster as a fair account of the
patient, long-horizon inquiry its schools are asked to sustain, and of the
care it expects such inquiry to take with the animals in its keeping.

Mr Nnamani described the work as "a good reason to keep asking what an
intervention leaves behind, and for how long."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.oqufpf.
