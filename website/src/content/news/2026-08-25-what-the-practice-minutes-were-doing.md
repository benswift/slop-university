The minutes on the screenshot were counted as practice. The piano was in the
next room. A research poster from the School of Emergent Priorities, published
today, surveys 186 suburban piano households whose teachers collect a weekly
metronome-app screenshot, and finds that time at the instrument is only the
largest class of those minutes, not most of them.

Kitchen-bench dinner is the second-largest class. Mean weekly minutes still
separate the music-teachers' association's 2025 eisteddfod recommendation
list; the share of minutes actually spent at the piano does not. The
association tabled the breakdown and kept the minutes total as the ranking
input for the following year, citing comparability with prior lists.

The work sits in the School of Emergent Priorities' longer effort to read the
instruments households already keep, and to notice what those instruments
record once they are asked to stand in for something else.

> A ranking that cannot tell dinner from practice is still a ranking.
> Comparability with last year's list is a reason to keep a figure, not a
> reason to believe it.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer

Households classified each week's screenshot the Sunday after it went to the
teacher, against four activity classes locked before the term began. The
association's published 2025 list was treated as the ranking those minutes had
already produced. Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master
of Priority Studies, said the finding "lands where a priority acquires a
number well before anyone agrees what the number is of."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bk7ajl.
