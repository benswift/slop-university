Give a thing a shorter name and it tends to be said more often. A new research
poster from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) reports that the
same holds for software that writes molecules.

[_Eight Tokens for One Group_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-chemical-language-gz6viw/)
is by [Ms Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral Candidate, with
[Ms Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), Doctoral Candidate, and
[Mr Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/). Chemical
structures are routinely handed to a model as a line of text, and a model reads
that line in pieces; a common fluorine-bearing group can cost eight of them.
The team trained two otherwise identical models on the same 1.4 million
structures, giving one of them a short abbreviation for six such groups, and
then compared what each proposed when asked for something new.

The abbreviation did what it was meant to, trimming the strings by about an
eighth. It also moved the chemistry. Groups with an abbreviation turned up in
the model's suggestions more often than they appear in the material it learned
from, and groups left spelled out turned up less.

> We set out to make the strings shorter and found that we had also changed
> what the model was willing to suggest. The University has been patient with
> findings of that shape.
>
> --- Ms Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate

The poster reflects a growing confidence at the University in work that sits
between two fields and settles in neither. "A vocabulary looks like a
housekeeping decision right up until you look at what it produced," said Mr
Nwabueze, who led the evaluation.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gz6viw.
