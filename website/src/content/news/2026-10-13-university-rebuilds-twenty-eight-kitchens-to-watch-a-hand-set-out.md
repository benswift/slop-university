A kitchen on castors has been assembled, taken apart and assembled again in a
Slop University laboratory, once for each of the twenty-eight people who agreed
to make tea in it. _The Drawer That Isn't There: Homeward launches of the
reaching hand in a rearranged kitchen_, published today, is a paper by Dr Iben
Chikere, Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities, with Tove Ilesanmi,
Doctoral Candidate in the same School, and Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer in the
School of Continuous Improvement.

Each kitchen was built to match the participant's own and then altered, with
four of the eight things needed for a cup of tea put somewhere new. Participants
were shown the changes until they could point to every item without error. The
paper reports that one in five of their first reaches to a moved item
nevertheless set off toward where it lives at home, and that in the week
afterwards eleven of them reached, in their own kitchens, for a place that had
only existed in the laboratory.

> Twenty-eight people let us into their kitchens with a tape measure, and then
> came and stood in ours. We owe them a debt we are unlikely to be able to
> repay in tea, though it was offered, thirty times each.
>
> --- Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The study couples a reconfigurable domestic environment to a movement-capture
capability, bringing the opening instant of an everyday action into alignment
with a long-running scholarly conversation about how far thinking reaches into
the rooms it happens in. Tove Ilesanmi said the teaspoons had "asked more of the
team than any other item, and given more back".

The University holds that an institution is entitled to some quiet satisfaction
when its researchers go looking for the mind and return with the location of a
cutlery drawer.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.uadu4b.
