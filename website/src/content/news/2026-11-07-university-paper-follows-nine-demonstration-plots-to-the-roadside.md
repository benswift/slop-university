At one lowland agricultural extension station, every demonstration plot a
development programme ever laid out is still in the ground. That is the
starting point of
[_From Hollow Way to Lay-By_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-contemporary-archaeology-fm037c/),
a paper by [Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), Doctoral Candidate,
[Dr Marek Solheim](/people/marek-solheim/), Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the
Horizon Register, and [Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral
Candidate, all of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> A school that studies how priorities arrive has learned a great deal about
> itself from a place where none of them ever left. We went looking for nine
> beginnings and found that each one had been careful to leave the others
> standing.
>
> --- Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Bringing the methods of field archaeology to the recent past of rural
development, the team surveyed the station's grounds and opened a series of
small trenches across plot boundaries, marker posts and sign footings,
establishing the order in which successive programmes arrived and tracing how
each one positioned its demonstration for the visitors it hoped to reach. The
resulting sequence moves steadily from the middle of the station towards its
road frontage, and from a footpath worn by walking visitors to a gravel lay-by
built for stopping ones.

The paper sits squarely within the School's long-standing interest in what
institutions keep once they have moved on, and the University is pleased to see
that interest carried into the paddock. Dr Solheim said he hoped "colleagues in
extension will come and walk the ground with us, preferably from the gate".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fm037c.
