Egg masses in the University's insect rearing room now sit at least four
centimetres apart, on separate leaf sections, and the reason is a new paper
from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

[_Whose Capsule?_](/outputs/slop-paper-whose-capsule-hatchlings-27cco0/), by
[Dr Ingrid Vasseur](/people/ingrid-vasseur/), Lecturer and Convenor of Futures
in Committee, with doctoral candidates
[Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/) and
[Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), asks a question that begins with a
mother and ends with a neighbour. Stinkbugs of this family leave small
capsules of gut bacteria beneath their eggs for their young to feed from. The
team wanted to know whose capsules the young actually use.

> The School spends most of its days on how a commitment passes from one group
> to the next. It has been a privilege to watch that question play out at the
> scale of a leaf, and we have come away with a more generous sense of what
> inheritance can involve.
>
> --- Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor, Futures in Committee

The study brings careful arena design, a single-letter genetic marker and more
than a thousand hatchlings to bear on the everyday logistics of symbiont
provision, aligning bench practice with the broader conversation about how
partnerships are handed on. Its central finding is that a borrowed partner
serves a nymph as well as its own, and that one generation of borrowing is
enough for the arrangement to stick.

The University regards the work as a model of a question that starts at the
bench and finishes in the rearing handbook.

"There is a good deal more to watch on that leaf," said Mr Nnamani, "and the
School intends to keep watching it."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.27cco0.
