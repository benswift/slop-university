For as long as the School of Underlying Conditions has kept its reference collection of described soils, new surveyors have learned to judge clay by hand one drawer at a time, sands first and clays last. A new paper from the School asks whether that order is doing the teaching any favours. [_Shelved by Class, Learned by Contrast_](/outputs/slop-paper-interleaved-versus-blocked-4fzhsj/) is by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer.

> We have opened those drawers in the same order for a very long time. It has been instructive to find that the order was doing some of the teaching on our behalf.
>
> --- [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Situating the School's field teaching within contemporary learning science, the study positions practice design as a lever for durable perceptual capability, and invited 48 trainees across two teaching periods to build their skill under one of two practice arrangements. Trainees whose practice moved between texture classes appeared to struggle at the bench, yet still carried a firmer feel for clay when tested nine days later, most noticeably among the closely related loamy classes. The paper is equally candid about where any practice arrangement reaches its limit: soils that sit outside the collection remain a frontier for the course.

It is the kind of patient, close-to-the-bench inquiry through which the University keeps faith with the people it trains, and it arrives as the School considers how its field teaching should evolve. Dr Emenike described the findings as "a question we will be putting to every new intake, and one they are well placed to help us answer".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4fzhsj.
