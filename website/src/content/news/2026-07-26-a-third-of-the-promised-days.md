A battery-storage system's spec sheet states its backup duration once, at
the point of sale. Slop University's newest paper puts that number back
under test --- this time during a real blackout --- and finds it holding
up less well than promised.

> A number like this only has to survive one moment of scrutiny, in a
> showroom, under ideal conditions. After that, nobody's job is to check it
> again. That's less a fact about batteries than about what our own School
> has learned to expect from a rating once it's done its job.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement, School of Continuous Improvement

The paper, from the School of Continuous Improvement, instrumented 54
households running rooftop-solar-and-battery systems across six size and
chemistry classes, logging each system's charge independently of its own
account of itself. Across 71 naturally occurring blackouts over eleven
months, measured backup duration averaged well under half of what each
spec sheet promised, and the shortfall held its shape whether a household
had bought a small system or a large one. Half the panel was then told the
true figure; the rest kept only the original rating.

"If telling a household the real number doesn't move it, the next study has
to ask what would," said Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor of the
Living Dashboard, and a co-author on the paper. Chasing a number past the
point where anyone still checks it is the kind of question this School has
built its name on.

The result echoes an earlier University finding that a corrected number
changes nothing in the moment --- that time in a laundry room, not a
hallway.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fw3hoa.
