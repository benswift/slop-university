Anyone who has met a delivery robot in a narrow hallway will know the moment it seems unable to decide which way to go. [_Replan Fast, Commit Slowly_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-often-a-jw8dzm/), a new paper from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) and the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), traces that hesitation to the planner itself and offers the University's shared campus fleet a way past it.

> My School spends most of its days watching people decide things together. It was a pleasure, for once, to watch something decide on its own, and to help it decide a little less often.
>
> --- Dr [Anneke Tolan](/people/anneke-tolan/), Senior Research Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Futures in Committee, School of Emergent Priorities

Across 24,000 simulated passes and 412 passes in a campus service corridor, the team positions the robot's choice of passing side as a commitment in its own right, aligning how often a machine looks with how readily it changes course. A two-part rule, anchored in long-standing control practice, held reversals near 0.13 per pass at every replanning rate the team tested, and the robots spent markedly less time hesitating in front of walkers.

The work reflects the University's steady investment in robots that share its spaces gracefully. "The corridor volunteers gave the study its rhythm, and we hope they enjoyed the walk," said Dr [Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/), Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Living Dashboard.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jw8dzm.
