> A team that already holds the right answer still has something to learn about
> itself. Our players let us watch that learning happen, one slip at a time.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

That learning is the subject of
[_Known at the Table, Lost on the Sheet_](/outputs/slop-poster-whether-the-quiz-team-qfhygh/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) by
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), with
[Dr Ingrid Vasseur](/people/ingrid-vasseur/), Lecturer and Convenor of Futures
in Committee in the School of Emergent Priorities, and doctoral candidate
[Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/).

Across a full season of a suburban quiz league, the team brought a private-slip
protocol to every table, pairing each player's own answer with what the team
went on to submit, and setting both against a pre-season measure of how well
each player reads emotion in faces and voices. The result is a season-scale
evidence base for how knowledge travels, or fails to travel, from the person to
the group.

Teams submitted 81 per cent of what their members privately knew. Where a
single player held the correct answer alone, those who read emotion more
accurately saw it written down more often, most of all when the team's scribe
had a rival answer in mind. How emotionally intelligent players rated
themselves made no measurable difference.

The poster carries the School's long interest in what a single reading is asked
to stand for into the warmer setting of a weeknight function room, and the
University welcomes it as a sociable addition to its thinking on collective
judgement. "Every committee I convene has a scribe, and I suspect I will now
watch that chair with fresh eyes," said Dr Vasseur.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qfhygh.
