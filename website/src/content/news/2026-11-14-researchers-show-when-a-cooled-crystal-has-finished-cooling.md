Forty-one times, a small crystal was cooled by laser light in a vacuum chamber and then left alone, long after anyone would ordinarily have written down its temperature and switched off the pump. [_Declaring the End of a Cooldown_](/outputs/slop-paper-when-has-a-g9xa2c/), a new paper by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/) of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), reports what those extra hours showed.

> We now write down, beside every result, how we decided it was finished. It is a small habit, and I suspect the School will find more uses for it than the laboratory did.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The paper situates its contribution where laser cooling, thermal systems modelling and sequential decision-making meet, offering cooling laboratories a principled pathway from a trace that looks settled to a figure that can be carried forward with confidence. Its central finding is that a familiar rule of thumb tends to stop the clock while the sample is still turning, and its proposed replacement trades a modest amount of additional pumping for a result that states its own uncertainty.

Dr Ntuli described the collaboration as "a welcome chance to sit with a question that keeps its answer until the very end, and to learn the discipline of staying for it".

The University counts the paper among the clearest signs yet that the School of Settled Matters' long interest in when a question can be called closed travels well beyond the courtroom, and is pleased to see it arrive in the cooling laboratory.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.g9xa2c.
