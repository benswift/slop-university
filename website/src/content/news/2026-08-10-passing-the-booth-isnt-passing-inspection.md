A self-service photo booth at the post office, the pharmacy, or the station
kiosk gives exactly one verdict on a captured face --- accept or retake ---
before it will print anything, and that verdict has quietly become the
compliance check itself: a printed photograph counts as compliant because the
booth accepted it, not because anyone independent looked. A new Slop
University paper puts that verdict against a trained eye for the first time
at deployment scale.

> An instrument that only has to convince itself is always going to agree
> with itself. The interesting number was always going to be how often it
> agreed with somebody else.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead, Adaptive Metrics Lab, School of
> Continuous Improvement

Working with a single national photo-booth operator, the School of
Continuous Improvement team audited 2,400 captures across 58 booths --- 1,612
accepted, 788 rejected --- against masked judgements from three trained
proofing officers applying the same national photo standard by hand. The two
verdicts agreed only fairly (Cohen's kappa of 0.34, against 0.79 among the
officers themselves), and the disagreement ran heaviest in one direction: 56
percent of everything the booth rejected, an officer would have passed. Where
a session ran to several retakes before the booth cleared a frame, the
finished print was markedly more likely to be one an officer would have sent
back too.

"We keep finding the same shape in a different fixture," said Dr Dagny
Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation. "A device gets asked
to certify something, and the certifying turns out to be the only part
anyone ever checks."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ssfiey.
