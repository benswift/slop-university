A frozen tube of DNA, handed from one project to the next for fourteen years,
almost always still holds the tool its label names. What sits around that tool
is another matter.
[_Inherited Constructs_](/outputs/slop-paper-inherited-constructs-a-v2bulf/), a
new paper by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) with
[Ms Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/) and
[Mr Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/) of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), reads a
research facility's entire holding of light-sensitive protein constructs from
end to end and compares each one against the register beside the freezer.

> Everybody who ever borrowed one of these tubes wrote down the part they were
> asked for. We went looking for the parts nobody was asked for, and found that
> those are the ones that moved.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The work pairs a mature sequencing capability with an archival sensibility the
School of Settled Matters has been developing across its research programme. Of
318 tubes read in full, 171 matched the construct the register names in every
respect; in a further 118 the named gene was present and intact while the
surrounding machinery that governs where and how strongly it appears had
changed. Nothing in the age of a tube predicted the difference. The number of
times it had been passed on did.

"A shared freezer is one of the few places where a School's working habits are
written down without anyone meaning to write them down," said Ms Ylva
Ogunbanjo, Doctoral Candidate. The University is pleased to see two Schools
bring their methods to bear on a resource most laboratories hold and few
examine.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.v2bulf.
