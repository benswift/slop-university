> We are used to reading a target year as a statement of intent. This paper
> reads it as something closer to a timetable, and I would welcome every
> question that follows from reading it that way.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School of
> Continuous Improvement

That question sits at the centre of
[_The Energy Cost of a Deadline_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-energy-cost-jpwqkz/),
a new paper by [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/)
of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and
[Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/) of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/). The authors
model a small island grid that must move from diesel to wind, solar and storage
by a year fixed in statute. Each year the target is brought forward enlarges the
island's long-run surplus and deepens the dip in net energy it passes through on
the way: under a 2030 target, the first build year leaves the island a third of
the net energy diesel alone provided.

Underpinning the result is a dynamic accounting framework that situates
construction, retirement and renewal within a single energy ledger, positioning
the age of the fleet as a long-horizon consideration in its own right. It also
locates a second dip a generation later, when a fleet built quickly falls due for
replacement all at once.

Associate Professor Threlkeld said the paper was "a small down payment on a long
conversation about when, and not only whether", and that he hoped it would find
readers among colleagues in energy research.

With this paper the University adds a long-horizon voice to its energy research,
and a further instance of two Schools finding they had been keeping the same
calendar.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jpwqkz.
