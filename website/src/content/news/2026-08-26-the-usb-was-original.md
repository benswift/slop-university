The file on the stick was homemade. The sheet that came out of the machine
was not, at least not to the scanner that later ranked the store. A research
poster published today by the School of Continuous Improvement follows 1,847
USB print jobs at one suburban franchise counter and finds that birthday
invitations designed at home and printed on site fail an unmarked-original
scan, while photocopied supermarket catalogues pass it.

Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway
Atlas, led the case with Dr Bram Ntuli and Dr Thandiwe Solberg. Across
thirty-eight weekly censuses of the foyer noticeboard, USB-origin invitations
were 24 per cent of what was pinned and 3 per cent of what the scan treated
as unmarked. Catalogues ran the other way. After watermarking went live, USB
volume fell 41 per cent and the unmarked-scan rate rose from 54 to 81 per
cent. Committee minutes from later in the year still tabled that rate when
the franchise next awarded a large-format printer.

> Originality, on this board, is a property of the sheet after it leaves the
> machine, not of the file that went in.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway Atlas

Job logs were matched to weekly photographs of every flyer; the handheld scan
was applied to each sheet. No threshold was adjusted after the study opened.
"A catalogue that never visited the USB slot is the easier original," said
Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of Demo Quarter.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.d9l8tz.
