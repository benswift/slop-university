Not every invitation that asks for no gifts means the same thing once the
party actually happens, Slop University research published today finds. A
new paper from the School of Continuous Improvement audits 1,748
guest-arrivals across 163 children's birthday parties, cataloguing what
turns up instead of a present when one has been explicitly declined --- and
finds that how often nothing turns up at all depends less on the
instruction itself than on which four words carry it.

> Ask a guest not to bring something and most of them will still bring you
> something. Ask them to redirect it somewhere specific instead, and a lot
> more of them actually will.
>
> --- Senior Lecturer Torun Ezeigwe, Director, Master of Applied Measurement

The University treats the finding as one more entry in a ledger it keeps
intending to close: how rarely a stated rule and an enacted one turn out to
be the same rule.

Researchers tracked every gift-table arrival across four wordings in
circulation among the families they recruited --- a plain refusal, a softer
appeal to presence alone, and a charitable-donation redirect --- coding each
non-compliant arrival into one of five recurring substitute forms. Gift-free
arrivals ranged from a third of guests under the softest wording to well
over half under the charity redirect; a randomised reminder text sent 48
hours beforehand moved the rate by less than the day's ordinary variation.

"A reminder assumes people forgot," said Lecturer Ingeborg Nwachukwu,
Convenor of Capability Sprints. "Ours mostly hadn't. They just didn't have
anywhere specific to put the impulse."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.vwdh9c.
