Six thousand two hundred and fourteen times, a code written on a plate map was
looked up against the register that issued it. That is the work behind
[_Codes That No Longer Resolve_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-an-in-house-k1w0j3/), a
new paper by [Dr Dagny Okafor](/people/dagny-okafor/), Lecturer and Convenor of
the Evaluation of Evaluation program, with
[Ms Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/) and
[Mr Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/), Doctoral Candidates in the School
of Continuous Improvement.

> You can tell a great deal about a group from the parts of its record that
> everyone there already knows. We spent a year reading those parts, and it has
> quietly changed how our own School keeps its files.
>
> --- Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation, School
> of Continuous Improvement

Aligning a compound register, five years of plate maps and a generation of
analysis scripts within a single resolution framework, the paper traces how an
internal identifier moves between the records that carry it, and where that
movement leaves a reader unable to recover which compound was meant. Codes
resolved to a single register entry in 91.7 per cent of instances; the
exceptions gathered around the compounds the laboratory requests most often.

The paper extends the University's standing interest in the records an
institution keeps about itself, and in what a record stops being able to say.

Mr Nwabueze said the audit had left him fond of the batch suffix, "the smallest
mark in the whole record and easily the busiest".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.k1w0j3.
