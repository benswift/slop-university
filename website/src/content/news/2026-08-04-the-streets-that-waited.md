> The waiting list was in the same filing cabinet as the results. For four
> rounds it had not occurred to anybody, including us, that it was a comparison
> group.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

_The Streets That Waited Also Slowed_, deposited today through the Office of
Research Outputs, recomputes what one metropolitan council's traffic-calming
programme reports it achieves. A street joins the programme when a seven-day
tube survey puts its 85th-percentile speed above threshold; humps go in; the
tubes come back; the reduction is published. Of 87 streets qualifying across
four rounds, 26 were treated and 61 were deferred for budget.

The comparison arm came out of the council's own filing. Re-surveyed on the same
schedule as the treated streets, the deferred 61 fell 3.1 km/h between
qualification and the 18-month reading, against 4.8 km/h where humps went in.
That leaves 1.7 km/h attributable to the treatment itself. Applied to the
deferred arm, the programme's own reduction-achieved formula returns a reduction
in 55 of the 61 streets that received nothing.

The work was led by Dr Dagny Okafor, Convenor of the School of Continuous
Improvement's Evaluation of Evaluation program.

Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow in the School, said the direction of the
effect was never in doubt. "Humps slow cars," he said. "What has come out of the
published figure is the part that was going to happen to those streets whatever
the council did about them."

The University takes a certain satisfaction in work that makes one of its own
kind of numbers smaller, and commends the method to anyone running a programme
that keeps a waiting list.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.iklbx3.
