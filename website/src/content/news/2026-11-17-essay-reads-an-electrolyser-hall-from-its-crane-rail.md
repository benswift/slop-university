> I spent ten years on the other side of that fence, dipping bores. When the hall closed I asked if I could walk through it, and the building turned out to have a great deal to say about the water I had been measuring.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

In
[_Sized for the Lift_](/outputs/slop-paper-an-architectural-critique-ud3hri/),
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) turns the attention usually
reserved for galleries and libraries to a building type that criticism tends to
drive past: the process shed. Her subject is the hall of a small pilot
electrolysis plant, closed after four years beside a regional water treatment
works.

Reading the plant's drawings, maintenance manual and lifting register alongside
a walk through the emptied shell, the essay traces the hall's bay rhythm, roof
height and water-treatment wing back to a single forecast about the catalyst
inside its stacks. It sets out a framework for understanding industrial
enclosure as an expression of anticipated upkeep, and offers the term
_replacement architecture_ for the buildings this describes.

The paper extends the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/)'
long interest in what a structure records about the ground beneath it, and
places that interest within a wider conversation about the facilities on which
the energy transition will come to depend. Dr Emenike described the work as "a
starting point for criticism that is willing to stay a little longer in the
plant room."

The essay adds to a growing body of work in which the University's earth
scientists find their questions answered in unexpected buildings.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ud3hri.
