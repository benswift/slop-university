> For ten years I was taught that a reading is only as good as the person who
> took it. A vial, it turns out, keeps a perfectly good record of its own if
> nobody goes near it, and that has been a restful thing to learn.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Dr Emenike and Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) have
published
[_Reading the Drawdown_](/outputs/slop-poster-reading-the-drawdown-oa6xkl/), a research
poster that carries the School's long practice with standing water levels into
the chemistry of buckminsterfullerene.

The team set out a bench of 48 small vials of C60 solution across six solvent
systems, half under pierced foil and half open to the air, and read each one's
falling level every day through the glass without once dipping it. Placed on
that record, the first crystals told a consistent story: every vial went on past
saturation before it crystallised, and the open vials went furthest.

The work brings field-hydrology instincts and bench-scale crystallisation
practice into a single frame, positioning patient, contact-free observation as a
capability the School can extend across its laboratory agenda and, in time, into
its teaching.

The University is pleased to see a method learned at the bore head find so
settled a home at the bench, and counts the poster among the steadier pieces of
work it has had occasion to announce this year.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.oa6xkl.
