> Restoration is a long conversation between a crew and a coastline. We are glad to have been able to listen in on part of it, six years after it began.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints, School of Continuous Improvement

A new paper,
[_Out of the Water Too Long_](/outputs/slop-paper-planting-crew-practice-m2ljha/),
returns to a seagrass transplant on a sheltered estuarine flat six years after
its sixty-four plots went into the mud. It is the work of
[Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu](/people/ingeborg-nwachukwu/) of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), writing
with [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

Bringing the planting crews' original field records together with a full
relocation of the site, the study aligns process data from a single planting
season with outcomes at the resurvey, and positions the humble field sheet as a
restoration asset in its own right. Twenty-seven plots still held seagrass, and
those planted after the crews changed how they held their harvested sprigs
persisted at more than twice the earlier rate.

"Six years is a short time for an estuary and a long one for a set of stakes,"
said Dr Emenike. "We were pleased to find most of them waiting for us."

The University counts the paper among the clearest expressions yet of a
conviction its two Schools share: that improvement begins with what a team
thinks to write down, and that a good record is worth going back for.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m2ljha.
