A fluorescent mark joined to the cloth of a retired work uniform by a copper-catalysed reaction has come back from fifty trips through an industrial laundry with four fifths of its signal intact, according to a new paper from the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

The study, led by doctoral candidate Edda Nwafor with Halvard Nnamani and Dr Thandiwe Solberg, took cotton drill from twelve trousers withdrawn from a rented workwear fleet and returned it to that same fleet's weekly round, sewn into garments still in service. Swatches bearing a merely adsorbed version of the same dye kept under a twenty-fifth of theirs.

The research situates catalytic surface chemistry within the lived cycle of the working garment, aligning bench-scale reaction design with the collection, laundering and return processes that already structure a fleet's year. It also finds that the cloth's own service history shapes how much of the mark it accepts in the first place.

> Every garment in a rental fleet comes back, and every return asks the same question of the same cloth. We would like to keep asking it for as long as the fleet will have us.
>
> --- Edda Nwafor, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The publication adds a chemistry bench to a School better known for its registers and review cycles, and the University welcomes the breadth it signals.

"Within the School we have sometimes treated the laundry round as a cost to be managed," said Dr Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Demo Quarter. "This work invites us to read it as a schedule of observations already paid for, and that is a lesson we are glad to have learned on our own cloth."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qry32k.
