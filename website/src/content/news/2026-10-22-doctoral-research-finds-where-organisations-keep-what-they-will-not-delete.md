A folder that explains why it should be kept can, in the end, be destroyed. One
that simply says "do not delete" almost never is. That contrast sits at the
centre of [_The Drive Genizah_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-drive-genizah-mvavur/),
a research poster released today by the School of Emergent Priorities, which
follows several thousand shared-drive folders through three organisations'
moves to a new records system.

The poster is led by [Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/), Doctoral
Candidate in the School, with [Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/),
Doctoral Candidate, and [Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), Lecturer.

> Every organisation carries something forward that it has not looked at in a
> while. This work gives us a vocabulary for that, and a reason to approach it
> with care rather than haste.
>
> --- [Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/), Doctoral Candidate, School of
> Emergent Priorities

Bringing the interpretive resources of religious studies into dialogue with
contemporary records practice, the team aligned each folder's naming
conventions with a long-standing framework for the stewardship of texts that
cannot be discarded, and situated the resulting picture within each
organisation's broader migration journey. Of the 1,565 folders bearing a name
or a bare prohibition, 1,308 crossed the migration without appraisal or
remained on a decommissioned drive.

Dr Chikere described the poster as "a natural next chapter for a candidature
that has always been interested in what travels with us, and what we are
prepared to leave unexamined for now".

The University regards the poster as a timely addition to its growing body of
work on what institutions choose to bring with them, and on the quiet places
they make for it.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.mvavur.
