Nine checkout counters, thirty-four belts, and one plastic bar that nobody had catalogued before.

A new Slop University research poster from the School of Continuous Improvement sorts what a checkout divider bar is actually doing at the moment of placement into four kinds — a property line between orders, a courtesy signal, a hygiene reflex, and simple inertia. Lead author Dr Solveig Adeyemi worked with Dr Bram Ntuli, Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds, and Dr Dagny Okafor, Convenor of Evaluation of Evaluation, coding 1,240 belt placements across nine sites.

The property-line kind, the most common in the baseline sample, has since become the basis of an Orderly Checkout certification offered to participating grocers: badge-holders are scored on how often the bar goes in exactly where it is designed to. Five certified sites lifted that rate from 61 to 94 per cent of transactions within eight weeks, while four uncertified sites barely moved.

> A measure that only counts one kind of placement will eventually get nothing but that kind, whether or not it was needed.
>
> — Dr Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer, School of Continuous Improvement

Dr Ntuli, who set up the site-level video coding, frames the badge as an unplanned test of the taxonomy's own durability — an instrument built to describe a shelf item is now shaping how the item gets used. Dr Okafor, whose Evaluation of Evaluation program reviews how the University's own scoring instruments hold up under use, says the certification is now under her program's first review cycle: "the belt hasn't gotten any more orderly. The bar has just learned to show up more."

The full research poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.p2app2.
