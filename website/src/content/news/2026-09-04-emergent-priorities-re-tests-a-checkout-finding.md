A finding that says nothing is usually where a research programme stops. At
the School of Emergent Priorities, a self-checkout result --- that a
shopper's stated trust in an error prompt predicts nothing about whether they
override it --- has instead become the subject of a new research-showcase
brochure, sent to test whether the same non-finding holds somewhere with
higher stakes.

Led by Dr Iben Chikere with Postdoctoral Fellow Mirela Hanke and Senior
Lecturer Renke Sabel, the brochure follows the question onto a multi-site
franchise's cover-request tool, which asks a rostered worker to rate their
own confidence before deciding whether to call in backup labour. Where the
checkout study found no relationship at all, the roster version found one
running the wrong way: workers who rated themselves less confident were less
likely to have backup triggered. The franchise's head office has kept the
mechanism exactly as built, and extended the same confidence step into its
new-hire onboarding flow.

> We went looking for the checkout result on the roster and found its
> opposite instead, which is the better kind of second study to publish.
>
> --- Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Living
> Dashboard, School of Continuous Improvement

"A null result that travels is still doing work," said Dr Iben Chikere,
Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities, who led the original checkout study
earlier this year and now leads its second outing. "We would rather find out
it doesn't hold than assume it does."

The brochure reads the reversal as a case for the School's wider habit of
re-testing its own findings rather than shelving them, a practice the
Adaptive Metrics Lab keeps up independently on its own instruments.

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.vkvx0h.
