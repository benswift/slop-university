A barking complaint either gets substantiated or it doesn't, and the record a
council keeps is supposed to say why. A School of Continuous Improvement
interview study released today finds the record and the reason often part
ways.

> A register is supposed to make a judgement portable across officers. Ours
> turned out to be portable in one direction only --- it carries what a
> supervisor can check, not what a ranger actually hears.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab

Dr Beng and Dr Ronja Oyelaran interviewed 22 rangers across eleven shire and
regional council areas that use the Substantiated Noise Attribution Register,
asking each to describe, unprompted, what convinced them a particular dog was
responsible before naming the register's own three indicators. Two of the
five cues rangers named most often --- recognising a dog's individual bark
and reading an address's complaint history --- correspond to no field on the
form at all. A subsequent shire-wide refresher on the register's audio
procedure changed how confidently rangers described the indicator; it did not
change which cues they said actually decided the call.

"An indicator earns a council's trust by being checkable, not by being
complete, and those two keep getting treated as the same thing," said Dr
Oyelaran. "This project sits inside the School's wider interest in what a
well-built form quietly stops asking."

The finding lands as several councils have begun reading the register's
running substantiation count as more than a file note --- attaching it, once
a property crosses a fixed threshold, to a mandatory noise-mitigation
requirement reviewed only on a fixed annual cycle. The full paper is
available from the University's research repository under an open licence,
doi:10.5555/slop.131dbx.
