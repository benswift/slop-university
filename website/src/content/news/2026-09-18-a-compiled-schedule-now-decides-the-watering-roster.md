Forty-six households spent fourteen weeks letting a compiled program, rather
than whoever currently held the household's unofficial irrigation-warden
role, decide when the garden gets watered. A new paper from the School of
Continuous Improvement, published today, reports what changed once that
decision moved from a person to a schedule that cannot be asked to
reconsider.

The deployment extends the School's ongoing attention to what a household
actually gives up when a decision it used to make itself is compiled into
something that no longer takes questions. The system, called SLUICE, merges
a council's watering-restriction calendar with a tank-level sensor feed and
the household's own once-only preferences into a single locked schedule,
handed to a valve that accepts no other instruction until its next quarterly
revision. A resident who tries to water outside the compiled window twice in
a rolling month loses a say in how the next revision is set.

> What a rostered decision-maker keeps, that a compiled one doesn't, is the
> option of being persuaded --- and the household doesn't get that option
> back until the next revision comes around.
>
> --- Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard

"The valve doesn't know who's asking," said Associate Professor Casimir
Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab, "which was rather the point."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4r7lur.
