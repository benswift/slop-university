Divided committees keep pilots alive for reasons that have little to do with
the pilots themselves, according to
[_Neither Confirmed nor Terminated_](/outputs/slop-paper-perpetual-pilots-in-zatrh2/), a new paper by
[Dr Lindiwe Achterberg](/people/lindiwe-achterberg/), Lecturer, and
[Dr Ingrid Vasseur](/people/ingrid-vasseur/), Lecturer and Convenor of Futures
in Committee, both of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> Every organisation has a decision it is still in the middle of making. Our
> hope is that the paper helps people see that middle as a place with a
> structure of its own, rather than a delay on the way somewhere else.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper brings the tools of game theory to bear on a familiar institutional
setting: a standing committee that can confirm a trial or wind it up, but only
with a supermajority, and that carries the trial forward when it does neither.
Its model shows how each side's ability to block the other turns renewal into
a stable resting point, and how the gradual turnover of a committee's
membership, rather than any change of view, ultimately settles the matter. On
the paper's figures, a committee of thirteen working to a two-thirds rule can
expect to renew a pilot around sixteen times before deciding it. The authors
also set out a sunset arrangement under which the resting point disappears.

The University regards the paper as a careful and timely addition to the
School's work on how organisations hold their options open. Dr Vasseur said
Futures in Committee would take the paper up in the coming semester. "Much of
the School's thinking becomes practice in a committee room," she said, "so it
seems right that this paper should be discussed in one."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.zatrh2.
