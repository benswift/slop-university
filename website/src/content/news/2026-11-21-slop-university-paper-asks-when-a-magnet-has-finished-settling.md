For two hundred days, six slivers of metal sat between the poles of six small electromagnets in a temperature-controlled cabinet, and the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) kept watch. [_When Is a Remanence Settled?_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-magnetic-aftereffect-zl3geh/), by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) and [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), reports what became of them.

> Patience is not usually listed among a laboratory's instruments. We are grateful to everyone who walked past that cabinet for two hundred days and left it alone.
>
> --- Dr [Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Senior Lecturer and Lead, Received Readings Group, School of Settled Matters

The paper places long-horizon magnetometry, activation modelling and a working definition of settlement within one temporal frame, extending the University's attention to the demagnetisation curve beyond the minute in which it is usually read.

Four of the ribbons slowed and began to level off. Two kept losing magnetisation at the same steady pace from the first hour to the last day. Across all eighteen holds, only two came to rest before the two hundred days were up, and the others that level off would need between a little over a year and sixty-six years.

Associate Professor Uzoma described the collaboration as "a natural meeting of two Schools that are used to waiting for the ground to tell them something", and said the Basement Relations Group would welcome the ribbons' next two hundred days.

The University counts the study among the most patient work it has yet published.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.zl3geh.
