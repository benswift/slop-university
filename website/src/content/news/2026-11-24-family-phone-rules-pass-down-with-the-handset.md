> We set out to ask a small question about one phone changing hands and came back with a much larger one about how a household keeps its word. That is a good problem for a School like ours to have, and we are in no hurry to put it down.
>
> --- Dr [Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Dr Tredinnick leads [_The Rule Comes With the Phone_](/outputs/slop-poster-hand-me-down-phones-and-h5moq6/), a new research poster from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) written with Ms [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) and Mr [Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/). It asks what becomes of the rules a family makes for a teenager's phone once that phone is passed down to a younger brother or sister.

The team brought parent and sibling perspectives together into a whole-of-household account of how a rule travels, following each one from the day it was made to the day it reached a younger child's hands. Across 46 households, 63% of the rules on the older sibling's handset were in force for the younger one within a month of the handover, and the younger sibling typically met each rule well before the age at which an elder had. The reasons travelled less reliably than the rules themselves.

The poster reflects the School's growing confidence in taking its interest in settled arrangements to the kitchen bench, and it is the kind of close, patient inquiry the University is glad to see its newest scholars help lead.

"Phones will keep being handed down, and so, it seems, will the reasons nobody gives," said Ms Ekwueme. "There is a good deal more listening to do there."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.h5moq6.
