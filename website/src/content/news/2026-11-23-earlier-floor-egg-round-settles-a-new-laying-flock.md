The time of day a farm gathers its stray eggs may matter as much as the fact
that it gathers them, according to
[_Picked Up Before the Peak_](/outputs/slop-poster-floor-eggs-in-cg22p0/), a new
research poster from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/)
by Ms [Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/) and Dr
[Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/).

> Every routine has a time of day folded into it. We are only starting to ask
> how much else in a working week has been settled by when someone happens to
> walk past.
>
> --- Ms [Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidate, School of
> Emergent Priorities

Working alongside the stockperson of one commercial aviary house across a
flock's first fourteen weeks of lay, the team brought a fine-grained,
grid-referenced approach to a familiar husbandry task, aligning the cadence of
the collection round with the flock's own settling behaviour. Pens collected at
08:00 saw their share of eggs laid on the floor fall below one per cent by the
end of the trial, while pens collected at 13:00 levelled off near three, with
most of their late floor eggs turning up where the flock had first laid them.

For the University, the poster opens an agricultural chapter in a long-running
curiosity about the daily round, from the backyard washing line to the barn
aisle, and about the steady authority of whoever walks it first. Dr Obiora, a
Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, said the work had
"sent a soil surveyor back out before breakfast, which is no bad thing", and
that he expected the early starts to continue.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.cg22p0.
