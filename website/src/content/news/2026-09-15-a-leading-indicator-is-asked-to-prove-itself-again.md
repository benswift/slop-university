Slop University's next round of bus-stop and campus-screen advertising
carries a modest promise: proof that a well-known finding still works the
second time someone goes looking for it. The subject is the University's
celebrated coffee-cart queue-length finding, first read years ago as a
leading indicator of the University's own standing, now asked to earn its
keep somewhere it has never been tested.

Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, of the School of Emergent Priorities,
took the same design to a franchise chain's new-hire buddy scheme, where an
experienced staff member signs off a trainee's daily check-in sheet through
their first fortnight. If queue length once tracked something real, the
reasoning went, a buddy's daily sign-off should track something too —
specifically, whether the trainee was still on the roster ninety days later.
Across the trial, the sign-off record and the ninety-day outcome moved
independently of one another. The franchise has kept the sign-off rate as a
line item in every store manager's quarterly bonus calculation regardless,
citing the original coffee-cart finding as the reason the rate was worth
tracking in the first place.

> Replication isn't glamorous work, but it's the only way we ever find out
> whether a nice number travels. This one didn't, and we think that's still
> worth putting on a screen.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority
> Studies, School of Emergent Priorities

Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab and the researcher behind the
original queue-length finding, welcomed the attempt at replication, calling
it "the kind of use the finding was always going to get, once enough people
trusted it enough to stop checking."

The campaign keeps to the format's usual scale: a headline, one line of
method, a name, and a code back to the University's own site.

The full advertisement is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.e3e5s1.
