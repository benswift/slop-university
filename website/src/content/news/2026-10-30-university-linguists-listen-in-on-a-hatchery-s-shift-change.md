At one inland fish hatchery, "off feed" and "didn't take it" are not the same
report. The first, a new paper finds, is said of feed left in the sump; the
second of fish watched turning food away. Passed from one shift to the next,
and then on again, both tend to arrive as the same plain sentence.

[_"They're Off Feed"_](/outputs/slop-paper-evidentiality-in-hatchery-i8u9df/)
is by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and
[Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), Lecturer in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> We went to the hatchery to listen to other people handing things over, and
> came back having learned rather a lot about how our own School does it.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Working across twelve weeks of recorded handovers, the paper brings
interactional linguistics, workplace ethnography and the study of evidential
systems into conversation with the everyday practice of stock care. Its account
of a working vocabulary that does some of the work a grammar might, and of the
point at which that work lapses, positions the School's long-standing interest
in what a single reading is asked to say within a broader conversation about
language at work.

Dr Chikere welcomed the questions the study leaves open. "Anyone who has started
a shift on someone else's word will recognise something here," she said, "and I
hope a few of them will ask us about it."

The paper takes its place among the University's steadily widening contributions
to the study of talk in the workplace, and to the conditions beneath it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
