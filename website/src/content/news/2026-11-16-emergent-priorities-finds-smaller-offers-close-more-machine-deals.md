How long should a machine take to read an offer? For the negotiating software
agents studied in
[_Fewer Edits, More Deals_](/outputs/slop-poster-offer-legibility-in-m3kp54/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), the answer goes
a long way towards deciding whether a deal is reached at all.

The poster is the work of
[Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/), Doctoral Candidate, with
[Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/) of the School of Continuous
Improvement and [Dr Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/), Postdoctoral Fellow.

> We set out to study software and ended up looking harder at our own correspondence. The School has learned a good deal this year about what it asks of the people who have to read what it sends.
>
> --- Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Across 27,648 simulated negotiations, the team brought concession behaviour,
preference elicitation and deadline pressure together into a single account of
agent-to-agent agreement, placing the legibility of an offer alongside its
generosity as a lever on outcomes. Agents whose counter-offers changed only one
or two terms at a time kept agreeing as the cost of reading rose: at the
highest cost tested, the strongest of them closed 87 per cent of sessions,
against 58 per cent for an agent that conceded freely across every term.

Dr Hanke welcomed the poster as "a timely step in a conversation the University
has been having, in one form or another, about how its decisions are received
as well as how they are made".

The University counts the poster among a widening body of cross-school work on
how agreement travels between the parties to it, and on what each side spends
to get there.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m3kp54.
