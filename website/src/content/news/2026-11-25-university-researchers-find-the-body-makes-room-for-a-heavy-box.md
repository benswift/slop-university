Pick up a heavy box and the space your brain treats as close at hand grows to fit it. Pick up the same box empty and nothing changes. Whether the lid is on makes no difference either way.

That is the finding of [_Taken In by Its Weight_](/outputs/slop-paper-whether-a-sealed-nfhchk/), a new paper by Ms [Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/) and Ms [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) of the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) and Mr [Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/) of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

> I spent six years watching people carry other people's boxes down corridors. It means a great deal to see that experience take its place in the University's longer journey toward understanding what we carry, and why.
>
> --- Ms Hedvig Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The study brings the methods of multisensory neuroscience to one of working life's most familiar tasks. Thirty volunteers walked a short course with a standard archive carton in four different states, then stood holding it while a sound approached their hands and a light vibration touched their forearm. By timing how quickly each participant noticed the touch, the team mapped where the body's sense of nearness begins, and how far a carried load moves it.

Ms Ekwueme, who managed the timing, said the team had come to regard each response as "a small, honest message from the arms about what they were holding."

The paper adds to the University's growing body of work on things that are moved without being opened, and the School of Emergent Priorities welcomes this latest contribution to it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nfhchk.
