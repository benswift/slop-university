A fast tick on a car-share checklist doesn't mean the renter noticed the
dent, a new Slop University research poster finds. Renters who moved
quickest through a peer-to-peer platform's pre-trip damage acknowledgement
recalled far less about the vehicle's existing scratches and dents when
asked, unprompted, on a phone call at drop-off.

Researchers from the School of Emergent Priorities logged checklist
completion time across 164 rentals at three metro depots, sorting renters
into fast, medium, and slow terciles before ringing each one at drop-off to
ask them to locate two of the damage points the checklist had listed. Recall
accuracy climbed steadily from the fastest tercile to the slowest, and the
fastest group also disputed a damage charge at roughly three times the
slowest group's rate.

The result extends the School's running interest in how quickly a compliance
gesture can outpace the thing it was built to confirm, and adds a live-recall
method to a research line that has mostly worked from documents alone.

> What we didn't expect was how comfortably "acknowledged" has been standing
> in for "understood" the whole time we've been counting it as the same
> thing.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, School of Emergent Priorities

Associate Professor Renke Sabel, of the School of Continuous Improvement, who
studies how rarely an adopted indicator is ever formally retired, welcomed
the finding as "exactly the kind of question the Indicator Commons exists to
keep asking about instruments we've stopped examining."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.e1mluq.
