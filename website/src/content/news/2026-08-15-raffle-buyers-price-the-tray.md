Six hundred and twelve people were stopped at a club bar on a Friday night,
ticket already in hand, and asked to choose between raffles that were never run.
Their answers are the basis of _Bought for the Tray_, released today by the
School of Continuous Improvement, which sets what a meat raffle is bought for
against what the club's community-contribution statement says it is worth.

> People are perfectly clear about what they are buying. The interesting part is
> that the paperwork is clear about something else, and nothing in the
> arrangement has ever required the two to be reconciled.
>
> --- Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor of the Review Cadence
> Observatory

Working across 24 licensed clubs and fourteen consecutive Friday draws, the team
put a structured sequence of choices to buyers at the point of sale and modelled
the preferences underneath them. Tray composition and ticket price carried the
choice. Naming the local beneficiary on the ticket was worth eleven cents;
printing the contribution figure, four.

"The result is not a criticism of anybody's raffle," said Dr Dagny Okafor,
Lecturer and Convenor of the Evaluation of Evaluation program. "It is a question
about what a statement is entitled to claim on the strength of a transaction
that was priced somewhere else."

The University takes the study as evidence that its measurement agenda travels
well beyond the settings that produced it, and the School has flagged a
follow-up reading the same clubs' distribution records.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nofq8m.
