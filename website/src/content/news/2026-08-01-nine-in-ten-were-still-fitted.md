Slop University has released a study that found nothing, and has released it on
that basis. A research poster deposited today through the Office of Research
Outputs reports a randomised trial of the free measuring stickers handed out at
boat ramps, and reports that they made no measurable difference to what stayed
in the bucket.

_The Ruler Was Still on the Boat_ randomised 1,914 launches across 26 ramps in
four estuary districts over eleven weekends, with retention scored at the
cleaning bench by surveyors who did not know which arm a boat had been in.
Undersize retention came in at 6.8 per cent where a sticker had been fitted and
7.1 per cent where none had, a difference the study declines to read as one. At
follow-up, 91.3 per cent of the issued stickers were still on the gunwale.

> The stickers went on and they stayed on. What we can say is that the people
> who measure their catch were measuring it already, and that the ones who
> weren't did not start.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift
> Survey

The poster sets that result beside the district compliance rate the same
fishery reports, which is assembled from inspections carried out inside
rostered patrol windows, and notes that the roster is drawn up from the previous
season's rate. "A district that reports well is visited less, and then reports
better," said Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of
Priority Studies. "We would like to know what that loop looks like from the
outside of it."

Publishing a result that declines to move is, in the University's view, part of
what a research repository is for, and the School of Emergent Priorities regards
the deposit as continuous with its longer interest in what a stated priority
leaves unexamined. A re-analysis pairing retention against patrol-window density
is foreshadowed.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jmvuan.
