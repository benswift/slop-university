How many times should a run of drill core be sawn so that it takes up less room? The question has been answered at the bench for as long as there have been benches. Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma and Dr Brynjar Obiora of the School of Underlying Conditions have now answered it on paper, in _Rows Against Cuts_, the School's first work of mathematics.

> There is a kind of problem that sits in a shed for years because everyone who walks past it already has an answer. We wrote ours down, and it turned out to have parts.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper sets the tray out as a sequence of pieces that must stay in the order they left the ground, a row of fixed length, and a short list of pieces, among them the blocks that mark lost core, that may not be cut at all. From there it establishes what an orderly layout looks like, how the fewest rows for any allowance of cuts can be worked out directly, and what a further cut is worth. In its worked example, eighteen pieces fill eleven rows uncut and nine with three cuts; the second of those cuts saves nothing.

The University counts it a mark of a maturing school that it can be exact about a tray, and sees in the paper a considered widening of the School's capability from the ground itself to the arrangements made for keeping it. Dr Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, said the authors were "content to have settled the part of the matter that can be settled sitting down."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jof0p6.
