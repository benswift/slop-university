A country road can be closed by a single notice. Its formation goes under grass within a season, and the land rejoins the paddock either side. [_What the Carriageway Left_](/outputs/slop-poster-lead-zinc-copper-8fwcso/), a new research poster from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), asks what the soil keeps after that.

> A law school learns a good deal about itself on its knees in a paddock. We arrived knowing exactly when each of these roads stopped being a road, and the ground has been patient with us about how little that date settles.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Dr [Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) led the work with Dr [Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and Associate Professor [Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group. Their poster brings soil survey, laboratory geochemistry and the record of each closure into a single line of inquiry, laid across eighteen reserves closed over six decades on one granite plateau.

Lead at the old verges shows no measurable decline across the whole sequence. Zinc, antimony and copper leave over decades, mostly by moving down the profile. On four reserves the lead sits several metres from the line the plan records, where the road as built had wandered from the road as surveyed.

The University counts the poster among a growing number of studies in which a question first asked of a title is carried out into the ground it describes. Dr Obiora said the transects had been laid out to be walked again, and that the team hoped "to keep these reserves company for some years yet."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8fwcso.
