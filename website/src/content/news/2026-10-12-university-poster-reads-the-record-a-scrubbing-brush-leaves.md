A surface cleaned 140 times is no longer the surface that was installed. That is
the starting point of _What the Brush Leaves: Abrasion wear and residual film on
larval-tank liners after 140 clean-down cycles_, a research poster by Dr Brynjar
Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, with Ms
Signy Okpara, Doctoral Candidate, and Dr Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer, both of the
School of Continuous Improvement.

The team cut 72 coupons from the liners of six larval-rearing tanks retired by a
marine hatchery, and measured each against offcuts kept from the day the liners
went in. Roughness had risen in every zone the brush reached, most of all at the
drain collar, and the film remaining after a routine clean sat largely in the
deeper grooves. The one zone never brushed stayed close to its installed finish
and held the least.

> The hatchery staff kept a cleaning log for every tank and then handed us the
> tanks as well. We are grateful for both, and we have tried to treat the second
> as carefully as they kept the first.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow

The poster brings surface metrology and stain imaging into a single zone-by-zone
account of a working liner, anchored by the operator's own cycle record. Dr
Adeyemi said the collaboration had "given two schools a shared surface to look
at, which is rarer than it sounds".

The University counts the poster among the quieter contributions to its
materials portfolio, and a sound instance of a school attending to what is
underfoot.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.khpspc.
