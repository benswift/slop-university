title: Express-lane item limits sort baskets but not checkout times
subtitle: A regression-discontinuity study across 22 supermarkets finds shoppers
  adjusting their baskets at the limit and the transaction taking just as long
description:
  A new Slop University paper treats a supermarket express lane's posted item
  limit as a threshold rule and finds it sorts baskets sharply while leaving
  lane service time where it was, even after nine stores lowered the number on
  the sign.
date: 2026-07-28
output: slop-paper-twelve-items-or-less-express-anpa3l
---

The School of Continuous Improvement has spent the year on measures that stand
in for the things they measure. Its latest paper picks one that hangs from the
ceiling of almost every supermarket in the country.

The number on an express-lane sign is understood to be about speed. Working from
lane-level records for 38,412 checkout transactions across 22 stores of a single
grocery chain, the team compared what happens either side of the twelve-item
limit. The limit sorts, and sorts sharply: baskets over it almost never reach an
express lane. The transaction itself does not notice. Service time either side
of the cutoff differs by 1.7 seconds against a mean of 96.4, and the things that
do move the clock --- how the customer pays, whether anything has to be
weighed --- are not the things a sign can carry.

Shoppers, meanwhile, adjust to the number. Baskets cluster at exactly twelve,
with a matching gap immediately above it. Nine of the stores lowered their
posted limit from fifteen to twelve partway through the study: the clustering
moved within three weeks, the service time did not, and the chain's measure of
express-lane compliance rose from 88.1 to 96.4 per cent.

> A number on a sign is the cheapest thing in a building to change, and it turns
> out to be the thing our compliance measure is most sensitive to. That seems
> worth knowing before anyone decides what to do about it.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

Dr Torun Ezeigwe, who led the work, said the study had "found a rule doing its
job perfectly well, provided you are clear about which job".

The paper continues the School's work on measures that outlive the reason they
were introduced. The full paper is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.anpa3l.
