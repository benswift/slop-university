Two boxes on a shire's exemption form --- heritage garden, safety hazard ---
were meant to hold every reason a verge goes unmowed. A poster published
today by the School of Continuous Improvement reports that applicants write
in categories the form was never built for, and that the compliance unit
issuing the form has started sorting its queue by them.

Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director of the Master of Applied
Measurement, led the work with Dr Anouk Mensah and Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior
Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas. Two coders read
214 free-text justifications lodged with a single shire compliance unit over
two years and reached a six-category scheme by consensus, agreeing on the
assignment often enough to call it reliable. The unit has since begun
issuing a different processing wait for each category, even though the
scheme was built to describe what applicants wrote, not to decide who waits.

> A form has two boxes because two boxes were once enough. Ours found six,
> and the unit is already using them.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement

"This is exactly the kind of drift the Impact Pathway Atlas exists to
catch," said Dr Sten Okwuosa, "a scheme built to read the file ending up
inside the decision it was only meant to describe."

The release reflects the School's growing comfort treating its own coding
schemes as findings in their own right, worth publishing before anyone
downstream mistakes them for policy.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.trqusc.
