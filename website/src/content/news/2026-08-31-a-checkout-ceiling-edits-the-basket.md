A payment terminal's switch from a tap to a PIN prompt was never meant to be a decision about what goes in the trolley. A new Slop University research poster reports that, at one national supermarket chain's self-checkout fleet, it has become exactly that. The finding lands in the University's broader effort to describe what a fixed operational rule does to the behaviour built up around it.

> We would like to say the finding settles something. Mostly it raises the more interesting question of what a chain does with a threshold once somebody has shown it what the threshold is actually doing.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor of Capability Sprints

The team applied a density-based discontinuity test to 486,214 self-checkout transactions across twenty-four stores, comparing the spread of basket values immediately either side of the chain's contactless payment ceiling against two placebo lines drawn well clear of it. Basket values cluster hard in the cents just under the ceiling and thin out just above it, a pattern absent at both placebo lines; whatever shoppers add or drop to stay under the limit changes neither how long they spend at the terminal nor how many items they carry out.

"We're grateful to the checkout hosts who let us instrument their lanes for seven months without ever asking why," said Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor of the Living Dashboard.

The chain is piloting a host prompt script at four stores this September, nudging any basket within two dollars of the ceiling toward one more item, and reviewing a proposal to raise the ceiling itself by an unannounced dollar each quarter. The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.h2k3m6.
