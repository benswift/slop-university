Every three months, the marked performance pitches in one city's pedestrian
malls are handed out in order of how many hours each permit holder logged in the
council's app. Researchers in the School of Emergent Priorities took that rule
out of the policy text, wrote it down as code, and ran it twelve times.

_Reassigned Quarterly, Decided Once_ sets the council's own rule against four
alternatives on the same twelve quarters of records: a lottery, a rotation, an
hours-per-week normalisation, and the live rule with its ties broken at random.
Under the rule as written, the median stay at a grade-A pitch runs to nine
quarters. Under rotation or lottery it is two. Seven of the eight best pitches
held in the twelfth quarter were held by people who held one in the first, whose
order was itself carried over from a three-month pilot in which the app was
available to 46 of the 412 holders.

> A round that runs every quarter can still be a decision that was taken once.
> What we can say is that the ordering step is doing less work than its cadence
> implies.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority Studies

The figure the council publishes from the same data, the share of scheduled
pitch-hours logged as activated, spans seven-tenths of a percentage point across
all five rules, while the assignments those rules produce differ by up to 71 per
cent. "An indicator that reads the same whichever rule produced it is still
telling you something," said Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer in the School of
Emergent Priorities. "It is telling you what it is not able to see."

The University regards the study as a useful addition to a body of work on how
allocation rules behave once they have been running long enough to have a
history. The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.swzvim.
