The [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) has long been
interested in what an old record still binds, and for whom. Its latest paper
puts that interest to work among six pinned wasps.

[_The Host on the Label_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-host-on-g6r3sa/), by Dr
Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer in the School, revisits a small parasitoid wasp
first described in 1897 and named for the caterpillar from which one of its six
original specimens was bred. Short genetic sequences recovered from the
century-old specimens, read alongside a careful transcription of their labels,
show that the bred example belongs to a different species from the other five.
That second species is described in the paper for the first time, and the
caterpillar record now goes with it. The original name stays where it was.

The study brings heritage collection practice, contemporary molecular approaches
and two summers of patient field rearing into a single integrated workflow, and
the University regards it as a model of what becomes possible when a
discipline's most durable instruments are given a second reading.

> Taxonomy makes its promises slowly and keeps them for a long time. I have
> enjoyed being, for a season, one of the people trusted to keep this one.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
