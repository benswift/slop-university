Of the twelve volunteer observers who watched a faint star through the night and saw it stay exactly where it was, one supplied the measurement that fixed the northern edge of a small, distant body beyond Neptune. That is among the findings of [_One Miss in Twelve_](/outputs/slop-paper-deploying-volunteer-observers-jz8tu2/), a new paper by [Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/) and [Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/), Doctoral Candidates in the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

> Most of the network spent the night confirming that nothing happened where nothing was expected to happen. A School that asks people to do that should be able to tell them beforehand why it matters, and this time we could.
>
> --- Signy Okpara, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The study brings the School's established interest in deployment and structured reporting to bear on observational astronomy, aligning prediction, recruitment and timing within a single coordinated campaign. It reframes the observer who records no event as a full contributor to the evidence base, and sets out a placement approach intended to make every station's night count, wherever the shadow happens to fall.

Mr Nwabueze, who led the network's timing work, was philosophical about the campaign's one equipment failure. "The difficult part of a measurement taken forty astronomical units away turned out to be the storage on a laptop," he said. "I find that oddly reassuring."

The University welcomes the paper as a sign that careful planning travels well, however far away its object.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jz8tu2.
