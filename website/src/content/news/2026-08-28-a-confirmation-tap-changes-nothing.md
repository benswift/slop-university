A loyalty program's automated systems make a great many decisions on a
retailer's behalf, and the School of Continuous Improvement's newest trial
checks what happens when a human is asked to make just one of them instead.

Released today, the trial covers 38 stores of one supermarket chain over a
26-week test of the chain's own churn-alert system, LERT, which flags a
lapsing loyalty member and drafts a reactivation reward once their card goes
40 days unscanned. Half the stores kept LERT's automatic release; the other
half required the loyalty-desk supervisor to confirm the reward with a tap
on the EFTPOS terminal first.

> A confirmation step earns its place by changing an outcome. Ours didn't,
> and there's no version of this write-up where we quietly stop mentioning
> that.
>
> --- Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

Ninety-day reactivation among flagged members came back at 6.1 per cent
under the confirmation step and 6.3 per cent without it --- a gap the
University's analysis treats as no gap at all, despite more than six
thousand taps recorded across the trial.

The step's real achievement, said Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor of
the School's Evaluation of Evaluation program, was never about the members
--- it was "a fortnightly audit that now has a reason to keep running,
whether or not the case for it still holds up."

The chain is proceeding regardless: the confirmation tap is being
formalised into a fortnightly, footage-cross-referenced reconciliation
audit, extended to every store from next quarter. A result this flat, the
University argues, is worth publishing exactly as flat.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m6vevy.
