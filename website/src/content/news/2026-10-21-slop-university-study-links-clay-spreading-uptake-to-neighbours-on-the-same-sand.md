A farm on water-repellent sand will try clay spreading once anyone over the fence has, but it goes on to spread a whole paddock only when the neighbour who went first farms the same sand. That is the central finding of [_Same Sand, Next Fence_](/outputs/slop-paper-clay-spreading-on-ws26yr/), a new paper from the School of Underlying Conditions, written by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and [Dr Lindiwe Achterberg](/people/lindiwe-achterberg/), Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities.

> Every line on the district map was drawn so that somebody could use it. It is good to learn that some of them are being used.
>
> --- [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings the School's district soil survey into conversation with two decades of on-farm practice change, aligning the survey's map units with a contractor's records, aerial imagery and the accounts of landholders themselves to build an integrated picture of how a soil-improvement practice moves through a farming community. Its attention to the holdings that began a trial and have kept it going for many seasons since connects the School's long engagement with the ground to the University's wider interest in how a decision is reached.

Dr Achterberg described those long trials as "a patient form of commitment, and one the University is well equipped to understand".

The paper extends a growing exchange between the two schools on the farmed landscape, and carries the University's growing presence in agricultural scholarship onto ground that the School of Underlying Conditions has spent many seasons mapping.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ws26yr.
