> The School has spent a great deal of its time on the moments when work gets done. This poster has turned our attention to the moments when it gets scheduled, and I expect we will be staying there for a while.
>
> --- Dr [Osei Vandermeer](/people/osei-vandermeer/), Research Fellow and Convenor, Review Cadence Observatory, School of Continuous Improvement

[_Thirty Minutes After the Draw_](/outputs/slop-poster-salivary-cortisol-sampled-j6deey/), a new research poster from the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), reports that the most demanding moment of a brass band contest day, as measured in the players' own saliva, arrives at mid-morning, when the order of play is drawn from a bag. For every one of the six bands in the study, the day's highest reading followed the draw. The performances themselves, hours later, registered as a comparatively gentle rise.

Dr Vandermeer worked with doctoral candidates [Mr Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/) and [Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) across one full contest day, combining half-hourly hormone sampling from 141 players with the contest's own running order to build an end-to-end view of competitive readiness, from the morning ballot through to the walk off stage.

The poster takes its place in the University's widening attention to the hours that sit either side of the work itself, the waiting, the queuing and the drawing of lots that every organisation keeps and few of them write down.

Ms Ekwueme said the bands had been "patient hosts to a remarkable number of sample tubes", and that the team hoped to be invited back for a second contest season.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.j6deey.
