Two venues in the same karaoke chain can run an identical Friday night and
land minutes apart on how long a walk-in waits to sing. A new Slop
University paper from the School of Continuous Improvement traces the gap
to a feature nobody had singled out as the reason.

Dr Osei Vandermeer and Dr Torun Ezeigwe compared two ways a 24-venue chain's
song-queue app handles a singer who has submitted a request but isn't back
in the room when their name comes up: skip the name automatically, or call
it anyway. Every request already carries a short visible countdown before
either policy acts. The pair set the automatic skip against the same
countdown with the skip switched off, then measured completed songs per
rostered hour and how long a walk-in singer waited to be called.

Auto-skip's advantage over calling strictly in order was modest --- a little
under half a song more per hour. Turning the automatic step off while
leaving its countdown running reproduced almost that same gain on its own.
The larger, steadier effect landed somewhere the skip decision was never
designed to reach: walk-in singers, whose median wait fell by close to four
minutes, a population the app doesn't track at all.

> The skip step got credit a plain countdown had mostly already earned.
> That's worth keeping straight before a venue spends more on the part
> that isn't carrying the weight.
>
> --- Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor, Review Cadence
> Observatory

Dr Torun Ezeigwe, the paper's co-author, said the walk-in result was the one
the team hadn't gone looking for: "the app was built to manage names already
in its queue. It's a little strange that the clearest winner is the singer
it was never tracking in the first place."

The School of Continuous Improvement adds the result to a running caution
about crediting the visible switch over the quieter machinery sitting
underneath it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nr8unm.
