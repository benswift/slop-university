When a community asks for its letters to be made readable by machine, the
harder question is who else the machine will then be able to read.
[_The Names That Were Signed_](/outputs/slop-poster-membership-inference-leakage-juvgwh/),
a new research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions, takes up that
question inside one support collective's archive, at the collective's
invitation and under its oversight throughout.

The poster is led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow, with [Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/), Postdoctoral
Fellow and Deputy Convenor of the Living Dashboard, and
[Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), Lecturer, both of whom bring their
own schools' habits of attention to the work.

> The collective decided a long time ago who could be named in its letters and
> who could not. Our part was to make sure the model honoured a decision that
> had already been made, and made well.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Bringing privacy evaluation, archival stewardship and the long-run reading of
handwritten records into a single line of inquiry, the team tested what a
trained recogniser retains of the people whose words it learned from. The
answer turned on form rather than rarity: names signed in full and repeated on
the newsletter's closing page carried nearly all of the exposure, while members
who wrote as initials remained out of reach.

The University sees in the poster a careful extension of its growing practice
of custody-first research, and a fitting place for its earth sciences school to
bring a surveyor's patience to a boundary drawn by others.

Dr Hanke described the collaboration as "a reminder that the most important
design decisions in an archive are usually made by the people who write it".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.juvgwh.
