Eleven community theatre companies took five minutes off their interval for a
season, and the audience came back at exactly the same moment it always had.

_Does a Shorter Interval Hold the Second Half?_, a paper released today by the
School of Continuous Improvement, reports the first randomised evaluation of a
duration nobody in the sector could account for. Every venue in the study began
its season on the customary twenty-minute interval and crossed over to fifteen
minutes on a randomly allocated night, with two observers on the auditorium
doors timing 4,118 returns to seat across 34 performances.

Nothing that the companies care about moved. Return by the five-minute mark
held at 96 per cent under both durations, bar takings per patron differed by
six cents, and late returns were separated by a tenth of a patron in a hundred.
The one reliable effect was arithmetic: the show finished 5.1 minutes earlier.

> Twenty minutes was not a decision anybody could show us, and it turns out not
> to have been costing anything either. Both of those are worth knowing, and
> only one of them is comfortable.
>
> --- Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Demo Quarter

The design gave each venue a staged crossover rather than a competing
allocation, an approach the team describes as considerably easier to put to a
volunteer committee. "An audience appears to be returning to the second half
rather than to the end of the interval," said Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research
Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas. "That reframes the interval as
a trading window, which is a more tractable thing to schedule."

The Indicator Commons has since ratified a Standard Interval Duration of twenty
minutes and added interval observance to the fields participating venues attest
each year. The University counts a well-measured absence among the results
worth putting on the record, and regards the standard built on it as the more
durable contribution.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.h6skfa.
