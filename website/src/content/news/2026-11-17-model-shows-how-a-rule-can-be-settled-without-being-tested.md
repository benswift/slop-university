A few lines of mathematics, a queue of filers and an enforcement agency that can only see some of what they file: from that modest equipment, the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) has built a formal model of how a contested question closes.

[_Settled Without Being Believed_](/outputs/slop-paper-an-economic-theory-model-o5lkew/), by Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, follows issuers as they file one after another under a disclosure rule that admits two readings. Each can see how earlier filers chose. The paper finds that when the cautious reading takes hold, nothing is left for the agency to examine, so the question may never reach a decision at all. Stronger enforcement, it shows, can make that outcome more likely rather than less, and in one worked example the market settles on a reading it believes is probably not the law.

> I owe a great deal to the practitioners who have explained to me, over many years and with real patience, why they file the way they do. This paper is an attempt to take them at their word and follow it as far as it goes.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The University welcomes the paper as a widening of the School's patient, long-running inquiry, carrying a question first asked of court registries and printed allegations into the formal language of economic theory. It sits alongside the School's empirical studies of firms, seminars and tribunals, and puts the record they assemble into conversation with an established literature on herding, deterrence and precedent.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.o5lkew.
