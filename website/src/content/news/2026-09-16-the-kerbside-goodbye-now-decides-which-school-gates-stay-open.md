A dwell timer and a coded glance now decide which primary-school gate keeps
its own drop-off window, and which gets folded into the gate next door. The
School of Continuous Improvement has published a paper describing the
instrument behind that decision — the Handover Resolution Index, deployed
for a full term at nine gates across six outer-suburban schools — and the
front-office rule that now runs directly off it.

The Index times each car's dwell at the kerb and notes whether the
departing child glances back before the door closes, sorting every handover
into one of four named classes. Three of the nine gates crossed a published
threshold for the class the Index calls Escalated Departure and were
consolidated into a single staggered-roster gate the following term, with no
discretionary override retained. Their share of that classification fell
sharply once consolidation began; a follow-up check that dropped the glance
component from the Index entirely found the same three gates would have
been flagged regardless. The School reads the result as confirmation the
Index earns its place at the gate, not cause to retire half of it.

> A gate that runs long has always told a front office something. The Index
> just means it's finally the same something every time.
>
> — Senior Lecturer and Director Torun Ezeigwe, Master of Applied
> Measurement, School of Continuous Improvement

Dr Joost Nwosu, the paper's second author, placed the result inside his own
interest in what a metric keeps doing once its job is done. "The glance was
always going to earn its keep somewhere," he said. "It just turns out to be
here, in the record, rather than in the decision."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.v4q39b.
