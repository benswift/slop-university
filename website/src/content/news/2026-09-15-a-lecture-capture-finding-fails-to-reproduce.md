Eleven months ago, a Slop University study reported that lecture theatres
with more automated camera-switching produced students who remembered less
of what they had just watched. A new paper from the School of Continuous
Improvement asked whether the same pattern turns up somewhere with no
lecture in it at all — a regional convenience-store franchise's shift-handover
notebook — and found nothing of the kind.

Dr Joost Nwosu and Dr Sten Okwuosa pre-registered the comparison before a
22-outlet rollout of an AI-summarised handover app reached its fourth wave,
then measured incoming staff's blind recall of the previous shift's open
tasks against the app's own structuring score across 84 store-weeks. The
correlation the original lecture-hall study reported does not appear here,
and an ablation matching the original's design leaves the result unchanged.
The School of Continuous Improvement points to the pre-registration and the
statistical power behind the null as proof its replication program is
maturing, whatever any one result turns out to say.

> A finding that only works in the room it was born in is still worth
> knowing that much about.
>
> --- Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

"The certification rule was never ours to revise," says Dr Sten Okwuosa,
Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas, "and this
result doesn't give the franchise reason to revise it either — that's a
separate question from the one we were pre-registered to answer." The
franchise's own Handover Excellence scheme, which ties shift-lead roster
eligibility to the app's structuring score and cites the original University
finding as its justification, remains in force at every certified outlet.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.k2w8oa.
