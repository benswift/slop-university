The longest stretch without a seat on a typical audited walking route runs to
780 metres. A research poster published today by the School of Emergent
Priorities maps where those stretches fall across eight wards of one
metropolitan council, and finds them poorly anticipated by the count the council
plans its seating from.

Researchers surveyed 1,206 public seats on foot rather than reading their
positions out of the asset register, then walked 96 routes residents nominated
as errands they actually make, to the shops, the clinic, the bus stop, measuring
each with a wheel. Coverage was computed along the footpath network itself
rather than as a radius drawn on a map, against a 200 metre rest interval fixed
before the fieldwork opened. Some 41.3 per cent of the walked network fell
inside it.

> You cannot see this from the register. You see it about four hundred metres
> in, when there has been nothing for a while and you notice that you have
> started looking.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Ward rank on seats per 1,000 residents turned out to be a poor guide to ward
rank on route coverage: three of the four wards ranked highest on the count sit
in the bottom half on the share of network within reach of a seat. Sixty-three
per cent of the seats installed over the past four years went to segments that
already had one.

"A gap is nobody's asset," said Dr Petra Umbile, a Postdoctoral Fellow in the
School. "It falls between two seats, and between two seats is the one place a
seat count has never been able to look."

The University holds that some questions are only answerable at walking pace,
and is pleased to have put the hours in. The seat-gap segments have been tabled
with the Indicator Commons as a supplementary series.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1cgy0h.
