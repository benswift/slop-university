The [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) has always
held that an outcome is best understood through the full record of what led up
to it. Its newest research poster,
[_Moved by One, Carried by Several_](/outputs/slop-poster-moved-by-one-xg6ozr/),
carries that conviction into the immunology laboratory.

The poster is led by [Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), Doctoral
Candidate in the School, with [Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/), Doctoral
Candidate in the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/),
and [Dr Ingrid Vasseur](/people/ingrid-vasseur/), Lecturer and Convenor of
Futures in Committee.

> I spent a long time writing down who said what, and in which order, before I
> ever looked down a microscope. It turns out the habit travels. What I value
> most about this poster is how much patience it asked of all three of us.
>
> --- Ylva Onyekwere, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Working across 24 tumour spheroids and close to ten thousand recorded contacts,
the team brings together long-form live imaging, careful annotation and a
modelling approach that keeps every encounter in view. Seven deaths in ten, the
poster reports, followed hits from at least two different T cells, and a hit
went on counting towards the next for something under an hour.

The University regards the work as a fine instance of the slow, cumulative
attention it hopes to cultivate across every discipline it houses. Dr Vasseur
described the collaboration as "a reminder that a school's best questions
sometimes arrive from the far side of the campus, and deserve a proper hearing
when they do."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.xg6ozr.
