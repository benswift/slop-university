A ring of clay can be worked, without tearing it or closing it up, into a cup
with a handle, and the study of shape regards the two as one object. That
observation is the whole of the University's newest display campaign, _What
Stays the Same When Everything Bends_, which enters the landscape rotation this
month.

> I am grateful to the colleagues who explained to me, more than once, why the
> ring and the cup are the same thing. I held the ring. They did the rest.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster shows Dr Obiora standing in a paddock at dusk, looking through the
ring toward the horizon, with a line of auger holes receding across the ground
behind him. Its supporting copy runs to a single sentence on the features of a
shape that survive any stretching, followed by a few words from Dr Obiora on
holes and the counting of them, and an invitation to meet the University's
researchers.

The University is glad to have this part of its work introduced by somebody
who arrived at it with an auger. The campaign points readers to two pieces from
the School: _Where the Knife Goes_, a paper on the soil pit as a workstation,
and _Mostly Empty Space_, a showcase of research on honeycombs, foams and
lattices and the soils that carry load the same way.

The artwork was prepared for bus-stop panels and campus screens, and a dark
rendering accompanies it for the evening rotation.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4c5imm.
