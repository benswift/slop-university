A maturity model built to tell a boutique fitness studio how sophisticated
its class-waitlist text system is turns out to say almost nothing about
whether its classes actually fill. A new Slop University paper from the
School of Continuous Improvement scored forty independently run studios
against the two-axis rubric vendors already use --- automated spot release
against staff-triggered release, visible queue position against a black
box --- then checked each studio's quadrant against fourteen weeks of its
own front-desk scanner data.

> A studio can score full marks on both axes and still run classes at the
> same fill rate as the studio next door that scores zero on both. The
> rubric is measuring something about how the system was built. It isn't
> measuring what happens to the seat.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement

The gap held everywhere the team looked. Mean class-fill rate sat within
three points across all four quadrants, including the "mature" one vendors
hold up as the target state, and self-reported capacity figures ran ten to
fourteen points ahead of the audited number regardless of quadrant.
Reclassifying every studio on automation alone, with transparency dropped
entirely, reproduced the same flat result.

Dr Thandiwe Solberg, the paper's co-author, said the finding fits the
School's long-running interest in what a classification system is actually
for: "we keep finding instruments that are internally tidy and externally
silent. This one joins a fairly long shelf."

The School treats the result as a caution against building its next
instrument on legibility alone; a rubric that earns consensus among its own
raters isn't the same rubric that predicts an outcome nobody consulted it
about.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.szpgft.
