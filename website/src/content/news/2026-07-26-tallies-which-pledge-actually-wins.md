A hand-lettered poster taped above a locker bank is a primary-school
student-council candidate's entire platform, and Slop University's newest
paper is the first to code what those posters actually promise.

The paper, from the School of Emergent Priorities, sorted 587 campaign
posters from 34 primary schools into three categories --- procedural
pledges within a council's actual power, aspirational pledges with no real
mechanism, and undeliverable pledges promising something the council could
never grant --- then matched each candidate's category against their
class's own vote. Undeliverable pledges won 51 percent of their races,
roughly double the 24 percent procedural pledges managed. The gap held even
at the eleven schools where a teacher now checks each poster's promises
before it goes up; the review shrank how many undeliverable posters reached
the wall, not how well the ones that survived did on election day.

Inside the School of Emergent Priorities, the finding is being read as a
small, sharp instance of the pattern its researchers spend most of their
time chasing: a stated priority and the response it actually earns, pulled
apart and measured separately.

> If our own students have learned anything from this, it's that a vote is
> a poor test of a promise's size. We're less sure yet what that says about
> the rest of us.
>
> --- Senior Lecturer Ronja Oyelaran, School of Emergent Priorities

"The posters don't lie about what was promised," said Lecturer Iben
Chikere, the paper's co-author. "What's harder to explain is why the class
kept voting for the ones that couldn't be kept."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.p3oadh.
