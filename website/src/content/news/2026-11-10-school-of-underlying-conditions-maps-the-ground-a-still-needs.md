The [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) has spent most of its short history looking down a bore. Its newest paper looks up. [_Room for the Still_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-land-a-osa1kb/), by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions, and [Dr Ronja Oyelaran](/people/ronja-oyelaran/), Senior Lecturer and Deputy Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey, in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), follows a fringe settlement whose households now draw their drinking water from solar stills, and asks what a draft planning code means for the ground those stills stand on.

> Our School has always assumed that the important things happen below the surface. It has done us real good to spend a winter afternoon counting shadows instead, and I suspect it will not be the last.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The study brings a parcel-scale, solar-access lens to every residential lot in the settlement, aligning household water needs, building envelopes and tree canopy within a single solstice-day framework. It finds that 863 of 1,153 lots hold enough sunny, level ground for their still today, and that 537 would once the draft code is fully built out, with most of the change concentrated where lots are large enough for a second dwelling and small enough to feel it.

The paper extends a growing line of University inquiry where earth science meets the built environment, and it closes with three planning pathways for the settlement to consider.

"A planning instrument is written in the hope that someone will read it very closely," Dr Oyelaran said. "It has been a privilege to do that alongside colleagues who know what lies underneath it."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.osa1kb.
