Four hundred and twelve membrane compartments, each watched from the moment it
appeared until the moment it was gone, sit at the centre of
[_Where the Early Golgi Ends_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-one-golgi-hifxjx/), a
research poster released today by the School of Underlying Conditions. Its
subject is the Golgi cisterna of budding yeast, and its question is how long
each one spends as two things at once.

The poster is led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, with
[Ms Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/) and
[Ms Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidates in the School
of Emergent Priorities.

> The School has spent a good deal of its short life asking where one thing gives way to the next. It is reassuring to learn that the question travels, and that it keeps its shape at every scale we have tried it.
>
> --- [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Bringing live-cell imaging, single-compartment tracking and a pre-registered
boundary call into one continuous line of observation, the team finds that a
cisterna's change of identity takes a median of 13 seconds of a 162-second life.
The slow, blended handover familiar from averaged data turns out to belong to
the population rather than to any compartment in it, and the few cisternae that
linger between identities are, without exception, ones that merged with a
neighbour along the way.

Ms Balogun described the study as "a reminder that most of what the University
studies is busy with its own affairs while we watch".

The University counts the poster among the more unexpected yields of its earth
scientists' attention, and is content to see a soil surveyor's eye turned on a
cell.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hifxjx.
