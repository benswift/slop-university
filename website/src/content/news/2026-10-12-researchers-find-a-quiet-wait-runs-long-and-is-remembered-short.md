> People ask me whether twenty minutes is a long time. I can now say, with some
> confidence, that it depends, and I am glad to have had so much company in
> finding that out.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The twenty minutes in question are spent beside a steel pipe in a paddock,
waiting for the water in it to come back up. _A Watched Bore: Prospective and
retrospective duration judgements during slow groundwater recovery tests_ is a
research poster by Dr Emenike and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and
it carries the School's fieldwork into the psychology of time.

The poster brings an established laboratory tradition out to the working day of
84 field technicians, connecting the stages of a bore's recovery with the two
ways a person can be asked how long something took: with warning, and without.
Late in a watched recovery, when the level had nearly stopped moving, technicians
judged the interval at 1.41 times its length as it passed and 0.70 times
afterwards. A clipboard of paperwork brought both figures back towards the clock.

The University counts the poster among the season's evidence that its schools
read beyond their own shelves, and that a discipline may be visited without being
moved into. Dr Obiora said the fieldwork had "kept excellent time, by at least
one of the definitions on offer".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.n4u6hv.
