The School of Emergent Priorities has long taken an interest in the signals an enterprise gives off simply by going about its day. Its newest research poster, [_Twice Daily, Down the Race_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-dairy-herd-s-w506bs/), follows that interest out of the office and onto a working dairy farm.

The poster is by [Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral Candidate, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions. Across three weeks the pair set a line of ground sensors along the verge of the laneway a herd uses on its way to the milking shed, and let the cows' own procession, morning and afternoon, stand in for the hammer a seismic survey would usually swing. Forty-two walks to milking were enough to bring a buried river channel into view, its gravel base nine metres beneath the laneway, as a cored hole later confirmed.

> Fieldwork on a working farm means fitting in around the farm. We let the day's own rhythm set the pace of the research, and I hope a little of that rhythm has found its way onto the poster.
>
> --- Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Pairing passive seismology with close attention to the routines of an agricultural business, the study places the School's interest in everyday operations alongside the University's growing work on the ground beneath its rural districts. Dr Emenike described the season as "a good reminder that the ground is rarely short of things to say, if someone is prepared to stand beside it long enough."

It is the kind of work the University hopes to see more often from its doctoral programme: patient, practical and at home well beyond the campus.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.w506bs.
