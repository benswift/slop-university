Take the yellow-brown film off a grain of subsoil sand, wet the grain, and the
film makes its start in the places where it started before. That is the finding
of _The Same Pits Twice: Recurrence of Iron Oxyhydroxide Nucleation Sites on
Stripped Quartz Grains from a Single Soil Profile_, a paper by Dr Brynjar
Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, both of
the School of Underlying Conditions.

> A grain of sand is a small thing to have got to know this well. I would not
> say it has made the School any wiser about sand in general, but it has made us
> considerably better company for these fifty-six.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper connects the School's reference collection of described soils to its
microscopy capability across a strip-map-wet-and-look-again cycle, repeated
until a pattern could be said to have settled. Of the 1,742 pits the authors
followed, 148 took up a particle on every one of five occasions and 1,012 never
did, and a more thorough cleaning between rounds left the arrangement as it
was.

The University has long held that returning to the same place is a method and
not a want of imagination, and it is pleased to have that view supported at the
scale of a few millionths of a millimetre. Dr Emenike, who scored each round
without sight of the others, said the grains had been "more consistent than the
people looking at them, which is the right way round".

The authors note that their findings describe one horizon of one profile.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jt651z.
