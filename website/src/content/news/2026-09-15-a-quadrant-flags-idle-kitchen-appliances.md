Sixty-two households spent six weeks with a power sensor clipped to every appliance on the kitchen bench, so that the School of Continuous Improvement could work out, appliance by appliance, which ones had stopped earning their space. The resulting instrument, published today, is now the School's reference method for any household surface carrying more idle capacity than it can account for.

The paper, _Towards a Usage-Footprint Quadrant for Kitchen Appliance Retention_, crosses how often an appliance is used against how much bench it occupies while idle, sorting a household's benchtop into four named bands. An appliance landing in the lowest band --- used rarely, and taking up the most room --- is relocated to a cooling shelf for ninety days under a co-signed disposal log; if nobody retrieves it within the window, the log closes with a referral to donation or e-waste collection.

> The quadrant didn't teach us anything about kitchens. It taught us how much of what a household keeps has simply never been asked to justify itself.
>
> --- Lecturer Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Convenor, Capability Sprints, School of Continuous Improvement

"A benchtop is one of the few surfaces in a household where idle capacity is genuinely visible," said Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab. "We would like to think the same instrument travels to the surfaces where it isn't."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1ohecb.
