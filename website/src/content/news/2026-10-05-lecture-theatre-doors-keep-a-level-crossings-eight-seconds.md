At forty-one level crossings, a camp chair, a stopwatch and a tally clicker were set down beside the footpath and the walkers were timed. "Does a Walker Stop for the Train or for the Boom Gate?", a research poster released today by the School of Continuous Improvement, reports that people stop for the arm, within a second of its first movement, whether or not a train is anywhere in view.

> Nobody at a crossing has been told where to be, so each person there is making a small decision about timing. A door is the same kind of place, and we are glad to give it the same courtesy.
>
> --- Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor, Review Cadence Observatory

The study treated the moment the arm begins to lower as a threshold and compared the walkers either side of it across a season of closures, with the cutoff, the bandwidth and the outcome fixed in advance. The walkers' behaviour changed at the arm and nowhere else.

On the strength of the finding, every lecture-theatre door chime in the University will sound eight seconds before its latch engages, the interval the crossings keep between bell and arm. The latch will engage at the eighth second whether or not anyone is on the threshold, and a person on the sill will be entered in the Observatory's late-arrival column. No exception will be sustained for more than a single review cycle.

"The walkers were courteous throughout, and the chair was comfortable," said Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow at the School of Continuous Improvement.

The release reflects the School's long habit of finding the University's next rule on the footpath outside it.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.feg1u1.
