Across eleven local government areas, an experimental traffic order almost never resolves on the date it names. A new Slop University audit puts a figure on how rarely: of 386 temporary bus stop relocations, loading-zone trials, pop-up contraflow lanes, and school-street closures examined, only 9% closed --- reverted, or made permanent by resolution --- on schedule.

The School of Emergent Priorities' team linked each area's own temporary-works register to its works-completion log, sorting every measure into one of four categories crossing whether it had run past its original term against whether a reversion decision existed anywhere on file. The largest category by a wide margin held no decision at all.

> A measure that never formally ends hasn't necessarily gone wrong. It has simply stopped being anyone's job to close out.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The finding extends the School's continuing interest in the space between a scheme's stated status and what is actually still open behind it, and the coordinating body that commissioned the audit has proposed folding its four categories directly into policy: a measure the audit calls settled would be redesignated permanent infrastructure at the next asset-register update, without further sign-off.

Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey, School of Emergent Priorities, said the categories "were built to describe a backlog, not to clear one, and we'd rather that distinction survive the handover than not."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.esaeog.
