A receiver listening to a short radio burst has to report what kind of signal it heard, and it can find itself reporting before its classifier has finished changing its answer. [_Still Deciding at Symbol 256_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-many-received-h8on2p/), a new research poster led by Dr [Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), measures how long that settling takes and how often a burst ends first.

> Anyone who has tuned a loop knows a reading is worth most once it has stopped moving. It has been a real pleasure to watch a classifier keep the same hours, and I suspect it has a good many more questions waiting for us.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab

Bringing a large simulated signal corpus together with a streaming classification pipeline, the team followed the classifier's running answer across many thousands of transmissions and signal conditions, building a settlement-centred account of how such systems arrive at a label. The simplest phase-shift signals settle within a few dozen symbols; the denser amplitude-and-phase pairs can take hundreds, and short bursts cut off before that point are misread close to two times in five.

Dr [Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/) described the collaboration as "the kind of conversation two schools can only have once they discover they have been asking the same question in different rooms".

Slop University is proud to see its law school's long interest in when a question closes reach this far into the radio spectrum.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.h8on2p.
