Erosion pins, sediment fences, a rain gauge and the control log of a solar farm have been brought together in [_One Edge All Night_](/outputs/slop-paper-drip-line-soil-erosion-t1sduu/), a new paper from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/). Its subject is the narrow strip of ground beneath the lower edge of each panel, where the rain from the whole panel comes down.

> The University has been walking this hillside for a while now, first to look at the panels and now to look underneath them. It feels like the right direction to be heading in.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Over two cool seasons, [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) followed twelve grazed interrows of a tracker array where most rain fell while the rows sat parked to the east overnight. In the second season, half the blocks were parked east and west by turns from one rain event to the next. Connected rill length in those blocks fell from 34 m to 7 m, and the sediment they lost fell by about 60 per cent.

The study brings soil survey practice, operational data and a carefully worded controller rule into a single integrated view of the land beneath renewable infrastructure, positioning the night-time habits of the array as a lever for ground stewardship.

Associate Professor Threlkeld, who drafted the rule the controller now follows, said six short paragraphs had "raised more questions than they answered, and the School would be glad to hear every one".

The University is pleased to add the paper to its work on how renewable infrastructure sits within the landscapes that host it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.t1sduu.
