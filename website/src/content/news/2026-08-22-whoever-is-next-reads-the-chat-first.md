A shift left uncovered becomes, in most cafés, a message typed into a group
chat and a quiet wait to see who answers. The School of Continuous
Improvement spent twenty weeks watching exactly that wait at fourteen sites,
and is now proposing to keep watching it for good.

> What the private arm tells us is that a rostering score doesn't need an
> audience to hold a shift together. It only needs somewhere to be seen once,
> by the person who sets the roster.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab

Fourteen independently owned cafés ran the pilot for twenty weeks, half
posting a weekly leaderboard to the same group chat staff already used for
swaps, half keeping the identical score private to the rostering lead.
Interviews with forty-two staff across every site turned up the same
unscored habit at each one: whoever is rostered next reads the chat first,
leaderboard or not.

The School treats the pilot as an early instance of the portable staff
indicator its Adaptive Metrics Lab has spent several rounds arguing the
sector still lacks, one that could travel with a casual worker from employer
to employer the way a credit score already does.

Dr Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer, said the interviews left the team more curious
about the habit than the score: "the score is the part we built. The habit
is the part we found."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.cdi4x2.
