> Chemists have been numbering their compounds this way for longer than any of
> us have been reading their papers. What I hope the paper adds is a little
> more confidence about which number belongs to which flask. Every laboratory
> that keeps a notebook is already most of the way there.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Those are the words of the author of
[_Compound 7 Was Once Compound 4_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-bold-compound-oum2ay/),
a new paper from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) that brings
the tools of information science to one of synthetic chemistry's most familiar
conventions: the bold numeral by which a paper names each new compound.

Working with one organometallic synthesis group,
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) rebuilt the complete draft
histories of fourteen manuscripts and followed 297 numbered species through
1,184 changes of number. The study aligns the group's manuscript practice,
recordkeeping and supporting information around a single question of identity,
and positions the notebook code, which never changed, as the stable anchor for
everything filed beneath it.

The paper is the latest step in a growing body of work at the University on how
laboratories name, keep and later find what they make, and it extends the
School's reading of the records that sit underneath a finished result. Its
recommendation, a concordance between bold numbers and notebook codes carried
in every manuscript's supporting information, is offered to synthesis groups as
a practical starting point.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.oum2ay.
