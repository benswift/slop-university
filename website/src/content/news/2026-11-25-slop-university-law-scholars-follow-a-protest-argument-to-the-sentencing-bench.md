In most magistrates' courts the plea in mitigation is the shortest part of the morning. For protesters charged with entering one defence installation, a new research poster from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) finds, it became the place where the longest argument was heard.

[_Heard at Sentence_](/outputs/slop-poster-heard-at-sentence-tm4cje/), by Dr [Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Dr [Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) and Associate Professor [Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/), follows 297 prosecution files across three decades. Before a 2004 appellate ruling closed the necessity defence on that footing, most defendants argued at hearing that the weapons the installation served were unlawful. Afterwards most pleaded guilty and made the same argument at sentence, where the remarks answering it grew roughly sevenfold in length.

> The School began by asking when a question of law is closed. These files taught us to ask where it went next, and I suspect we will be putting that question to our own work for some time.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer and Lead, Received Readings Group

Situating close reading of the court file within the wider landscape of protest, procedure and the law of armed conflict, the poster traces a single line of advocacy across every stage of a summary prosecution, from charge sheet to sentencing remarks, and positions the plea in mitigation as an underexamined venue for questions the trial has set aside.

Dr Tredinnick said the files had raised "more questions about sentencing than any of us expected to carry out of a trespass list."

The University is pleased to see its law school take up a subject more often left to its strategists, and to take it up from the public gallery.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.tm4cje.
