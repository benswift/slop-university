A camera was left pointed at a hole in the ground for nine days, and the School
of Underlying Conditions has today published what a video encoder made of the
result. _The Boundary Came with the Grid: Quadtree split decisions on time-lapse
footage of a drying soil pit face_ is a research poster by Dr Brynjar Obiora,
Senior Research Fellow, with Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, and Mr Eskil
Nwabueze, Doctoral Candidate, both of the School of Continuous Improvement.

> I have spent a good part of my working life looking at one side of a pit. It
> was a courtesy to be shown that something else had been looking at it too,
> and I hope we have returned the courtesy.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster brings a purpose-built encoder alongside 2,160 frames of a single
pit face and repositions the encoder's working grid sixteen times across each
of them. Where one soil layer met the next abruptly, the encoder's dividing
lines held their place in roughly four positions out of five. Where the soil
changed by degrees, they held in 0.41 of positions at the finest setting and
0.17 at the coarsest, and the authors conclude that those lines arrived with
the grid.

The University regards a pit as a reasonable place for two of its schools to
meet, and is glad of the footage. Dr Nwosu said the collaboration had been
"conducted throughout at a resolution all three of us were comfortable with".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4oxigi.
