A fence between two soils pays almost wherever it is put. So reports _A Straight Fence Through a Gradual Soil: A Partial Budget for Subdividing One Paddock Along a Mapped Soil Boundary_, a paper by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, of the School of Underlying Conditions.

The paper mobilises 105 auger holes, a winter of dip-tube readings and one family's farm books into a whole-of-paddock account of a single decision, costing a straight fence at every position across a 68-metre band in which a sandy rise turns into a clay flat. It finds the best line and the surveyed line 41 metres apart and $264 a year different, and it finds that a fence with bends in it does not earn back its posts.

> I drew boundaries for twelve years and was never once asked what one was worth. Now that I know, I think it was kind of people not to ask.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow

The University counts the paper among the returns on appointing people who have done the work they now study, and notes that the farm and its landholder are not identified. Dr Emenike said the dip tubes had "established that the flat is wet, which the landholder mentioned on the first morning, and which it was good to have from the ground as well".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.mu5qx3.
