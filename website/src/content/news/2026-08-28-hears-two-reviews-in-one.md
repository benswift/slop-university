A shift manager's twice-yearly sit-down with a team member is supposed to be
one conversation. A School of Continuous Improvement interview study released
today finds most participants describe it as two.

> Every review has always had a version for the file and a version for the
> person. What has changed is that one of them now has a name, a score, and a
> bonus attached.
>
> --- Lecturer Bram Ntuli, Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

Dr Ntuli and colleagues interviewed 42 shift managers and team members across
nineteen stores of a national retail and quick-service franchise, five months
after the chain rolled out an instrument that scores a review's audio
transcript for eleven prescribed coaching phrases. Participants described
building the phrases into the front of the conversation before turning to
whatever else needed saying, and several said the second half more often
happened once the recording had stopped than during the scored slot itself.
An inter-coder check confirmed the score itself is applied consistently; what
it captures, participants suggested, is a narrower slice of the review than
the instrument's own dashboard implies.

"People are remarkably good at telling you exactly what they think a
recording is for," said Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the
Adaptive Metrics Lab. "The interesting part is what they say happens once
they've decided."

Dr Ntuli's own project has spent several years asking what a standing review
format lets an institution stop noticing about itself; this study turns that
question on a review the University itself had no hand in designing. The
full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.usabhn.
