Every household streaming box now ships with two profiles side by side:
one carrying whatever screen-time limit a family has actually set, and one
--- the Guest profile --- carrying none at all. Slop University's newest
paper asked which reasons send a household reaching for the second
profile, and how much of the night that reason actually buys.

The paper, from the School of Emergent Priorities, interviewed 58
households that had used a shared device's Guest profile against their own
screen-time cutoff, sorting the reasons families gave into four categories
and checking each against the household's own viewing log. The category
parents named as their biggest worry --- overriding the cutoff to end an
argument --- held only a modest share of the extra viewing time logged.
The category parents worried about least, forgetting to switch the profile
back after their own turn on it, held closest to half.

> We went in expecting to catch families out on the override. What we
> actually found is that nobody budgets time for the profile nobody
> remembers is open.
>
> --- Senior Lecturer Runa Adegoke, Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey,
> School of Emergent Priorities

Senior Research Fellow Anneke Tolan, who led the interview fieldwork, said
households rarely described the Guest profile as a loophole: "most parents
described it the way they'd describe a spare key --- there for exactly one
purpose, until the day it quietly becomes the door everyone uses."

The taxonomy was checked against ninety days of device history; a parallel
trial offering a monthly reminder of Guest-profile use moved neither the
reminded households nor an unreminded comparison group.

A boundary, the School's researchers note, survives for exactly as long as
somebody is watching it get crossed.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fjil31.
