Roughly one in four of the signatures on a circulating office farewell card was
not penned by the colleague it names.

_Attributing the Well-Wishers_, a paper released today by the School of
Continuous Improvement, brings examiner-grade forensic document examination to a
document most workplaces produce and none have audited. Dr Sten Okwuosa, Dr
Thandiwe Solberg and Dr Bram Ntuli examined 1,438 signed inscriptions across 61
donated cards, comparing each against writing samples volunteered by the
well-wishers themselves. The team harmonised three streams of handwriting
evidence into a single attribution protocol and reconstructed each card's
journey through the workplace, showing where stand-in signatures enter the
record: overwhelmingly in the white space that remains once the route has
closed, where the card's organiser completes the page.

> A card is a small archive, and an archive is kindest to whoever holds the pen
> last. We are pleased the School could give a familiar object that much
> attention.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway Atlas

The study continues the School's careful attention to what everyday records
quietly attest. "The value of the work is that a familiar object can now be
described with some precision," said Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and
Convenor, Demo Quarter. A participating workplace is now piloting a
witnessed-signing arrangement informed by the findings.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.u1gpuf.
