Given loose ground, the earthworms of one Slop University laboratory colony dig
deep. A research poster published today reports what became of that depth when
the chemical signalling thought to lie behind it was switched off, and finds
that the answer changed with the soil. _The Soil Sets the Floor: Serotonin
receptor blockade and burrowing depth across five soil textures_ is the work of
Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior
Lecturer, both of the School of Underlying Conditions.

> The soils were on the shelf already, described and labelled, waiting for a use
> none of us had thought of. It was agreeable to find one that asked so little
> digging of the School itself.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The study anchors a behavioural question in a sand-to-clay continuum, pairing
treated and untreated worms across 120 columns and reading the depth each had
reached after three days. In sand the treated worms stopped 12.8 centimetres
short of their untreated neighbours. In clay the two groups finished together,
the soil having settled the matter before the treatment could.

The University counts it a mark of a mature research culture that a question
from the life sciences can be handed to its earth scientists and returned
answered in their own materials. Dr Emenike said the project had "obliged the
School to notice that the ground it studies has tenants, and that they hold
views on it".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.g4yj6a.
