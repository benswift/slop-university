Some gaps are left on purpose. A new paper by Dr Renke Sabel of the School of
Continuous Improvement makes that case for fourteen rings of dots in an
anonymous commentary on rhythmic cycles, each drawn with one place empty in the
oldest surviving copy, and each completed by the copyists who came after.

> Our School gives most of its days to making things better, so it was a
> pleasure to spend a few months with a copyist who stopped three times and
> left the page as he found it. I like to think there is something in that for
> all of us.
>
> --- Dr Renke Sabel, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Indicator Commons, School of Continuous Improvement

The commentary sets out each cycle as a ring, one dot to every beat. Working
across all three copies, the paper aligns the oldest manuscript's open rings
with the commentary's own sign for a cycle that comes round again, sets each
empty place against the spacing of its neighbours, and follows the later
scribes as they add dots, strike through glosses and write new ones. Along the
way it finds that one copyist left open exactly the three rings whose glosses
argue for the gap at length.

The paper joins a widening thread of University scholarship that treats the
copy as a document in its own right, and the corrector as an author worth
reading closely.

Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions, whose
own work on an anonymous keyboard cycle the paper cites, said she was "delighted
to see an empty place given a whole paper, and a little envious of the copyist
who knew when to stop".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bntkrb.
