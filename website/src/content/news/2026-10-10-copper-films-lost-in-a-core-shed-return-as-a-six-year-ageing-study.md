Copper nanowire films that spent six years beside limestone came through in
better order than an identical set kept in an office desk. That is one of the
results of _What the Films Lay Beside_, a paper published today by Associate
Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Brynjar
Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, of the School of Underlying Conditions.

The forty-eight films were made for a project that ended, boxed, and then
distributed during a move to wherever there was room. Five boxes were set down
in vacant rows of drill core trays. All were recovered seventy-four months
later, and because each film had been characterised before it was put away, the
authors were able to say how much every one of them had changed.

> We are grateful to whoever put the boxes where they did. It is not a design we
> would have had the nerve to propose, and they carried it out without telling
> anyone, which is the only way it could have run its full term.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, School of Underlying Conditions

Every film had lost conductance. A protective coating helped, and helped most in
the hardest positions, but the paper finds that where a box had been left counted
for more. Films stored beside sulphide-bearing core fared worst, and four of
them no longer conducted at all.

The paper is, for the University, a welcome return on its settled practice of
keeping things, and an indication of the range a core shed can support. Dr
Emenike said the team had "begun a second set, and this time written down where
it is."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dwely5.
