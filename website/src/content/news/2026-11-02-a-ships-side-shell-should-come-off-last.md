Seventeen loading conditions, fifteen numbered blocks of steel and one cutting
drawing make up the working material of
[_Shell Last_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-the-cutting-fvveyr/), a new paper led by
[Dr Anneke Tolan](/people/anneke-tolan/), Senior Research Fellow and Deputy
Convenor of Futures in Committee in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), with
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/), Lead of the Adaptive
Metrics Lab, and [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and Convenor of
Improvement Grand Rounds, both of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

> Futures in Committee has spent years watching plans being written. This is the first one we have watched float, and I suspect it will change the questions we bring to the next plan we meet on dry land.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Futures in Committee

The paper follows an offshore supply vessel through its dismantling alongside a
recycling quay. Working from the yard's own cutting plan, the team recast every
stage of the work as a loading condition and brought hydrostatic modelling,
rainfall scenarios and a search across every feasible order of cuts into a
single stage-by-stage view of the hull. The plan as drawn scored best on the
measure the yard was tracking, yet left the least room between the water and the
lowest cut edge: 1.64 m, against 2.49 m when the side shell is taken last.

The University sees in the paper a welcome sign of its planning scholarship
finding its sea legs. Dr Ntuli said the collaboration "puts the order of
operations at the front of the conversation, which is where the people doing
the cutting have always kept it."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fvveyr.
