A shift can be swapped in a single tap, but whose request clears first still comes down to a Priority Score, and a new brochure from the School of Continuous Improvement follows that score into the back room where it's decided. The instrument is not a new one: it is the University's own staff car-park Priority Score, previously found to track how confidently a form was filled in rather than any independently logged need, now re-run on a retail chain's shift-swap queue to see whether the same gap holds.

> We took an instrument that had already told us what it actually measures, and asked it the same question somewhere it had never been asked before.
>
> --- Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

It didn't answer the same way twice. Genuine rostering need and the Priority Score converged within a fortnight of the trial's start, a shift the researchers trace to the back room's existing loss-prevention camera rather than to any change in the form. For the evaluation-ecosystem agenda, the result is the kind of instrument-testing the programme exists to produce: a published finding taken somewhere new, and reported on regardless of which way it moves.

"An instrument doesn't stop being tested once it's adopted," said Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab. "Ours keeps getting asked the same question in rooms it wasn't built for, and we keep publishing whatever it says back."

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3pap1i.
