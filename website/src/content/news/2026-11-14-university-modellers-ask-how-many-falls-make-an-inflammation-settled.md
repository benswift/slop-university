Two thousand immune responses, none of which ever happened, were watched for
three weeks each by a team from two of the University's Schools, and every one
was asked the same question at every reading: is it over yet?
[_Called While Still Open_](/outputs/slop-paper-when-a-simulated-n0diyp/),
published this week, reports how often the answer came back yes too soon.

The paper is led by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), with
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/) of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) and
[Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/). Pairing the law
school's long interest in closure with an engineer's sense of stability, the
team built a computational model of the inflammatory response, observed it
through a single simulated marker, and set it against a family of rules for
declaring the matter resolved. The study finds that a response which has
stopped moving will, given enough readings, appear to fall several times in a
row, and that the rules strict enough to resist this leave many genuine
recoveries uncalled.

> A control engineer is trained to ask how long a system takes to settle, and
> rather less often who decides that it has. It has been a real pleasure to
> spend the year on the second question in such good legal company.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead, Adaptive Metrics Lab

Dr Penhallow welcomed the questions the paper leaves open, "which, in our
School, is generally where the interesting work begins".

The paper takes its place in the University's growing body of work on when a
question may be considered closed, a body the University is pleased to see
extending well beyond the law library.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.n0diyp.
