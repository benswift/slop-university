The [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) has spent much
of this year on how things end, and its newest paper takes the question
offshore.
[_Sized for the Deck_](/outputs/slop-paper-piece-small-removal-of-nj34g1/)
follows a normally unattended gas platform as it is cut apart and carried home
one section at a time, and asks a question the work rarely stops to ask: how
large each piece ought to be.

> Every section of that platform passed through the hands of people who knew
> its weight long before our records did. The paper is theirs as much as ours,
> and we would like it to be on board for the next deck that needs it.
>
> --- [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/), Doctoral Candidate, School
> of Emergent Priorities

Written with [Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/) and
Dr [Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/), the paper brings cutting progress,
vessel capacity and the patience of the sea into a single planning frame, and
finds that the right answer shifts with the seasons. Its recommendation, a
smaller piece in summer and a larger one in winter, positions the School's
priorities work at the meeting point of engineering practice and operational
judgement.

"A plan that changes with the weather is still a plan," said Dr Mensah, "and
the School is getting comfortable with that."

The paper extends a growing conversation across the University about the
sequencing of dismantling, and the School welcomes the interest of colleagues
who move heavy things for a living.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nj34g1.
