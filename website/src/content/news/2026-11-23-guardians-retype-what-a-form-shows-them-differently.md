Given the chance to go back through an online form, people check their answers. A surprising number then type them in again. [_It Doesn't Look Like What I Typed_](/outputs/slop-paper-defensive-re-entry-why-7vzyvx/), a new paper led from the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), gives that habit a name, defensive re-entry, and traces it to the moment a form hands someone's own answer back to them in a shape they do not recognise.

> Every winter a great many families sit down at the kitchen table and trust a club's form with the details of a child's weekend. We are grateful to the guardians who let us sit beside them while they did it, and to the volunteer registrars who thought the pattern worth a second look.
>
> --- Dr [Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

Working with Mr [Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/) and Ms [Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidates in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), Dr Ntuli brought registration logs, a season-long field experiment across 9,780 registrations and a series of think-aloud sessions into a single account of how people come to rely on what a form keeps for them, and what an interface can do to earn that reliance.

Mr Nnamani, who spent seven seasons as a junior sport registrar before his doctoral studies, called the paper "a small kindness to everyone who has ever uploaded the same photo twice, just to be sure".

The University welcomes the work as a useful contribution to the everyday experience of the people its own processes, and the sector's, ask to fill things in.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7vzyvx.
