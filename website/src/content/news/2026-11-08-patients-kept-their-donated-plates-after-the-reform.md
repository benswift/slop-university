A country hospital's minute book can say in a single line that its funding has changed. Its patients, a new paper from Slop University suggests, may not find out for some years, and when they do it will be because a plate has broken.

[Dr Dagny Okafor](/people/dagny-okafor/) of the School of Continuous Improvement and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) of the School of Underlying Conditions excavated two rubbish pits behind a long-closed cottage hospital founded on subscriptions and gifts and later passed to public funding. One pit served the wards and the other the board room. Across 885 vessels and six phases of rubbish, the board's own crested crockery arrives on the board's table almost as soon as it was ordered, while the wards go on eating from mended, mismatched household plates, many of them gifts from local families and some given after the reform.

> Most of what we counted was looked after by people whose names are not in any minute book. They washed it, mended it and handed it on, and the School is grateful to have been trusted with what they left.
>
> --- Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation, School of Continuous Improvement

The study brings stratigraphic method and the history of hospital finance into a shared conversation about how change in a health service is carried from the committee room to the bedside. Dr Obiora said the team had approached the dividing layers of the tip "with the patience of a soil survey" and hoped to take it to further sites "as their custodians allow".

The University counts the paper among its contributions to understanding how health services change, and how long the change takes to arrive.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.sqixwu.
