A short timber stair, a band of cliff no taller than a raised arm, and a camera carried down to the same rock platform each late winter for seven years are the materials of the University's newest paper on the places where geology is taught.

[_Scars Beget Scars_](/outputs/slop-paper-scars-beget-scars-wtu94j/) is by [Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), Doctoral Candidate in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), and [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> Most of us can still find the first contact we were ever shown. It has been a privilege to spend some time thinking about the students who will be shown this one next.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings together repeat three-dimensional survey, a careful census of fresh rock faces and the site's own visit register to build a richer picture of how a teaching outcrop is used, and of how its use shapes the outcrop in turn. It finds that new marks gather where earlier ones are freshest, because a freshly exposed face shows the boundary between the two rock units more plainly than anything around it, and it offers a framework for thinking about where, as well as how often, a visit leaves its trace.

Mr Nnamani described the stretch of platform as "a classroom that has been keeping its own minutes", and said he was glad to have been able to read some of them.

The University sees the paper as part of a longer undertaking to leave its field sites at least as instructive as it found them.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wtu94j.
