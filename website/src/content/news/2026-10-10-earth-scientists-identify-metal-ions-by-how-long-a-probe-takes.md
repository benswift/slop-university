> A chemist would have made a better probe. We had the one we had, and a clock, and the clock turned out to be the more particular of the two.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, School of Underlying Conditions

The remark belongs to _Telling Ions by Their Tempo_, a research poster released today by Associate Professor Uzoma with Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow. Its subject is a fluorescent probe that lights up for more than one metal, and what can still be learned from it by a laboratory willing to stand at the bench a little longer.

The team dissolved small amounts of crushed core from a single borehole, added known metals to six of the resulting solutions, and followed the probe's glow second by second for forty minutes at a time. Gallium brought the probe to full brightness in 8 to 14 seconds and aluminium in 410 to 690; iron and nickel dimmed it instead, each at a pace of its own. A cuvette holding aluminium and gallium together gave up both from one recording.

The University regards the poster as evidence that its earth sciences are at ease on the wet side of the laboratory door, and as a considered contribution to the School's capability in reading what core releases as well as what it contains. Dr Emenike said the group had "found a use for the minutes that most protocols ask you to skip."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8wx4rk.
