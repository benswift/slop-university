When a community sets new words to a melody it already loves, which part of the
song is still its own? A new research poster from the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) puts that
question to a single hand-copied songbook, kept for three decades by a Jewish
mutual-aid society in a provincial town, and finds the answer in the tune.

_One Tune, Three Allegiances_ is the work of
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/), Lead of the Adaptive
Metrics Lab, with [Dr Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/), Postdoctoral Fellow,
and [Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), Lecturer, both of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> We would be delighted to hear this songbook sung again one day. The Lab can
> offer the tolerances; the voices will have to come from somewhere else.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab

Bringing a measurement laboratory's methods into dialogue with the long
practice of communal song, the team mapped the songbook's 214 entries onto the
61 melodies they were written under, aligning the inherited, the translated and
the newly composed within a single framework of tune, text and occasion.
Yiddish songs, Spanish renderings of Kipling's ballads and Borges's milongas
turn out to share the same melodies, matched by metre rather than by origin.

"It has been a pleasure to follow the words from page to page while the
melodies stayed put," said Dr Mensah.

The poster takes its place in a growing seam of University scholarship that
lets an instrument built for one discipline listen carefully to another.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9dehnm.
