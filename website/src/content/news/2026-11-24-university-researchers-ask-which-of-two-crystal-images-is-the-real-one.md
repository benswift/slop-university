The School of Settled Matters has brought its long practice of asking what decides a question to one of the most exacting imaging techniques in the physical sciences. [_Same Pattern, Opposite Strain_](/outputs/slop-poster-which-way-is-9ngnit/), a new research poster by Associate Professor [Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/), Ms [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) and Ms [Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), looks at coherent X-ray images of crystals far smaller than a grain of dust, each of which can be read two ways.

> There is something reassuring about a result that arrives with its own reflection. It kept us careful all the way through, and it kept us company.
>
> --- Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Bringing iterative reconstruction together with electron microscopy, the team examined forty-one faceted nanocrystals, a hundred times each, sorting every image against its mirror-image twin and documenting, crystal by crystal, the outside evidence that settled which way its strain ran. The result is a practical, transparent account of interpretation that the University expects to inform imaging practice well beyond a single beamline.

The University counts the poster among the clearest signs yet that a question of interpretation travels comfortably between the law library and the experimental hall.

Associate Professor Threlkeld described the poster as "a small piece of housekeeping that we hope others will find useful to borrow."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9ngnit.
