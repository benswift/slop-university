The editions that restore a missing inner voice to a set of late-sixteenth-century
motets have, at most of the moments that count, been restoring a voice that
adds nothing the other three parts were not already singing. That is the
central finding of
[_Where the Altus Could Have Been_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-the-altus-a2ov3x/),
a new research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions by
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/),
[Dr Marek Solheim](/people/marek-solheim/) and
[Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/).

> This is the first time the Basement Relations Group has worked from a page
> rather than a tray, and the method travelled further than any of us had
> planned. The School will be finding other collections for it to visit.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The poster aligns the analytical traditions of music theory with the geometry
of chord spaces, mapping every possible reconstruction of the absent part
across a corpus of 31 motets and benchmarking two published completions against
the original voice where it survives elsewhere. Its outcome is a principled,
sonority-by-sonority account of which choices the surviving books already make,
and which remain genuinely open to an editor.

Dr Solheim, Deputy Convenor of the Horizon Register, sees the work as a
question of inheritance. "An edition is a decision made long ago that every
choir since has sung without asking," he said. "The poster lets us tell those
decisions apart from the ones the sources made for themselves, which seems to
me the least a historian owes them."

The University counts the study among a widening body of its work on what a
record keeps of what it has lost, and welcomes its arrival in the choir loft.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.a2ov3x.
