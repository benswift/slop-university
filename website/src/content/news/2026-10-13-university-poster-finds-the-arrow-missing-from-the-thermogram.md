Some time around 2011, the last hand-drawn arrow left the thermal analysis curves of one soil and clay laboratory. Its departure is recorded in _Which Way Is Up? How forty years of one soil laboratory's thermograms told the reader the direction of an exotherm_, a research poster by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, both of the School of Underlying Conditions, with Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities.

A curve of this kind may be drawn with its peaks pointing up or pointing down, and something on the page has to say which. The authors coded 1,264 curves made between 1981 and 2020 for where that instruction sat. On the earliest chart rolls an operator's pen supplied it 71 per cent of the time. In the latest period 54 per cent of curves carried no instruction at all, and of 212 curves later reproduced in another document, 31 had been turned over on the way.

> A curve that has lost its arrow still has a reader, and we know very little about what that reader does next. We would be glad to be told.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster aligns the study of documents and media with the working record of an analytical laboratory, placing the axis label within a wider account of how scientific images travel from the instrument that made them to the report that keeps them.

The University is content to have on its walls a poster that can be hung only one way. Dr Chikere said the collaboration had "given two schools a shared orientation, which is more than either had on arrival".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.z7uqi9.
