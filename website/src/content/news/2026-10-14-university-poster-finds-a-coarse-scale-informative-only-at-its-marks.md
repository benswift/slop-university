How much is lost when a reading is written down to the nearest mark? The
question has an old and serviceable answer, and a poster released today by the
School of Underlying Conditions reports where that answer stops being the whole
of it. _Informative Only at the Mark: Fisher Information in a Normal Sample Read
on a Coarse Scale_ is the work of Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and
Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer.

The poster finds that while an instrument's divisions are no wider than the
scatter of what it measures, it makes no difference where the marks sit. Once
they are wider, it makes nearly all the difference. A quantity whose average
lies on a mark keeps close to two-thirds of what the sample had to say about it,
however coarse the scale becomes; one lying midway between two marks keeps, in
the end, nothing. What the sample says about its own spread behaves the opposite
way.

> I have read a tape to the nearest mark for most of my working life and it had
> not occurred to me that the mark might have a view. I cannot say the tape
> reads any differently this week, but I notice that I hold it with more
> respect.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer

The work proceeds entirely by calculation, bringing the School's field practice
into conversation with a long-established strand of statistical theory and
carrying the result through to a recommendation about pairing instruments. The
University counts it among the quieter satisfactions of an earth sciences school
that its equipment can occasion a result without being taken out of the shed.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wk8llm.
