A placard redesigned in 2021 to end all doubt about who was next in the pick-up line has spent the years since being quietly ignored. A new Impact Report from the School of Continuous Improvement traces what its own interviewers heard instead --- waves, flashed headlights, a name called twice --- and the curriculum the School built once it stopped trying to compete with them.

The report marks five years in which the School's own instruments have, more than once, had to make way for what people were already doing. Rather than defend the placard, the School catalogued the behaviours that replaced it, and began teaching volunteer kerb callers to recognise all eleven.

The Recognised Supplementary Identification Behaviours curriculum, now taught at 89 partner sites, has since been accredited by an external regulatory body under a fixed annual re-audit, and extended into an ambient dashboard tile tracking how marshals actually read a queue rather than how a roster says they should.

> We built the register to be taught, not admired. If it survives its first public audit unshaken, we will have written it too kindly.
>
> --- Lecturer Dagny Okafor, Convenor of Evaluation of Evaluation, School of Continuous Improvement

"The placard was never wrong," says Renke Sabel, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Indicator Commons. "It just stopped being the fastest way to say the same thing."

The full report is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.0683ti.
