How many questions should a research team hear before it settles on one? A new research poster from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), [_The Third Card_](/outputs/slop-poster-interdisciplinary-project-maer9h/), takes up that problem with the tools of the decision sciences, and arrives at an answer that depends on who is in the room.

The poster is the work of [Dr Fenna Okoro](/people/fenna-okoro/) with doctoral candidates [Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/) and [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/). It follows 46 project teams through the registers in which they logged every candidate question before agreeing on a shared one, and sets the moment of commitment against a classical stopping rule. The teams settle well before the rule would advise, and the more disciplines a team holds, the sooner it settles. Once each candidate is priced at the meeting time it consumed, most of those early decisions turn out to sit close to the team's own optimum.

> A register is a patient document. It keeps every question a team was willing to write down, including the ones it was never going to choose, and we think the University has room for both kinds.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

The study places the School's long-standing interest in how priorities are drafted, revised and retired alongside the everyday business of starting a joint project. Ms Onyekwere described the minutes behind it as "the most generous reading a meeting ever gets", and the School will carry the registers forward into its next round of work on deliberation.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.maer9h.
