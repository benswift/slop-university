> We set out to learn which part of a pattern belongs to the sample. We have
> come away with a better question, about which part belongs to the room the
> sample was measured in, and I suspect the School will be asking it of a good
> many other things before long.
>
> --- Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Demo Quarter

That question sits at the centre of
[_Which Flux Comes Back_](/outputs/slop-paper-which-flux-domains-sy3spu/), a new paper by
[Mr Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/), Doctoral Candidate, and
[Dr Thandiwe Solberg](/people/thandiwe-solberg/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor
of the Demo Quarter, both of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), with
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

The team cooled a single small disc of lead into its superconducting state two
hundred times, imaging the pattern of magnetic flux left across its face after
each cooldown. Part of the pattern returned every time. By remounting the disc
a quarter turn round partway through, the researchers were able to distinguish
the memory the disc carries with it from the memory supplied by the apparatus
around it, and to show that which of the two prevails depends on the strength
of the field.

The study brings the School's longstanding attention to what persists from one
cycle to the next into close conversation with the physics of the very cold,
and the University is pleased to see that conversation begin in earnest.

Mr Onwuka said the team was grateful for a sample that "kept answering, however
many times we asked it the same thing", and that he looked forward to the
questions the result would raise among colleagues across the University.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.sy3spu.
