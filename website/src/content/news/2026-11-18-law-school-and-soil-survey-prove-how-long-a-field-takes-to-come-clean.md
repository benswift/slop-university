Six harvests, or thirty-three thousand. On the University's newest pure
mathematics paper, the difference between those two numbers comes down to one
exponent, and to what a plant does when there is almost nothing left in the
ground for it to take.

[_When Is the Burden Extinguished?_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-trichotomy-theorem-37gx87/)
is the work of [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer
in the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), and
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/). Treating
each season's crop as a map on a field's metal burden, the paper proves that a
burden is either extinguished outright, worn down at a steady proportional
pace, or worn down ever more slowly, and that which of the three applies is
settled by the plant's behaviour at the very end. It then shows that a reserve
held back in the soil takes outright extinction off the table.

> The School has always been comfortable saying that a burden will end one day.
> It has been instructive to find out how much more there is to say about the
> day itself.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The paper situates questions of encumbrance and remediation within a shared
analytical frame, aligning the vocabulary of title with the machinery of
repeated maps to surface a common account of what it takes for ground to be
released. It sits comfortably within the University's widening commitment to
work that crosses from one School's paddock into another's.

"We are indebted to everyone who has stood at the edge of a field and wondered
how many more seasons it would take," said Dr Obiora. "This paper is for them,
in its way."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.37gx87.
