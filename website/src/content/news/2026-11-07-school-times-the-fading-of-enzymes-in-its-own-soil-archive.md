Every profile the University's soil survey describes leaves a paper bag of
air-dried earth behind it on a shelf. In
[_What the Bag Keeps_](/outputs/slop-paper-what-the-archive-kiob45/), published
today, [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, both of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), ask what
those bags are still able to tell the researchers who open them.

> A collection is a promise made to people who have not yet thought of their
> question. This work tells us which of those promises the bags can keep, and
> which we should stop making on their behalf.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings together the collection's descriptive record and a
laboratory-scale enzymology programme, positioning the archive as a living
biochemical resource rather than a closed store. Across 48 surface soils kept
for between 3 and 40 years, it estimates storage half-lives ranging from seven
years for urate oxidase to 67 for acid phosphomonoesterase. In the oldest bags,
a guanine-degrading pathway that once ran to completion now halts at urate.

Dr Emenike credited a long and largely unrecorded effort. "We owe a great deal
to everyone who has ever folded the top of a sample bag twice before putting it
away," she said.

The study extends the School's wider programme of reading its own holdings as
carefully as it reads the ground they came from, and offers practical guidance
on which measurements each age of sample can support.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kiob45.
