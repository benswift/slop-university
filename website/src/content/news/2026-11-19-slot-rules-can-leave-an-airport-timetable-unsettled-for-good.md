An airport timetable can be settled the way a point of law is settled, and under the right conditions it can also stay open indefinitely. That is the double finding of [_Grandfather Rights as Precedent_](/outputs/slop-poster-grandfather-rights-as-3uguku/), a new research poster from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) and [Tove Ilesanmi](/people/tove-ilesanmi/).

The team treats the rule that lets a carrier keep a well-used slot series from one season to the next as a kind of precedent, and asks how many seasons pass before nobody has a reason to reopen the allocation. Where carriers hold on to every series they can use, the answer is bounded, and in 10,000 simulated instances no allocation took longer than eleven seasons. Once a carrier may move a series to a neighbouring window, some allocations never close at all.

> We went looking for how long an airport takes to stop arguing and found out a good deal about how long we take. The School is at its most comfortable with questions that have closed, and it was instructive to spend a season with one that may not.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer and Lead, Received Readings Group

In formal terms, the poster brings fixed-point theory and computational complexity to the stewardship of scarce shared infrastructure, placing scheduling practice, allocative continuity and institutional memory within a single analytical frame.

For a School devoted to the question of when a matter may be treated as closed, the work extends a familiar inquiry into new and considerably busier territory. Mr Ilesanmi thanked the colleagues across the University who read early drafts of the proofs "in the spirit in which they were offered, which was provisional".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3uguku.
