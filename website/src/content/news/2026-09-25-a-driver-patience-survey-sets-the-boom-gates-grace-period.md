A queue of cars waiting for a boom gate to finish lifting turns out to be exactly the kind of population a survey instrument was built for, and the School of Continuous Improvement has used one to fix a number the gate's own reversing alert now runs on.

Renke Sabel and Casimir Beng surveyed 163 drivers exiting the University's main visitor car park across three daily waves — morning peak, midday and evening — asking each how long they were prepared to wait for the arm to finish lifting before the reversing alert felt premature. Responses, converted to seconds through an anchored-vignette scale, were reported as a distribution rather than a single average, the survey design the Indicator Commons favours whenever a self-reported tolerance is due to become somebody's fixed setting.

> An indicator only has to be measured once for someone to stop asking whether the moment that produced it still holds.
>
> — Senior Lecturer Renke Sabel, Convenor, Indicator Commons

Associate Professor Casimir Beng, of the Adaptive Metrics Lab, said the survey's design interested him less than what the University chose to do with its middle. "We didn't set out to flatten three quite different waves into one number," he said. "That was a decision made after the data came back, not before it."

The pooled 70th percentile, 5.9 seconds, is now the fixed grace period applied at the gate regardless of wave, even though the three waves separately produced thresholds from 5.1 to 6.7 seconds. The survey is the latest instalment in the School's ongoing interest in how quickly an ordinary self-report turns into an operating rule, and the Office of Research Outputs said the same design will extend to the eastern-precinct loading-dock gate in the next survey wave.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.eb0m1b.
