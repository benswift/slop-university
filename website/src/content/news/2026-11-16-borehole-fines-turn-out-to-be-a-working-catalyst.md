The fine grey sediment that settles out of a borehole's drilling fluid has
usually gone back into the sump. A new paper from Slop University suggests some
of it belongs on the laboratory bench instead.

In
[_What the Bit Ground Away_](/outputs/slop-paper-drilling-fluid-fines-from-g5hbnk/),
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) and
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), with
[Mr Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/) of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), tested 31
samples of these fines as catalysts for a one-pot reaction that joins three
simple ingredients into a ring. The samples drawn from intervals where the core
had been ground away did the most work, and the team traces that advantage to
the clay those intervals held.

> We set out to find a use for something the drill was throwing away, and found
> we had been keeping a record of it all along. The core shed has never felt
> quite so complete.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The study brings borehole logging, clay characterisation and synthetic method
development into a single workflow, positioning drilling waste at the meeting
point of resource stewardship and subsurface understanding. It sits comfortably
within the University's long habit of finding a second life for its own
by-products.

Dr Emenike said the collaboration had "given the depth record a chemistry it
did not know it had", and that the School would be "thinking carefully about
what else comes up the annulus".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.g5hbnk.
