Not every finding survives a second look, and the School of Continuous
Improvement is candid about which one of its own just didn't. The
instrument's first outing, published earlier this year, found a school
travel-pass scheme's straight-line eligibility radius diverging from the
route a child actually walks in a specific, patterned way --- the gap
widening with each rail line or waterway a route had to get around. A
pre-registered replication released today, testing the same pattern against
a state transport subsidy scheme's straight-line catchment rule, finds no
such widening at all.

The University counts the null result among its more useful ones: proof, its
media office says, that an instrument earning trust on one network can't be
assumed to travel with it to the next.

The team repeated the original design as closely as the new setting allowed
--- the same straight-line-versus-route comparison, the same
severance-feature grouping, run this time across close to two thousand
claims filed with the Protocol over fourteen months. Where the original
corridor's detour index climbed step by step with each barrier a route
crossed, the replication's held flat throughout, a pattern the team
attributes to a road network that is already indirect long before any single
crossing enters into it.

> A tolerance that was never re-estimated for this network was never really
> a tolerance for this network. It was a number that happened to travel
> well.
>
> --- Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation

"The Protocol's own review flagged the mismatch in June," said Dr Sten
Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas.
"What happens to a finding after that point is really the more interesting
question."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6wq9jx.
