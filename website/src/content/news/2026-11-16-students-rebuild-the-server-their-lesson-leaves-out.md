First-year students asked to play the parts of a peer-to-peer network learn
quickly that an update can cross a room without anyone in charge of it, and
then appoint someone to be in charge of it anyway. That is the central finding
of
[_Somebody Has to Know We're Done_](/outputs/slop-paper-where-first-year-students-shi40j/),
a new paper from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/)
by [Mr Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/),
[Dr Lindiwe Achterberg](/people/lindiwe-achterberg/) and
[Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist](/people/kwame-lindqvist/).

The team followed sixteen tutorials across two iterations of a card-based
classroom activity, bringing video observation, exchange records and
stimulated-recall interviews into a single design-led account of how learners
inhabit a decentralised system. Students kept tallies, drew up registries and
asked their tutors whether the room was finished, and when the second
iteration took away the whiteboard, the same habits gathered at the end of the
activity instead.

> I spent seven seasons as the person who could say when registrations had
> closed. It was strange, and rather moving, to watch a room of eighteen-year-olds
> decide within four minutes that they needed one of those too.
>
> --- Mr Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper sits within the School's continuing attention to the moments when a
group decides what it needs to know, and who should know it.

Associate Professor Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority Studies, said
the activity would run again next year, and that he "fully expects the students
to find somewhere new to put the server, which is exactly what a good tutorial
should leave room for."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.shi40j.
