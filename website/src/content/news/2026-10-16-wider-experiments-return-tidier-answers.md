The School of Underlying Conditions has always been interested in what sits beneath a result, and its latest paper takes that interest into the laboratory, where an industrial enzyme is coaxed towards its best yield one setting at a time. _The Tidier Optimum: Design Width and Centre-Anchoring in Sequential Response-Surface Optimisation of Tannase Production_, by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds, asks whether an optimum found at the middle of an experiment was discovered there or placed there.

Across 48,000 simulated optimisations, the authors find that the screening stage usually leaves the experiment in a reasonable place, and that it is the breadth of the follow-up design that matters most. Narrow designs followed the true peak; wider designs returned cleaner-looking maxima that stayed closer to where they started. The paper closes with a simple test that experimenters can report beside any optimum they publish.

> An optimiser who arrives somewhere comfortable ought to ask how much of the comfort was brought along. I am delighted that the question now has a School-wide home, and curious to see who takes it up next.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

The paper extends the University's methodological programme on the conditions under which a finding is produced, a body of work it regards as among its most quietly consequential. Dr Emenike described the study as "the sort of question a referee asks in the margin, given the room it deserves."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.eiiz8b.
