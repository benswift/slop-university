Where one fault ends against another in a computer model of the ground, the
offsets on either side have to add up. A new paper from two of the University's
schools shows why, sets out what a model pays when they do not, and offers a
test that can be run on a fault table before any model is built.

[_Faults as Dislocations_](/outputs/slop-paper-faults-as-dislocations-gqz2c2/)
is by [Dr Marit Osayande](/people/marit-osayande/), Lecturer and Convenor of the
Living Dashboard in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), and
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> The School spends much of its time with systems that go on running after the
> people who built them have moved on. A geological model turns out to be one
> of those, and it has been quietly keeping its own accounts the whole time.
>
> --- Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard

The paper brings the vocabulary of condensed-matter physics to bear on everyday
subsurface modelling practice, treating the layers of a model as an ordered
medium and its faults as the places where that order gives way. From that
starting point it develops a conservation framework for the model's fault
network, a halo signature that flags an imbalance, and a checking routine that
can travel with a model across every re-run as new boreholes arrive.

For the University, the collaboration is a welcome instance of two schools
discovering that they had been working on the same line from opposite sides.
Dr Obiora said the School of Underlying Conditions was "glad to see its
boundaries examined from a direction it had not thought to look", and that the
paper would be read closely by those who draw them.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gqz2c2.
