Between the step that captures carbon dioxide and the step that turns it into methane, a well-run reactor pauses to clear its lines. A new research poster asks how long that pause ought to be, and arrives at an answer measured in seconds.

[_How Long to Purge_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-long-to-pkqlgj/) is by [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group, and [Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/), Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab. Working across fourteen campaigns on a bench-scale rig, the team brought capture, sweep and conversion into a single carbon account, so that every portion of the captured gas could be followed to the end of its journey. A sweep of around thirty seconds kept the most methane; the eight-minute sweep the rig had carried over from earlier work sent more than a fifth of the captured carbon to the vent.

> Every rig in the University runs on settings that someone chose for good reasons on a different day. We are grateful to the colleagues who keep those reasons in the logbook, and glad to have added a few lines of our own.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group, School of Underlying Conditions

For Associate Professor Beng, whose lab sequenced the valve timings, the collaboration was a natural meeting of process and practice. "A sequencer does whatever it was last told," he said. "It has been a pleasure to tell it something new."

The poster joins a widening seam of process research in the School of Underlying Conditions, and the University regards it as a steady contribution to its carbon dioxide programme.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pkqlgj.
