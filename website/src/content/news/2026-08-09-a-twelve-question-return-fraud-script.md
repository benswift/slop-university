Forty-six stores, one fixed twelve-question script, and fourteen weeks of
matched refund decisions are behind the School of Continuous Improvement's
newest research poster, and the finding undercuts the assumption behind most
of them: a script standardises what a desk asks, not what fraud gives away.

> A script tells the desk what to ask. It has nothing to say about what a
> scripted answer is worth once fraud has already decided not to give one.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School
> of Continuous Improvement

Dr Solveig Adeyemi and Dr Ntuli held each store's protocol, scripted or
unscripted, fixed for the full trial window, matching confirmed-fraud
outcomes against loyalty-linked purchase history rather than staff say-so,
and timing every interview from the fraud-risk flag's first alert to the
refund decision. Across the fourteen weeks and seven store-format clusters,
the scripted and unscripted protocols caught fraud at statistically
indistinguishable rates, while the scripted desks ran a longer queue in most
of the formats measured.

"The desk was never short of confidence in the flag," said Dr Adeyemi.
"What it lacked was any evidence a script does anything the flag doesn't
already do on its own."

Whether the twelve questions survive past the trial is now a rostering
decision, not a research one; the School has flagged the queue-time cost for
whoever makes it.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.vza4af.
