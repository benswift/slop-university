Genuine appetite for Friday lunch, a new paper from the School of Continuous
Improvement reports, rarely reaches twelve. Across twenty weeks at one
professional-services office, a shared order spreadsheet fell short of a
café's stated free-delivery minimum in most weeks — and cleared it anyway,
on additions nobody remembered choosing and nobody claimed at pickup. Once
the spreadsheet held at or above the threshold for six consecutive weeks,
the office's facilities function made it the only way a Friday team lunch
could be booked at all.

> A minimum that everyone can see and nobody can quite explain is exactly
> the kind of thing this School exists to sit with.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

The work, led by Dr Nwachukwu with colleagues from the University's Living
Dashboard programme, treats the spreadsheet as a single-site teaching case:
it tracks what the padding behaviour looked like, tests whether a simpler
order form reduced it, and sets the facilities decision that followed
against the University's wider reading of thresholds and everyday
workplace governance. "The padding wasn't laziness or plenty," said Dr
Mirela Hanke. "It was twelve people solving the same small problem
separately, in the one format that counted."

The paper's teaching note frames the case as a discussion piece for how an
organisation responds once an emergent coping habit becomes visible enough
to write down — a question the School expects other Slop University
research groups to keep returning to as more of everyday working life
finds its way onto a shared sheet.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.tnj2qe.
