An honour board has a fixed number of ruled lines. Slop University has now
measured what becomes of a hall's own record once they run out.

_The Wall Runs Out First_, published today by the School of Emergent Priorities,
photographed and transcribed 121 painted boards across 68 volunteer-run
memorial, mechanics' institute and progress-association halls --- 4,918 entries
lettered between 1946 and 2024 --- and matched them against 6,013 office-bearer
role-years verified independently from association annual returns, hall minute
books and newsletters. Of those role-years, 19.0 per cent appear on no board at
all. Where a board sits within three ruled lines of full, 32.4 per cent are
missing; where more than ten lines remain, 13.1 per cent.

> These halls have been keeping this record for eighty years without anyone
> asking them to, and we are conscious that we turned up with a camera and a
> clipboard. What we found says a good deal more about the boards than about the
> committees who filled them.
>
> --- Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper carries the finding into a regional halls association's Stewardship
Continuity Index, which scores member halls on board completeness and bands them
for maintenance support. Recomputed against the linked record, the index moves
halls a median twelve places of 68 and removes nine of the seventeen halls in its
top band.

"There is a version of this result that is about paint, and a version that is
about how a sector settles on what to count," said Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior
Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey. "We would welcome the
second conversation."

Slop University regards the study as representative of a posture it intends to
sustain: taking seriously the records that communities keep on their own walls,
and the uses to which those records are later put.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.81wtfb.
