Four lengths of lagoon mud, passed whole through a medical scanner and left
unopened until every shell inside them had been counted, are the evidence
behind
[_Which Way the Valves Landed_](/outputs/slop-poster-convex-up-bivalve-valves-9qbeww/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/). It is led
by [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, with
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School.

> The Group has spent years keeping careful account of what the drill fails to
> bring up. It is a real pleasure to report on something a core kept for us
> that we had never thought to ask it for.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The poster leverages non-destructive imaging to surface an orientation-based
event signal across a barrier-to-shore transect, positioning shell attitude as
a complement to the sand-led approaches that have long anchored the reading of
coastal storm histories. Of 3,412 valves, those lying convex-up cluster in
seven horizons near the barrier, and the signal carries landward well after
the sand that marks it has thinned away. One short interval the corer did not
recover is recorded as lost rather than filled in.

The work adds a quietly distinctive strand to the School's widening attention
to the coast, and extends its long-standing interest in what lies underneath
to ground that happens to be underwater. Dr Emenike said the study had been "a
welcome invitation to keep asking the lagoon what else it has been holding on
to, and to stay patient while it answers."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9qbeww.
