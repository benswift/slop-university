The School of Emergent Priorities has long treated an ordinary rule as a
statement about the future it expects. Its newest paper,
[_What the Carpet Was Expected to Survive_](/outputs/slop-paper-fair-wear-and-7z6lm3/),
carries that habit into the end-of-tenancy inspection, and into the materials
engineering of the rented home.

Doctoral candidate Ylva Ogunbanjo, writing with Edda Nwafor of the School of
Continuous Improvement, codes 1,186 item claims from one residential tenancy
tribunal's written reasons as specimens, each with an installed age, a
description and an outcome. Their reading positions fair wear and tear as an
implied damage-tolerance criterion, one that separates bounded defects from
distributed degradation with real consistency, and that meets its limit at the
swollen edge of a laminate sink cut-out.

> Every condition report I ever filled in was a small theory of how a house gets
> older. It is a pleasure to see a few thousand of them finally read together.
>
> --- Ylva Ogunbanjo, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Ms Nwafor said the collaboration had given her school "a new respect for the
kitchen bench as a place where institutions reason out loud, and a great many
questions we are glad to be left holding."

The paper adds to the University's widening practice of reading everyday
administration with the patience a laboratory brings to its samples, across
schools and across the materials of daily life.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7z6lm3.
