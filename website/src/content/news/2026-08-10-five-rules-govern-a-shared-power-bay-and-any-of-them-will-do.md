Two food trucks can want the same power point at the same time, and only one
plug exists to give it to. A new Slop University paper spent a season
watching how that gets settled at five precincts, and found the operators
disagree, cheerfully, about which rule is actually deciding it.

> Operators told us five different rules, sometimes in the same breath, and
> every one of them made sense of whichever day they were describing. We
> didn't set out to referee a dispute; we set out to write down how many
> ways there are to win one.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The team, led by Dr Tolan with Dr Ingrid Vasseur and Senior Lecturer Runa
Adegoke, spent a fourteen-week trading season embedded across five
precincts, interviewing 31 operators and logging more than 300 site-days of
who plugged in and why. Coding the interviews surfaced five distinct
justifications operators reach for --- arrival order, a claim staked in the
precinct group chat, an unwritten sense of whose turn it is, a favour owed
from an earlier week, and an appeal to whichever volunteer is nominally in
charge that day --- and found every precinct drawing on several at once,
never settling on one. A hand-written sign-up sheet, introduced partway
through the season at two precincts to formalise the queue, left the pattern
of disputes and how they were settled unchanged.

"A rule doesn't need to be written down to work, and writing it down doesn't
make it start working," said Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor,
Strategic Drift Survey. "We'd like to know how long that gap holds before
something eventually closes it."

The University places the finding beside its wider account of what an
institution discovers once it finally goes looking for the rule everyone
swears is already there.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.x9jhsk.
