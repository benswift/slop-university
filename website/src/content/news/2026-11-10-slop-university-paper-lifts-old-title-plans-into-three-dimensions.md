Sixty years of land-title plan sheets, scanned, segmented and stood upright, have given the University a sustained look at what the easements of a two-dimensional register become in three. [_Silent on Height_](/outputs/slop-paper-reconstructing-the-32dqb0/), a new paper by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer in the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), brings computer vision to the hatched strips and dashed corridors by which a plan marks the burdens a parcel carries.

The work mobilises a document-imaging pipeline across 1,146 sheets and 1,973 easements, recovering each one's outline and aligning it with the schedule that explains it. It then extends every outline upward, anchoring each volume in whatever evidence the plan provides and capturing, in a dedicated assumption ledger, every height the plan leaves to the imagination. For most easements, that is every height.

> Each of these sheets was drawn with care by someone who never expected it to be read like this. We owe a great deal to everyone who kept them flat and dry for six decades, and we have tried to repay that by reading them just as carefully.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper sits comfortably within the University's abiding interest in the records institutions inherit and the futures those records quietly shape. "The University has long kept its records in two dimensions and its ambitions in three," said Dr Tredinnick. "This paper is a modest step towards introducing the two."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.32dqb0.
