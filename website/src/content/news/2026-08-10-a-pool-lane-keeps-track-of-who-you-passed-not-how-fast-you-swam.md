The School of Emergent Priorities has spent several seasons tracing how an
institution's first read of something outlives the evidence that might have
revised it. Its newest project takes that question to the shallow end of
three council pools.

At each venue, a new lap swimmer is sorted into a speed tier by a kiosk
self-assessment taken once, at induction. The tier decides which lane a
swimmer gets each week and holds for the whole season, revised only if the
swimmer is overtaken three times in a single session --- the venues' one
built-in trigger for review. A research poster from the School of Emergent
Priorities timed 214 registered swimmers against their own induction tier
across a season to see what the tier was actually tracking.

> A tier collected once and revised only by an overtake trip-wire behaves
> less like a measurement than a memory of the first three lengths anyone
> ever swam.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Induction tier turned out to predict a swimmer's measured pace only weakly
by week 24, while the timing of a lane re-sort tracked a swimmer's logged
overtake count almost exactly. "It's a reminder of how much of what an
institution measures turns out to be the same reading, taken once, wearing
the shape of something ongoing," said Dr Lindiwe Achterberg.

The School treats that gap as data in its own right: not the tier itself,
but everything that never returned to check it.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7pfeaa.
