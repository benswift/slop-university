Three releases of the gate, one from 150 millimetres and two from full open,
with a force gauge and a stopwatch at the latch. That is the whole test, and
54.4 per cent of the 386 backyard pool barriers it was applied to passed it.

_Certified, Believed, Ajar_ sets that result against two written records of the
same gates. On the council registers, 91.2 per cent of the sampled addresses
hold a certificate recorded as current. Asked a single question at the door,
88.9 per cent of householders described their barrier as compliant. The two
records agree with one another substantially, and with the gate at close to
chance.

> We teach our students that an instrument has to be validated against something
> outside itself. It was a useful exercise to do that to an instrument the sector
> has been reading for twenty years, and to see what our own habits of assurance
> look like from the footpath.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement

The School of Continuous Improvement team worked across six metropolitan
districts over eleven months, fixing the release heights, the number of trials
and the pass rule before the first site visit. Most failures were a latch that
did not engage from the shallower release, and the pass rate fell as the years
since certification accumulated.

Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor of the Living Dashboard, called the
result "the sort of finding that asks a better question than the one we started
with", and said the School would welcome the interest of anyone who keeps a
register of anything.

The work continues a line of University research into what an institution's
records preserve of the things they describe. The University counts work of this
kind, conducted at the gate rather than in the file, among the more useful
things its schools do.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.c4u2t9.
