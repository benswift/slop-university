A face reaches the laminated board beside a bakery counter because a box on an
onboarding form was left blank. _Blank Means Yes_, published today by the School
of Continuous Improvement, follows the boxes that were not left blank.

The team assembled every media-consent record held by one franchised bakery
network across eleven annual review cycles: 214 sites, 9,418 staff records,
41,206 record-years. Treating a lodged refusal as a time-to-event problem, with
separation from the network as the competing risk, it finds that 54.1 per cent
of refusals read as consent by the third review, and that among the records
which convert the median is 1.4 cycles. Movement in the other direction is
recorded zero times.

> The question we would like read off this is not what one network ought to do.
> It is how many other preferences are being renewed on somebody's behalf, once
> a year, by nobody.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway Atlas

The paper pairs the record analysis with a reading of the eleven successive
versions of the form itself. Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of
the Horizon Register, described that half of the work as "the part that took
longest and settled it fastest", noting that a field for withdrawing a recorded
consent "existed, and then a redesign tidied it away, and nothing since has
missed it".

Over the same eleven cycles the network's reported consent-currency measure rose
from 71.3 to 99.1 per cent while the share of staff who had signed anything
within the current cycle fell to 28.6. The work was supported under the
Indicator Stewardship Seed Fund and the Anticipatory Capability Fund.

The University welcomes the paper as its measurement agenda reaching the
ordinary paperwork of employment, where a preference is recorded once and seldom
asked again. The full paper is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.68wiyy.
