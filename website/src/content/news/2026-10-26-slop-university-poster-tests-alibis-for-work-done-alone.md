Ask a person where they were on a weekday morning and the answer usually arrives with somebody else in it. For anyone whose work is done alone in a paddock, it rarely can.

[_Nobody Saw Me Do It_](/outputs/slop-poster-when-nobody-saw-pahe80/), a new research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions, takes up that problem from the side of forensic psychology. Its authors are [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, with [Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of Emergent Priorities, and [Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of Continuous Improvement.

The team invited 96 volunteers either to walk a groundwater dip round alone or to study its route and claim they had, then examined the accounts each group gave at interview. Situating established verifiable-detail practice within the lived texture of solitary fieldwork, the poster identifies a class of detail it calls action traces (the gate latched from the far side, the cap left finger-tight) as the point at which truthful and fabricated accounts part company.

> A paddock is a quiet place to have been, and I have been in a good many of them. It has been a pleasure to discover that a quiet place still has a great deal to say for itself, if we take the trouble to ask it properly.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Ms Ogunbanjo thanked "everyone who gave up a morning to walk the round, or to imagine it on our behalf", adding that the team had "learnt as much from the volunteers' patience as from their transcripts."

The work stands as a fresh instance of the University's confidence that a question met in one school's fieldwork can be carried, in good faith, into another discipline's laboratory.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pahe80.
