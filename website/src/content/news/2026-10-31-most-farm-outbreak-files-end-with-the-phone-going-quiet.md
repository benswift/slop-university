When does an outbreak on a farm actually end? According to a new paper from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), the files kept
by one mixed-animal veterinary practice rarely say. Of 212 files opened for
respiratory disease in cattle, sheep and goats, nearly two in three were never
closed at all. They simply stopped, often on a telephone note that the mob was
going better.

[_Closed by Going Quiet_](/outputs/slop-paper-closed-by-going-iad216/) is by
[Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of
Emergent Priorities, with [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/),
and [Dr Solveig Adeyemi](/people/solveig-adeyemi/), Lecturer and Deputy
Convenor of the Review Cadence Observatory in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

> The School has always been most at ease with the things it has formally
> concluded. A year among files that simply stopped has been a gentle lesson in
> how much of our own work ends the same way.
>
> --- Ylva Onyekwere, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The team brought an archive of donated case files into conversation with the
established tools of outbreak epidemiology, aligning each file's closing moment
with a transmission-informed estimate of whether the outbreak was likely to be
over, and situating the practice's own records within a wider understanding of
how surveillance depends on someone choosing to call.

Dr Obiora described the collaboration as "a natural next step on a path the
University has been walking for some time, from the paddock to the record and
back again."

The paper sits within the University's widening commitment to the records that
working life leaves behind, and to the people who keep them.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.iad216.
