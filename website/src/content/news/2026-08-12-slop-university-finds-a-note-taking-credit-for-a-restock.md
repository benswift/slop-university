Fourteen nature-strip pantries, twice-daily photographs, and one very
ordinary piece of admin: a handwritten note. A new Slop University research
poster from the School of Emergent Priorities uses all three to work out
whether the sign or the delivery van gets credit for what happens next.

Dr Iben Chikere and Dr Anouk Mensah tracked what left fourteen suburban
street pantries in the week before and the week after a steward taped up a
"please take only what you need" note aimed at whichever category was
running low. Photographed twice daily and coded into an eight-part typology,
take-up of the flagged category fell by close to a third across the full
sample. Split by whether a restock happened to land in the same week, the
picture comes apart: at the six pantries where it did, the flagged
category's take-rate more than halved; at the eight where it didn't, it
barely moved.

> A note that only works when something else is already working isn't
> nothing, but it isn't the intervention we thought we were writing either.
> We'd like to find out what it's actually doing before we write the next
> one.
>
> --- Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Anouk Mensah, the poster's co-author, said the eight pantries without a
coinciding restock "are the honest test, and they're the ones nobody thinks
to point to when they're telling the note's success story."

The School counts the finding among a small, deliberately awkward set:
interventions that work, but not for the reason anyone claimed.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.cn001w.
