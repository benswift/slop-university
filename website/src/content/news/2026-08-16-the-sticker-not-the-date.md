Two hundred and fourteen people at a service-station forecourt were each asked,
eighteen times over, which of two gas cylinders they would take out of the cage.
_Towards a Cage-Loading Standard_, released today by the School of Emergent
Priorities, turns their answers into a price. A collar sticker still legible
enough to read is worth 41 cents against the swap fee. The certification date
printed on that sticker is worth six.

The University regards the work as characteristic of the School's interest in
how a preference, once measured, becomes an instruction. The exchange network's
revised loading standard now prescribes a presentation order taken from the
three attributes customers priced highest, and audits that order every quarter.

> The interesting part is not what people chose. It is what the choosing was
> then used to arrange.
>
> --- Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor, Futures in Committee

The study pairs a designed choice experiment with a follow-up count of
front-row cage positions across 34 forecourts, bringing stated preference and
physical arrangement onto a single footing. Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral
Fellow, said the exercise had shifted the questions the group asks of its own
instruments: "We are practised at asking whether a measure is sound. This one
asked what a measure gets used for once everybody agrees it is."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
