Eight times over two dry seasons, two researchers from Slop University walked a
circle around each of forty-six cattle troughs, separately and without
conferring, and wrote down what had been left on the ground. The record they
came back with is published today as _Where the Trough Overflows: Illegal
Bird-Trapping Sign at Artificial Water Points in a Semi-Arid Pastoral District_,
a paper by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior
Research Fellow, both of the School of Underlying Conditions.

The paper reports that signs of trapping were found at 14 of the 46 water
points, that five of them accounted for more than half of what was recorded, and
that the strongest association was with a pool of overflow on the ground beside
a trough whose float valve had stopped sealing. Where station lessees replaced
the valve between seasons, the paper reports, most of the signs went with the
pool. The depth of the groundwater beneath each bore, which the authors measured
throughout, was found to have no bearing on the matter.

> The lessees let us follow their bore runs for two dry seasons and never once asked
> what we expected to find. I hope the paper is a fair return on that patience.
> I expect they will let us know.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer

The study connects an established body of thinking about where offences
concentrate with the School's standing field presence in pastoral country,
aligning place-based analysis, repeat observation and routine infrastructure
assessment within a single survey framework. Dr Obiora, who judged the age of
each mark from the soil surface around it, said the work had been "a reminder
that a surface is always in the middle of saying something, whoever happens to
be listening".

The University counts the paper among the returns of a field programme that was
established for one purpose and has been permitted to notice others.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1a55ov.
