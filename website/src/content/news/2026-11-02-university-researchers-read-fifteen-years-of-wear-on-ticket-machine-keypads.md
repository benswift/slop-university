The Living Dashboard has long asked who reads a record once it has been switched on. Its newest paper puts the same question to a sheet of brushed steel.

[_Where the Steel Went_](/outputs/slop-paper-abrasive-wear-on-si85t8/), by [Dr Marit Osayande](/people/marit-osayande/), Lecturer and Convenor of the Living Dashboard, and [Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/), Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor, both of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), with [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the Basement Relations Group in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), examines 31 keypad plates from a fleet of railway ticket machines retired after some fifteen years of service.

> We came to these panels expecting to learn about the machines, and I think the Living Dashboard has learned rather more about itself. That is generally the sign of a good year.
>
> --- Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard

The team brought together laboratory surface measurement, a re-enactment programme on a restored machine and structured conversations with former station and maintenance staff, aligning each strand into a single account of how a public control surface accumulates its history. The Confirm key, as expected, wore most. Close behind it came places where nothing was pressed at all: the edge of the keypad where passengers steadied a hand while reading the screen, a narrow ledge beneath the card reader, and one fixing bolt within reach of a waiting hand.

The work extends the School's commitment to records that keep themselves, and brings that commitment, for the first time, to the scale of a fingertip. Dr Hanke described the paper as "a welcome reminder that a good question can arrive by surplus disposal".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.si85t8.
