Where a sheet hangs on a backyard line has a measured bearing on when it comes
in dry, according to
[_The Last Sheet_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-clothes-line-8zu2c8/), a new paper by
[Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidate, and
[Dr Runa Adegoke](/people/runa-adegoke/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the
Strategic Drift Survey, both of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> My thesis spent two years asking when households peg out. It never got round to asking what the washing on one wire does to the washing on the next, and once the question was on the line it would not come down.
>
> --- Ylva Ogunbanjo, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper reads a loaded clothes line the way an engineer reads a cooling
tower. Across 41 instrumented drying mornings in two suburban gardens, it
marries continuous field measurement with established thermodynamic models of
humid air, and arrives at a unified account of how the air, the fabric and the
order of hanging shape one another. In calm weather, it finds, the last wire
dries at about a third of the pace of the first.

Dr Adegoke described the result as "the kind of finding a School is glad to
keep on file: it alters nothing we had planned, and quietly improves all of
it."

The University welcomes the paper as further evidence that the most ordinary
fixture on a suburban block still repays a patient instrument and an open mind.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8zu2c8.
