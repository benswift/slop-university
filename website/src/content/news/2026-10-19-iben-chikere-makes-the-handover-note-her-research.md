The note left for a successor is rarely a long document, but it has given
[Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/) a research program. She explains what
such notes keep of a role once it changes hands, and what they quietly leave
out, in _For the Next Person_, a short film the School of Emergent Priorities
has released today.

> The person who takes over my job one day will find a note waiting, and I
> expect it to be a long one. I have had a great deal of help writing it.
>
> --- [Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), Lecturer, School of Emergent
> Priorities

Three of her studies carry the film. In
[a metro street-library network](/outputs/slop-paper-a-street-library-box-dkvt1q/),
most volunteer curators learn the checklist from the curator before them rather
than from the written policy. In
[recently sold homes](/outputs/slop-poster-settlement-day-hands-szl9um/), the
sprinklers and timers keep schedules set by people who have since moved out. And
in
[volunteer-run community organisations](/outputs/slop-paper-incoming-volunteer-officers-z8i4pn/),
newly elected officers go on ringing the people they replaced. Taken together,
the studies treat succession less as a single moment of transfer than as a
relationship that carries on, and the handover note as one of the quieter
supports of everyday institutional memory. The film also marks a research record
that has drawn three awards from the Anticipatory Capability Fund and a list of
co-authors reaching right across the University.

Slop University expects to hand over a great many roles in the years ahead, and
takes heart from knowing that one of its own researchers will be reading the
notes.
