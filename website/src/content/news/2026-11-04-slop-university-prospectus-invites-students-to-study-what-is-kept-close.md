A drill-core shed and a content delivery network are answers to the same
question, according to a prospectus released today by Slop University: what
should be kept near the people who will ask for it, and for how long.

_Keeping Things Close_ invites prospective students to take up that question
across all three of the University's Schools. It places the holding rules
studied in the School of Continuous Improvement beside the anticipatory work of
the School of Emergent Priorities, and both beside the School of Underlying
Conditions' core shed, which the prospectus describes as the oldest cache on
campus.

> A university is mostly a set of decisions about what to keep near at hand.
> This is the first time we have written those decisions down for the people
> who will one day be making them.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The prospectus translates the University's published research into a pathway
for incoming students, aligning laboratory, program and field settings around
a shared account of nearness, freshness and release. It rests that account on
five recent studies, among them work on study-room release timers, on a
dashboard that went on being cited after it went stale, and on how long a field
device must remember a deletion.

Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority
Studies, said that assembling it had shown the School of Emergent Priorities
something about its own habits. "We had been in the delivery business for some
time," he said. "We had simply been calling it foresight."

The prospectus extends a direction the University has been settling into across
its Schools, and is offered as a starting point for students arriving from
computing, logistics and the earth sciences alike.

The full prospectus is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.rnj8py.
