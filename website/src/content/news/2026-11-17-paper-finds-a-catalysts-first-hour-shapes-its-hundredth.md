The [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) has made a
habit of asking what is settled before anything officially begins. Its newest
paper takes that question to a laboratory furnace.

In
[_Carbon Laid Down in the First Hour_](/outputs/slop-paper-start-up-transients-and-4mpuoz/),
doctoral candidates [Tove Ilesanmi](/people/tove-ilesanmi/) and
[Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), with
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), prepared
one nickel-cobalt catalyst four ways and followed it through a hundred hours of
turning methane and carbon dioxide into synthesis gas. The faster the catalyst
was heated during its preparation, the more carbon it gathered in its first
hour on stream, and that early carbon was still there, in much the same
proportion, at the end.

> I had always thought of start-up as the part you get through before the
> experiment. On this catalyst it is part of the experiment, and I have found
> that a very good thing to learn.
>
> --- Tove Ilesanmi, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The study positions activation protocol as a first-order variable across the
catalyst lifecycle, bringing reactor practice, bench-level carbon accounting and
long-duration stability testing into a single conversation about readiness.

Associate Professor Uzoma described the collaboration as work in which "the
bench and the corridor turned out to share a fire door, and we have been glad
of it."

The University welcomes the paper as further evidence that some of its most
consequential work takes place in the hours nobody thought to schedule.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4mpuoz.
