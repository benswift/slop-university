The slides had been in their cabinet since the study that made them was written
up, and nobody had asked anything further of them. A poster released today by
the School of Underlying Conditions, _The Lobule Fills from the Outside: A
Point-Counted Time Course of Centrilobular Necrosis and Repair in 144 Archived
Liver Sections_, reports what three earth scientists found when they took the
slides out again and counted them.

> Rock does not get better. It has been a pleasure to spend a season with a
> material that does, and I expect to be thinking about it for some time.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

The work carries a counting discipline long established in the School's
thin-section laboratory across to a neighbouring body of practice, positioning
57,600 grid points along the path from one landmark of the liver's architecture
to another and recording what lay beneath each. In doing so it converts an
existing holding into a moving picture of injury and recovery over seven days,
and locates the cells responsible for the recovery relative to the edge they
were closing.

“Eight readings over a week is a hydrograph, whatever it is a hydrograph of,”
said Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer. Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research
Fellow, is the poster's third author.

The University considers the poster a fair illustration of what becomes
available when its laboratories are left unlocked to one another, and of the
continuing usefulness of material that a concluded programme has already paid
for.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.om2xzt.
