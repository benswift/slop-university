A pole at the roadside has been asked a question first put to a screen, and
has answered it plainly. Slop University's School of Emergent Priorities has
published a paper testing whether a bus-stop flag that stops listing route
numbers and reads MULTIPLE ROUTES changes how often people waiting beneath it
speak to one another. Across 118 stops, it does not.

> The pole did not behave like the group chat, and we have decided to be glad of
> the difference.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

The study sent pairs of trained observers to stops across three municipal
areas, where they tallied waiting passengers and the remarks passed between
strangers over a season of morning peaks. The estimate at the flag's changeover
sat close to zero and within the bound the team fixed before the first
observation. The quietest stops, where the earlier result had looked most
pronounced, behaved no differently from the rest.

On the strength of the finding, the School will set every flag in its advisory
to read MULTIPLE ROUTES, whatever the number of routes a stop serves, with no
local override and a fixed review cycle. The University's earlier group-chat
result stands as it was.

Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, described the kerb as "a setting that
answers what it is asked and asks very little back." The work reflects the
University's continuing interest in the everyday displays people read without
noticing.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ohh1ss.
