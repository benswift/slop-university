The School of Settled Matters' collection of annotated second-reading speeches has yielded a full-length history drawn from the volumes themselves. [_Debated Once_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-work-test-proviso-ovj9ry/), by [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), traces a single condition attached to one colonial parliament's relief law in 1894 and carried, unaltered in substance, until the whole Act was repealed in 1971.

The paper brings a drafter's eye and a historian's patience to nineteen bound volumes of debates, pencilled over by the officers who prepared the bills. Across seven enactments it maps where the house's attention went and where it did not, and surfaces the ordinary procedures that let a contested provision pass from one statute book to the next without a vote: an amendment ruled beyond the scope of its bill, two consolidations certified as making no change in the law, and a repealing schedule.

> Every statute book is also a record of what a parliament decided it need not revisit. It has been a quiet privilege to spend time with that record, and I suspect the School will be returning to it for some years yet.
>
> --- Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld, School of Settled Matters

The study sits squarely within the School's continuing attention to what a decision leaves behind once its makers have moved on, and the University counts it among the clearest expressions yet of that agenda.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ovj9ry.
