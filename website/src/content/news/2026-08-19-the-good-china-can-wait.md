For eighteen months, 341 households kept a diary of every event that might
have justified the good china, then convened to rule on each one. A research
poster from the School of Emergent Priorities, released today, reports the
verdicts: of 5,118 candidate occasions, 4.1 per cent were ruled worthy of the
reserve, and most of those were deferred anyway.

_Counting the Good China_, by Dr Runa Adegoke, Dr Iben Chikere and Dr Lindiwe
Achterberg, inventories 2,904 household effects held for best --- the china,
the guest towels, the scissors nobody may use --- and asks each object's
custodian what occasion would finally qualify. The answer moves: each year an
object goes unused raises its elicited threshold, and the fine china's median
bar now sits above the most formal occasion any household recorded. The work
carries the School's stated-versus-enacted agenda into the china cabinet,
treating the gap between a designation and a dinner as data rather than delay.

> No one we spoke to could say when the china would next be used, and no one
> considered this a problem. That difference of opinion between an object and
> its owners is exactly where the School likes to work.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

The study crosses a full-cupboard inventory with a prospective occasion diary,
consolidating elicited thresholds, minuted rulings and bequest histories into
a single account of the household reserve. A follow-up trial, in which one
logged occasion per household is pre-ruled eligible, is in design.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dy3i86.
