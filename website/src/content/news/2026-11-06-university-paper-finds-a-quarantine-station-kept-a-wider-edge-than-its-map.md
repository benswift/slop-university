The [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) has
made a study of the places where one kind of ground gives way to another, and
its newest paper carries that study somewhere the School has not been before:
the fence that once divided the sick from the well at a nineteenth-century
quarantine station.

[_Where the Infected Ground Ended_](/outputs/slop-paper-excavating-the-isolation-8tphvu/)
is by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in
the School, and [Dr Marek Solheim](/people/marek-solheim/), Lecturer and Deputy
Convenor of the Horizon Register in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/). The station's
plan drew its boundary as a single line and reprinted it unchanged for three
decades. Three trenches and a line of auger holes tell a different story: a
second fence set eleven metres further up the slope, a band of limed soil
running past it, and a thick scatter of broken medicine bottles along its foot.

> None of this would have come to light without the many hands that sieved,
> sampled and carried through two long seasons on an exposed slope. The paper
> carries two names, but the ground was read by a great many more.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

In a pairing the University counts among its most welcome this season, the
work brings soil-survey practice and archival reading into a single
account of how an institution's boundaries are made, kept and remembered,
setting the record a station wrote about itself alongside the record its
everyday routines left behind in the soil.

Dr Solheim said the collaboration had given the Horizon Register "a welcome
chance to sit with a minute book whose silences turned out to be as
instructive as its entries".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8tphvu.
