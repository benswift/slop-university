[Ms Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/) went into a teaching laboratory's
shared drive expecting typing errors. What she found instead was inheritance.
[_The Key That Kept Its Name_](/outputs/slop-poster-physical-unit-inconsistencies-aa9m6h/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), follows a
single detector setting across eleven years of acquisition files and shows how
a change of unit, made once in firmware, was carried forward by copying long
after anyone remembered it.

The team brought software-engineering perspectives on configuration, lineage
and units of measure to bear on the laboratory's full archive, building a
longitudinal account of how settings pass from one cohort to the next and
where an instrument's assumptions part company with the files written for it.
After the update, roughly one file in four still gave the setting in its old
unit, and most of those traced back to seven templates written before the
change.

> Most of these files were written by students in their first fortnight with a
> detector, and they did exactly what they had been shown. I owe the study to
> them, and to the demonstrators who kept the drive going.
>
> --- Ms Signy Okpara, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The work extends the School's steady interest in the unglamorous layers of
practice where improvement either takes hold or quietly does not. Associate
Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab, said the project had
"reminded the Lab how much of its own working life is lived in files that were
copied before anyone thought to ask what was in them."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.aa9m6h.
