A share house's pinned rule is meant to settle an argument before it starts.
A new Slop University study of 48 households asks how long that settlement
actually lasts, and finds most houses quietly move on from the wording long
before anyone thinks to change it.

Interviews with 63 residents found forty-two of forty-eight pinned quiet-hours
rules had drifted from what the household reported actually keeping, by a
median of over an hour, without a single word of the pin itself changing to
match. A follow-up nudge asking a subset of houses to update their wording
left the pattern exactly where it started.

> We went in expecting residents to say they'd forgotten the pin was there.
> Almost none of them had. They could quote it. They just weren't living by
> it, and hadn't worked out whose job it was to fix that.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Mirela Hanke, Deputy Convenor of the School of Continuous Improvement's
Living Dashboard, welcomed the finding as evidence for a pattern her own
program has tracked in indicators with a screen rather than a chat pin: "the
gap doesn't need anyone to be careless. It just needs a wording nobody's
job it is to revisit."

On the strength of the study, the School of Emergent Priorities' Horizon
Register will now require any group chat administered through a
University-affiliated residential, society, or committee channel to reissue
its pinned governance text every planning cycle, countersigned by at least
two channel members, with an unreissued pin auto-expiring to advisory-only
status in the interim.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ekhmep.
