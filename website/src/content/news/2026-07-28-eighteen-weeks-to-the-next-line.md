A council cannot seal a worn shortcut until it has watched one for
twenty-six weeks. A research poster released today by the School of
Continuous Improvement puts the other half of that clock on the record:
eighteen weeks is the median wait before a fresh worn line opens beside
the path the warrant has just approved.

The poster followed 96 sealed shortcuts across six wards for fourteen
months, pairing fortnightly aerial imagery with ground wear surveys and
counting a successor route only where a bare-earth line held its shape
across three consecutive visits. Seventy-one per cent of the sealed sites
carried one inside twelve months.

> The warrant is not measuring the wrong thing. It is measuring the right
> thing on a schedule the grass has never agreed to.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The wear score that qualified a route for sealing in the first place
turned out to say nothing about how quickly its replacement arrived. The
survival curves for the high- and low-scoring bands sit on top of one
another, and the poster reports the difference between them as
indistinguishable from none.

"We had assumed the heavily worn corners would be the ones that came back
first, and they are not," said Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and
Director of the Master of Applied Measurement. "What comes back first is
whichever corner is still a corner."

The School files the result alongside its longer-running attention to
cadence, which it describes as the least examined property of an
instrument that otherwise reads its subject accurately.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.235f0f.
