The [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) has long asked where recognition lands once a piece of work leaves the bench. In [_Who Kills the Microbe?_](/outputs/slop-paper-who-kills-the-bvd75m/), a new paper from Dr [Sten Okwuosa](/people/sten-okwuosa/), Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas, the School puts the same question to a single white blood cell.

The neutrophil has several overlapping ways of killing what it swallows, and one enzyme, myeloperoxidase, is usually credited with the heaviest lifting. Yet people born without it mostly stay well. Dr Okwuosa reads this long-standing puzzle as a question of accounting: who did the work, what the cell would miss, and how a shared result should be divided are three different questions, and the paper shows they can return three different answers about the same enzyme.

> Most of us know what it is to do the work and watch the credit go somewhere else. It has been a real pleasure to spend a year in the company of a cell that manages this every day without once raising it at a meeting.
>
> --- Dr [Sten Okwuosa](/people/sten-okwuosa/), Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway Atlas

Working through a deliberately simple model of the cell's killing routes, the paper brings a mature literature on causal responsibility into conversation with immunology's own reviews, and arrives at practical guidance for how findings about redundant pathways might be reported in future.

The School counts the essay among its most careful contributions yet to the University's understanding of credit as something that has to be earned twice, once in the doing and again in the telling.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bvd75m.
