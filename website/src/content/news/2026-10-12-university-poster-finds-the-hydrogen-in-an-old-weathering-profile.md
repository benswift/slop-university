> There is a good deal of core in this country that was drilled for one reason
> and looked at once. It is a quiet pleasure to take a tray down for a second
> reason and find it had been keeping something back.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Associate Professor and Lead, Basement
> Relations Group

The tray in question holds the place where one borehole passes out of sandstone
and into the much older rock beneath it. _Hydrogen at the Unconformity:
Water–rock reaction of drill core as the energy budget of a buried habitat_ is a
research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions by Associate Professor
Uzoma with Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior
Research Fellow. It asks where, across that single contact, rock and water
together make hydrogen, the fuel long proposed for the earliest life below
ground.

The team took core from twelve intervals, sealed it with water in glass vials,
kept the vials warm for 90 days, and measured what gathered above the water.
The sandstone gave nothing. The fresh basement gave a steady amount. The most
came from between them: the buried weathering profile at the top of the
basement, which at the cooler of two temperatures out-yielded the fresh rock
about four to one.

Dr Obiora, who logged the profile before any vial was opened, said the result
"sits well with a school that has always found the edges of things more
rewarding than their middles".

The University regards the poster as a fair instance of what its newest school
was founded to do, which is to look closely at a small piece of ground and
report what is there.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.aw86e0.
