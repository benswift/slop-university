description: A psychology paper from the School of Underlying Conditions finds that people who lived through three years of communal violence use the period to date their memories while recalling very little from within it.

---

One hundred and twelve residents of a river town, twenty everyday words, and
the years each of them reached for when asked to put a memory in its place:
these were the materials of
[_Dated By, Not Recalled_](/outputs/slop-paper-landmark-without-contents-3loqh3/),
a paper in cognitive psychology by
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/),
Senior Lecturer, both of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

The town went through three years of conflict between its two banks, ending
nearly two decades ago. Its residents still date ordinary events by those
years, placing a wedding or a new job before them or after. Far fewer of the
memories they produced came from inside the period than the surrounding years
would predict. A second study, with pairs of residents remembering the period
together, found that their conversations gathered around its first and last
months, and that what went unsaid alongside those edges was harder to recall
afterwards.

> A boundary can hold a great deal of weight without holding very much. We are
> grateful to the residents who sat with us and gave the time to finding out
> which is which.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

For a School better known for its work on the intervals a drill run fails to
bring up, the study carries a familiar question out of the core shed and into
the ordinary business of remembering together. Dr Emenike described the study as "a patient piece of
listening, and one the School expects to keep returning to."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3loqh3.
