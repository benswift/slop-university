Most of what an institution actually knows about a dispute never makes it
into a formal file. It survives instead in whatever was lying around when
someone decided to write something down.

A new research-showcase brochure, published this week by the Office of
Research Outputs, gathers six studies built around exactly that habit: the
honour board painted onto a hall wall, the incident log kept beside a share
house's robot vacuum, the correspondence thread left for a stranger inside a
street-library box. Drawing on work from across the School of Emergent
Priorities and the School of Continuous Improvement, the brochure follows
each record from the day it starts to the point, sometimes years later,
where the argument it was meant to settle has moved on without it.

Among the studies featured: an analysis of honour boards across dozens of
volunteer-run halls finding that whether a name goes missing tracks the
board's remaining space rather than anything about the person it belonged
to; and an item-response study of clothing size labels finding a label
accounts for barely a third of the variance in how a garment actually fits,
with the retailer it was bought from accounting for nearly as much.

> A record kept to settle an argument later is still a record someone has to
> keep. Ours asks what gets lost in the keeping.
>
> --- Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard, School of Continuous Improvement

"Every one of these records started as a shortcut," said Bram Ntuli,
Lecturer and Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds. "What's interesting is
how quickly the shortcut becomes the only account anyone still trusts."

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dqqt3r.
