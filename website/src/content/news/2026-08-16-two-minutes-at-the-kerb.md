An estate car with an instrument mast on its roof spent six evenings last
December moving at fifteen kilometres an hour along streets that had nominated
themselves. The rig it carried is called LUMEN, and it was built at the School
of Continuous Improvement to put a question to a festive-light display that
nobody had thought to put: what does it read, from the footpath?

Across 34 suburbs the survey reached 212 nominated displays, pairing every
vehicle-mounted estimate with a handheld reading taken at the property boundary
by readers who could not see what the rig had recorded. The two agree closely.
A display that occupies a walk-up team for more than fourteen minutes takes
LUMEN a little over two, and 94 per cent of nominated displays return a usable
profile from the road. Street trees account for most of the rest.

> We have spent a season learning that the measuring was never going to be the
> hard part. What a household puts on its own street is now easier to describe
> than it is to nominate.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

The work sits with the School's longer interest in instruments that arrive
before a use for them has been settled. "The distributions overlap a good deal
more than the categories suggest," said Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and
Convenor of the Review Cadence Observatory. "A class label turns out to be a
thin description of a street."

A second season is planned at the same transect speed. Each display's profile
will be lodged as a standing seasonal record and returned to its householder for
attestation before any listing goes out. The release marks the point at which
the University's measurement agenda arrived at the front fence and asked
permission.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
