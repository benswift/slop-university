By setting the growth rings of a farm windbreak beside the flaring records of
the gas plant it was planted to shelter, two Slop University researchers have
assembled a season-by-season account of how a rural energy facility and its
nearest trees have lived alongside one another for almost three decades.
[_Narrow Rings Downwind_](/outputs/slop-paper-tree-ring-growth-in-2kfkvh/), a
new paper by [Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu](/people/ingeborg-nwachukwu/), Lecturer and
Convenor of Capability Sprints in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), and
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), reports that
the trees nearest the stack registered the gas flared each spring and very
little of the rest.

> Every School finds its way to a question eventually, and ours appears to have
> found this one standing beside a boundary fence. We are delighted that it now
> belongs to the whole University, and to whoever picks it up next.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

The study brings together dendroecological fieldwork, operational record-keeping
and a comparative windbreak design, mobilising more than two decades of
monthly logbook entries to underpin a richer understanding of the relationship
between industrial routine and the landscape that receives it. Its account of
the years following the plant's adoption of flare-gas recovery is likely to
inform ongoing reflection on how environmental performance is understood at the
scale of a single site.

The paper sits comfortably within the University's longstanding readiness to
learn from whatever an institution's neighbours have been quietly recording.
"There will be more springs," Dr Emenike said, "and the School intends to be
there for a good number of them."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.2kfkvh.
