Dr Marit Osayande has built a career out of following what happens to a shared measurement once nobody's job it is to keep reading it. Her latest case study, with Associate Professor Casimir Beng and Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, turns that same attention on an object with no dashboard at all: the one good knife in a four-person share house's kitchen drawer.

Over 77 days the team tracked the knife's whereabouts each morning, drew on a diary the household kept of its own accord, and watched what happened when the house tried, partway through, to fix the problem with a laminated sign-out sheet taped inside the drawer.

> A knife doesn't need an owner until the moment it's missing, and by then the moment for assigning one has usually passed.
>
> — Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard

The sign-out sheet made no measurable difference to how quickly the knife returned to the drawer, a result the team is treating less as a disappointment than as the case's real finding: custody of a shared object settles on whoever is standing closest when it's needed, whatever paperwork sits beside it.

The case now joins the School of Continuous Improvement's Capability Sprints as a required teaching text, and the School is extending the household's own instinct rather than replacing it: the University's residential colleges will pilot a shared-asset attestation log of their own, covering every rotating appliance and tool across student housing for a full teaching period, with no exception carried past the first review.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.y9lntt.
