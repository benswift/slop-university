Before his candidature, [Mr Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/) spent seven
years compiling the weekly results of a cross-country league, and was regularly
asked to leave a name off the bottom of the sheet. His doctoral thesis,
[_Nobody Finishes Last_](/outputs/slop-thesis-nobody-finishes-last-oip71b/),
asks what community sport clubs do about that bottom row, and whether it changes
who comes back. _Nobody Finishes Last, Explained_, a new research explainer from
the School of Continuous Improvement, puts its answers on screen.

> Most of the clubs I visited had settled on an answer to the bottom row of
> their results long before I arrived. The School has now settled on one of its
> own, which seems to me the right order for a university to do these things in.
>
> --- [Mr Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/), Doctoral Candidate, School of
> Continuous Improvement

Four studies supply the evidence: a census of club series, an archive of weekly
timed runs, interviews at the back of the field and a trial in clubs that had
never had an appointed last, the volunteer rostered to finish behind everyone
else. The film draws them together into a sustained account of participation as
a matter of institutional design, with the thesis's own figures carrying the
findings and [Mr Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/)'s voice at either end. It
closes on the Last Place Roster, under which a member of staff will soon be
recorded one place below the last genuine entrant on every ranked list the
School publishes.

That a doctoral finding should be written into the School's own standings before
its examiners have reported is, in the University's view, research culture
working as intended.

The full explainer is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.p99wrv.
