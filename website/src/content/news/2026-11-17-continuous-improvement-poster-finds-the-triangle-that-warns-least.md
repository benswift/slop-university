The [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) has
always been interested in how early a difference makes itself known. Its newest
research poster carries that interest somewhere the School has not often gone:
into classical geometry, and one of its most patiently proved theorems.

[_The Triangle That Gives the Least Warning_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-sharp-quantitative-xnj9fl/)
is led by doctoral candidate [Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/), with
[Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/) and
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/). It takes the
long-established result that a triangle with two equal angle bisectors must be
isosceles, and asks how strongly the bisectors register a difference in the
sides when the triangle is not.

> Every field keeps a few results it trusts without asking how far they reach.
> It has been a privilege to spend part of a candidature on one of them, and I
> am grateful to a School that was willing to treat a triangle as improvement
> work.
>
> --- Signy Okpara, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The team pairs an exact treatment of the near-isosceles case with a
whole-of-shape-space sampling exercise across two million triangles, bringing
symbolic and computational capability into alignment around a single constant.
The tightest case, the poster reports, is not the equilateral triangle that
textbooks reach for first but an isosceles triangle with an apex of roughly
eighty degrees.

Associate Professor Beng described the work as "a reminder that the School's
questions travel well", and said he expected the Adaptive Metrics Lab to "keep
a respectful distance from the triangle while Ms Okpara finishes with it".

The poster takes its place in a growing body of work through which the School
extends its reach beyond the processes it has historically studied.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.xnj9fl.
