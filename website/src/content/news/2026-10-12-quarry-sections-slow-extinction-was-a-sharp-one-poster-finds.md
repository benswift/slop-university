> We picked forty-eight thousand shells in order to learn that we should have
> stood closer together. I am told this is how most fields proceed, and I see no
> reason for ours to be the exception.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The shells in question came out of sixty samples taken down a disused quarry
face, and they are the subject of _Last Seen Below the Ash: Finite-size scaling
of fossil range terminations in one measured mudstone section_, a research
poster by Dr Obiora with Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Associate Professor
and Lead, Basement Relations Group, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer.

Below a single thin ash bed the section's fossil forms appear to drop out one by
one over several metres. The poster aligns that pattern with a result from
theoretical physics about sharp changes in small systems, treating the number of
specimens picked from each sample as the size of the system. As the picking is
thinned and restored, the apparent extinction slides toward the ash in an
orderly way, and at unlimited effort it arrives there. Nine of the rarer forms
declined to take part.

The University takes some satisfaction in a result whose principal
recommendation is a shorter step between samples. Associate Professor Uzoma said
the School had "spent a good many years on what the ground withholds, and it is
agreeable to find some of it was only waiting to be counted more slowly".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.91zmdy.
