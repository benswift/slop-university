For most of the five years covered by its new impact report, the School of Continuous Improvement's most closely studied instrument was not a dashboard or a database, but somebody else's front door. The report traces four strands of work built from a single five-kind taxonomy of doorstep visitors: a classification scheme developed with a telecommunications partner and cleared by a regulatory review, a single-tile hallway panel that shows a household only its current visitor's kind, an independent audit checking the taxonomy against what a trained observer saw at the same doors, and a predictive layer that estimates a visitor's kind before the bell is ever pressed.

The University counts the report among the clearer examples yet of research staying in the room long enough to see what it changes.

> A working taxonomy and a true one are not the same thing. Five years in, we trust the first far more than the second, and that gap is most of what this report is actually about.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead, Adaptive Metrics Lab

"The panel earned its place on the wall by becoming boring enough that nobody checks it any more," said Dr Mirela Hanke, Deputy Convenor of the Living Dashboard, "which is closer to success than it sounds."

The report's final strand is also its most closely watched: in a season-long trial with twelve volunteer households, a predictive layer now silences the doorbell's chime and logs the visit without alerting anyone inside whenever it expects an uninvited caller, on a standing instruction reviewable only once the season ends. Eleven of the twelve households kept the instruction in place throughout.

The full impact report is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4luowu.
