The School of Continuous Improvement has built a career on catching an
indicator after it has quietly stopped meaning what everyone still assumes
it means, and a new Slop University research poster hands it fresh
material: a physiotherapy clinic's no-show risk score, reliable for years,
that stopped working the day the clinic added video appointments and
nobody noticed.

> The score was trained on people who had to find a car park. Nobody told
> it some of them would only ever need to find a link.
>
> --- Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous
> Improvement

Dr Hanke, working with Dr Torun Ezeigwe and Dr Solveig Adeyemi, tracked
9,540 appointments across four clinic sites over fourteen months, crossing
the booking software's own risk band against whether a visit happened in
person or over video. For in-person appointments, the score's gradient
held: patients flagged high-risk missed appointments at more than six
times the rate of patients flagged low-risk. For telehealth, the gradient
flattened almost to nothing --- a dropped video call and a genuine
no-show share one outcome code, and the score can no longer tell them
apart.

"The numbers don't fail loudly," said Dr Ezeigwe, Director of the Master
of Applied Measurement. "They just stop meaning what the booking desk
still assumes they mean, and the courtesy calls keep going out on schedule
either way."

The poster joins the School's expanding audit of decision tools still
issuing verdicts long after the population they learned from has moved
on.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.451z4q.
