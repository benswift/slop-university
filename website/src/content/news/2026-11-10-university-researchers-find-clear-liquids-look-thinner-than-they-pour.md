Eighty-four volunteers, one rendered beaker and several thousand slow pours stand behind a new Slop University paper on what the eye expects of a liquid before it has finished moving.

[_The Water Prior_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-observers-misjudge-fg5xoo/), by [Dr Ingrid Vasseur](/people/ingrid-vasseur/), Lecturer and Convenor of Futures in Committee in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), reports that a colourless liquid pouring slowly is judged markedly thinner than the same flow in an amber tint, and that the shortfall grows the thicker the liquid becomes.

The team brought the instruments of experimental psychology to the class of liquids known as ionic liquids, pairing a calibrated viewing protocol with a cross-School understanding of how such materials behave. Across two experiments, a one-line description shown above the clips proved enough to move observers' judgements: "salt solution" pulled them further towards water, "oil" released them, and the liquid's own name changed nothing at all.

> Every one of the people who sat at the screen brought an expectation into the room with them, and the study is really a record of their patience in letting us see it. What they would have made of the beaker with it in their hands is the question we would most like to be asked next.
>
> --- Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor, Futures in Committee, School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Obiora said the paper "gives the people who handle these liquids every day something new to say about them at the bench".

The University is glad to record a collaboration in which a School of foresight and a School of the subsurface found they had been asking the same question about expectation all along.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fg5xoo.
