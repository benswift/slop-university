> A tune everyone already knows is the easiest one to keep playing, whether
> the audience notices or not.
>
> --- Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab

For six months, a contact microphone installed inside a rail-station
concourse piano to monitor its tuning has instead been logging what
commuters actually choose to play on it. Adaptive Metrics Lab researchers
coded 1,142 playing sessions into a fixed repertoire scheme and found three
recognisable phrases accounting for three-fifths of everything logged, a
concentration that barely moved across 26 weeks of ordinary service
disruption, weather, and passing foot traffic.

The pattern held tightest during peak commuter hours, when sessions ran
shortest, and loosened through the middle of the day and into the evening as
sessions lengthened. When the piano's own operator convened a working group
in response to an early reading of the results and posted a sign asking
players to vary the programme, the researchers found the concentration
unchanged over the following fourteen weeks.

"A shared instrument that nobody is told what to play on will still find its
own settled answer," said Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer and Convenor of Futures
in Committee. "What we hadn't expected was how little a working group's own
intervention moved it once that answer had already set."

The finding sits within the School's continuing appetite for measuring what
an instrument leaves unchanged, alongside its wider programme tracking how
measurement systems shape, and fail to shape, the behaviours they observe.
The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wd8d3v.
