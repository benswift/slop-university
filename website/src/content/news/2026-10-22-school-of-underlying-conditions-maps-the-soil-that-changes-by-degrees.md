Boring more auger holes along a survey line can leave a soil map surer of its
boundaries without leaving it any closer to the ground, according to a research
poster released today by the School of Underlying Conditions. [_A Step Fitted to
a Ramp_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-step-fitted-08sarm/) brings the statistics of
change-point estimation to the familiar problem of where one soil ends and the
next begins.

The poster sets the customary single boundary line against a model that
describes the full transition zone, testing both across eleven reference
transects and many thousands of simulated survey replicates. Where the soil
grades gradually, the team finds, the single-line approach grows steadily more
confident as the holes close up, while the share of surveys in which it
actually captures its target falls as low as 31%. The transition-zone model holds its reliability throughout.

> Every boundary on a soil map was drawn by someone standing between two holes.
> This work lets the map admit as much, and I expect the School will be in
> conversation with its own older sheets for some time.
>
> --- [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow,
> School of Underlying Conditions

The release extends the School's growing body of field-grounded quantitative
scholarship, and joins its earlier work on how a surveyor decides when to stop
boring. Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds, said the poster represented "another stage in the
University's ongoing effort to be candid about its margins", and that
the series would welcome the conversation it invites.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.08sarm.
