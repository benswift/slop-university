The School of Emergent Priorities has spent much of the year on measures
compiled by the people they assess. _Twenty-Two Point Nine_, deposited today
through the Office of Research Outputs, is the plainest case the school has
found, because a checked suitcase happens to be weighed twice: once by an agent
at the acceptance desk, and once more in the baggage hall by a system that
weighs it for the aircraft's balance and reports to nobody.

Across four stations of one carrier's network over eleven weeks, the research
team matched 41,206 desk records to the ramp reading for the same bag. Away
from the allowance the two instruments agree to within a few tens of grams. In
the final quarter-kilogram below it, the desk record holds 8.9 per cent of all
bags against the 4.1 per cent the distribution predicts, and 22.9 kilograms is
the most common weight typed anywhere in the corpus. The bags pass through the
limit smoothly.

> We came to this expecting to write something about scales. What we have ended
> up with is a study of which of two numbers gets reported, and that has changed
> what the school thinks it is looking at whenever it looks at a rate.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer in the School, welcomed the questions the second
weighing raises. "Almost nothing we study comes with an independent measurement
sitting next to it," said Dr Solheim. "This one did, and we would like to know
what else would look like this if it had one."

The University regards the deposit as a useful addition to the year's work on
indicators that report on the conditions of their own making, and expects the
comparison to be attempted elsewhere.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ybc4oz.
