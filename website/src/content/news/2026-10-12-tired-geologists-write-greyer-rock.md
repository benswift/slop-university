By the tenth hour of a twelve-hour shift, nearly half the rock that one camp's
drilling records gave any colour at all was simply grey. That is the central
finding of _Grey by the Tenth Hour: Prior-Night Sleep, Time on Shift and the
Vocabulary of Core Description_, a paper by Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma,
Lead of the Basement Relations Group, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer,
both of the School of Underlying Conditions.

> I logged core for years and would have told you my afternoons were as good as
> my mornings. Twenty-three people let me find out otherwise on their time
> instead of mine, and I have thanked each of them more than once.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

Working in the register of occupational psychology, the authors followed 23
core loggers through three two-week swings at an unnamed exploration camp,
pairing a morning sleep diary with the time-stamped descriptions the loggers
wrote for more than 41,000 intervals of rock. As a shift wore on the
descriptions shortened and their colour words fell away by almost half, faster
after a short night, while the compulsory coded entries were filled in as
reliably in the last hour as in the first.

There is some quiet satisfaction at the University in a school of earth
scientists that has turned, for the length of one paper, from the core to the
people holding the pencil. Dr Emenike said the study "asked very little of
anyone except that they go on doing their jobs and tell us how they slept, and
they told us with rather more candour than we had left room for on the form".

The paper offers no view on the accuracy of any log or the merits of any roster.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.uefmvy.
