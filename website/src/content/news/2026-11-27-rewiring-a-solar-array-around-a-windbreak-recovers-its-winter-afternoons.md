A windbreak that cannot be cut down has become a wiring question, and the University's newest paper answers it with a cable drum and two days on the rack.

[_Stringing with the Shadow_](/outputs/slop-paper-shade-ordered-string-ox9f72/) follows a small ground-mounted solar array on a rural site whose western boundary carries a row of cypress the owner is obliged to keep. Each winter afternoon the trees' shadow moves across the panels, and the array as built met it with every string at once. Dr [Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), with Dr [Marit Osayande](/people/marit-osayande/) and Mr [Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/) of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), re-assigned the panels so that the shadow takes one string at a time. Over the following winter the array returned 12.1 per cent more energy for the light it received, and modelling puts the recovery at 79 per cent of what the original wiring had been losing.

> A shadow that keeps its appointments is a very good colleague to have. We spent two winters learning its habits, and I think there is a good deal more it would be glad to show us.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The study pairs whole-site shade surveying with array-scale circuit modelling and seasonal field monitoring, bringing installation practice and the long-term stewardship of fixed obstructions into a single frame of reference for operators who share their sites with something that is staying.

Dr Osayande said the School of Continuous Improvement had been pleased to lend its monitoring to "a site that asked very little of us and gave back a great deal, mostly after lunch."

The paper joins a widening body of University work on infrastructure that must come to terms with the ground it stands on.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ox9f72.
