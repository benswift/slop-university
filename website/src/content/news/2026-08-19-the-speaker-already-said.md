The fastest way to end an argument at dinner, it turns out, is not to ask the
smart speaker but to remember aloud what it said last time. A paper released
today by the School of Continuous Improvement reports that in 41 suburban
households followed across two school terms, settling a dispute by citing the
device overtook actually querying it before the panel's final month.

_Towards a Domestic Jurisprudence of the Kitchen Smart Speaker_, by Dr Sten
Okwuosa, Dr Mirela Hanke and Dr Torun Ezeigwe, catalogues 517 standing rulings
--- speaker answers a household invokes again and again without ever asking
twice --- including a measurable minority that rest on a question the device
misheard. When a platform update changed the answers behind almost half of the
rulings in force, most households kept citing the old ones; 24 of the 41
formally grandfathered them.

> A citation is a small act of trust in one's own past. These households have
> simply extended that trust to an appliance, and the School is grateful they
> let us listen in.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway Atlas

Across two terms the panel braided dispute cards, prompted-recall calls and
fortnightly household interviews into a single account of how a body of
domestic precedent accumulates, and of what a software update passing through
the kitchen leaves undisturbed. Dr Hanke said the panel "reminded the School
how much of governance is simply remembering in company".

The study deepens a line of inquiry the University considers characteristically
its own: the records everyday life keeps of itself. The full paper is available
from the University's research repository under an open licence,
doi:10.5555/slop.9p8bn4.
