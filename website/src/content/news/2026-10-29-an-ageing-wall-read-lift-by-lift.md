An ageing concrete retaining wall keeps a record of how it was built, and the
depth to which the air has reached into it is where that record can be read.
So finds
[_Mapped by Lift_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-gridded-core-6sxrac/), a research
poster in which the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) brings the
methods of soil survey to a structure poured in three stages.

[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, cored the wall at 96 points across its face. Rather
than varying smoothly from one end to the other, carbonation depth held nearly
level within each pour and stepped at both joints, and a map drawn lift by lift
proved markedly more accurate than one drawn across the wall as a whole.

> I have spent a career drawing lines across the ground and wondering whether they were really there. It was a pleasure to spend a season with a surface that had made up its mind, and I expect the School will be thinking about it for some time.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Combining field sampling, spatial modelling and the construction logic of the
structure itself, the work extends the School's long attention to boundaries
into the built environment, and positions survey practice to engage more
closely with the histories of the things it surveys. The University counts it
among the steadier signs of its schools carrying their instruments onto
unfamiliar ground.

Associate Professor Uzoma said the project had been "a welcome reminder that
the core tray is the same tray, whatever the core came out of".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6sxrac.
