Two engraved slabs laid in the same year, cut to the same specification and set
two metres apart, can differ almost fivefold in how much stone they have lost.
[_What the Puddles Decide_](/outputs/slop-paper-what-the-puddles-4m2vqe/), a new
paper led by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) with
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) and
[Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), traces that difference
to the minutes each slab spends under standing water after rain.

> Most of what an institution undertakes to keep is kept by something physical,
> and the physical thing has a drainage fall. We are interested in the places
> where those two facts have not yet been introduced to each other.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, School of Underlying Conditions

The study brings levelling, timed observation of standing water and
measurement of surface loss together across a single paved forecourt, pairing
each slab's record of laying with the conditions it has actually met since.
The approach anchors a broader School interest in the settings where a surface
is asked to hold a record over the long term, and in the conditions that
quietly govern whether it can.

"A register tells you which bay a gift sits in," said Dr Clemency Tredinnick of
the School of Settled Matters. "Putting that beside where the water goes is the
kind of patient, unglamorous alignment the University is glad to support."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4m2vqe.
