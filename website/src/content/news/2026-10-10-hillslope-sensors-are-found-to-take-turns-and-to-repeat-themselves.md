What does a conversation look like when none of its parties can hear while they are speaking? _After You: Turn Allocation, Overlap and Repair among the Nodes of a Hillslope Piezometer Network_, a paper by Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma of the School of Underlying Conditions, puts the question to a set of instruments that were installed to answer a different one.

The instruments are twenty-two bores on a grazed slope, each reporting its water level by radio four times an hour on a single shared channel. The paper sets the readings aside and attends to the reporting. It takes 120 rounds of radio traffic and writes each out line by line, as a linguist would write out a recorded telephone call, with every silence timed and every simultaneous start marked.

> I transcribed for most of a winter. I would not claim to have got to know the nodes, but I did come to know when one of them was about to go unanswered.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, School of Underlying Conditions

Read this way, the network proves to be orderly in the places talk is orderly and by other means. Two nodes that speak at once do not notice, and each learns of it only when no reply arrives. A node left without a reply has one remedy, which is to say the same thing again, and in 148 of the 402 cases examined it was repeating something that had been received the first time.

Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer in the School, said colleagues were "pleased to learn that the piezometers have manners, and will go on reading them for water." The University considers the paper a mark of the breadth with which its newest school is prepared to attend to its own equipment, and of the standing that careful listening holds across its research settings.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wh2bfv.
