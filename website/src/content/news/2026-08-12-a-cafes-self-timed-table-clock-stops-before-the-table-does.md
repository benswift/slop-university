> A patron stopping the clock at twenty-nine minutes rather than
> thirty-one isn't forgetting to tap out. They're making a decision, and
> making it in their own favour.
>
> --- Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

That decision, replicated across a board game café's self-service billing
kiosk, is the subject of a new Slop University paper. Dr Solheim and Dr
Ronja Oyelaran fitted eight of the café's sixteen tables with a silent RFID
sensor tucked beneath the tabletop, timed to a staggered equipment rollout
already under way for reasons of the café's own, and spent nine months
setting what patrons tapped into the kiosk against what the sensor actually
recorded.

Self-reported stop times bunched in the two minutes before each half-hour
billing boundary at roughly three times the surrounding rate. At the
instrumented tables, the sensor kept logging a table as occupied for a
median 27 minutes after the kiosk said it was free. An in-café placard
asking patrons to "please stop the timer when you leave" made almost no
difference to the gap.

For the School of Emergent Priorities, the finding slots into a settled
suspicion: that a self-reported measure sitting on a financial threshold is
the one an audit should check first, not last.

Dr Oyelaran, the paper's co-author, said the placard result was the one
that surprised her: "we went in assuming people forgot. What we found
looks much more like people noticing exactly where the line is and
stepping half a pace back from it."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8mpaeq.
