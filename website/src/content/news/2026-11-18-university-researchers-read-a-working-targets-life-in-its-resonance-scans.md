> Every target that goes into the chamber comes out a little different. For once we asked one of them how, and kept asking for twelve years.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

That patience is the substance of [_Target Seven, Twelve Years On_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-working-life-34jnvt/), a new research poster by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) and [Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/), Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab in the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/). The poster follows a single implanted neon target through ten experimental campaigns and 48 depth scans, and finds that it gave up most of the neon it would ever lose in its first campaign. What has changed since is a thin layer of carbon, settling on its surface at a steady pace.

The team aligned each campaign's opening resonance scan with the accelerator's own record of the beam delivered, building a whole-of-life view of target condition that lets every past campaign carry a correction of its own.

The University welcomes the poster as an early marker of its presence in low-energy nuclear physics, and as a model of the regard its laboratories are encouraged to show the small, hard-working objects at the centre of their experiments. "A controller is only ever as good as its picture of the plant," said Associate Professor Beng. "It has been a pleasure to work alongside a group that redraws that picture every time the beam comes on."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.34jnvt.
