Every entrance to every building the University keeps is to carry a mat, a rack
and a posted schedule of whose shoes come off at the door. The arrangement comes
from a new brochure, published today by the School of Continuous Improvement,
that reports householders' accounts of the doormat in the words householders
used for it.

> A threshold is one of the few places where a household says what it means
> before anyone has sat down. We are glad to have been let in far enough to write
> some of it down.
>
> --- Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School of Continuous Improvement

Researchers sat in hallways with householders and kept their vocabulary: 'the
pile', 'tradies', 'Nan keeps hers on'. The schedules that hang at University
entrances are worded in those categories, and they change only when a further
round of hallway interviews changes them. Where a schedule asks for shoes off,
staff say "don't worry about your shoes" as a request; where it asks for shoes
on, they say nothing. Improvement Grand Rounds will hear householders' latest
accounts each year and consider whether the schedules should move.

The brochure features the work of Bram Ntuli, Solveig Adeyemi and Iben Chikere,
and sets it beside five of the University's published studies of the signals
people read every day. Iben Chikere, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities,
said the team's thanks go to "the households who kept the kettle on while we
asked about their hallways."

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3nmhfn.
