A third of one laboratory's unexplained "metal-free" results can be reproduced,
it turns out, only with the stir bar that was in the flask at the time. That is
the central result of
[_What the Stir Bars Kept_](/outputs/slop-poster-retired-ptfe-stir-8gi2vp/), a
new research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions by
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer,
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group.

> I spent ten years taking one reading at a time down a bore. Opening the drawer
> felt very like that, except that this time every reading already had a number
> on it.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The team brought the School's stratigraphic habits to an unfamiliar archive: ten
years of retired bars from a single organometallic group, each etched with a
number its notebooks record. Assaying every bar for the metals it still held,
and aligning the results with the notebooks year by year, the authors recover
the group's movement from palladium to nickel to cobalt as a sequence of layers,
then return a dozen anomalous control reactions to their original bars to see
which of them were ever really free of metal.

Dr Obiora said the team had come away "with a great deal of respect for whoever
etched those numbers, and a fresh supply of bars".

The University counts the poster among the clearest recent examples of its
research culture taking the unremarkable object seriously, and of an earth
science school finding its methods at home on a chemistry bench.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8gi2vp.
