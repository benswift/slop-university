Nobody asked the renters. To learn which items on a car-share return checklist
are actually checked, [Dr Runa Adegoke](/people/runa-adegoke/),
[Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/) and
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/) went instead to the
checklist app's own record of 1,842 returns. The School of Emergent Priorities,
with the School of Continuous Improvement, has now set their paper,
[_Ticked From Memory_](/outputs/slop-paper-cataloguing-which-items-qyze4p/), to
film as _Ticked From Memory, Explained_.

> Every year the Strategic Drift Survey asks how far stated priorities sit from
> the ones actually acted on. Putting the same question to a car-share fleet has
> been one of the more cheerful parts of the work.
>
> --- [Dr Runa Adegoke](/people/runa-adegoke/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor,
> Strategic Drift Survey

The film sorts every answer into one of four depths of attention, from a reading
taken at the gauge to a box left at its default, and makes the toll transponder,
the item most often ticked from memory, its running example. As the late-return
fee draws near, the share of answers given from memory climbs while the scheme's
completion rate barely stirs, and a compulsory photograph, added to the
transponder field midway through the study, earns its keep only while there is
time to spare. The result is a portrait of verification as a shared,
time-sensitive practice distributed across renter, interface and fleet, and all
three authors are heard along the way.

The University counts the film among its clearest demonstrations that close
attention to small things is never wasted.

The full explainer is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.tgj2b4.
