A segment is supposed to be a group of people who resemble one another more
than they resemble everybody else. The School of Continuous Improvement has now
held one set of six against that definition.

_Six Names for the Same Crowd_, a research poster released today through the
Office of Research Outputs, re-derives the buyer segmentation an estate agency
group runs across its 27 sales offices. Led by Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research
Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas, the study applies the group's
own model to 48,206 open-home registrations gathered over fourteen months.
Under resampling, median cluster stability sits at 0.40; on held-out
registrations it falls to 0.31, with five of the six segments below the level at
which a cluster is conventionally read as dissolved. Between consecutive
quarterly refreshes, 46.8 per cent of registrants move segment. Added to a count
of how many open homes a person attended in the previous ninety days, segment
membership shifts the model's discrimination from 0.673 to 0.681, an interval
the poster reports as containing no effect.

> Nobody set out to sort strangers into six kinds. Somebody chose six because
> six fits on a screen, and everything since has been the model doing as it was
> told.
>
> --- Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

The group's own reporting records 99.6 per cent of registrations as allocated to
a segment across the period, and books each quarterly re-derivation as a
coverage improvement. "An indicator that only moves when somebody re-runs the
model is reporting on the model," said Dr Renke Sabel, Senior Lecturer and
Convenor of the Indicator Commons.

Slop University offers the poster as an instance of the School's standing
question about a category: whether anything answers to it.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.yrnz28.
