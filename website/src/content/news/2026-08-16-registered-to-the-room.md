A registration belongs to an address, not to a person. _The Certificate Stays
With the Wall_, released today by the School of Emergent Priorities, asks what
follows from that for a council programme which reports public-health assurance
as premises inspected, and finds that the people the assurance is read to cover
move between shops several times faster than the inspections arrive.

The team coded each of the checklist's 46 items against what it can be observed
on, enumerated the 402 operators working across 88 registered premises in three
council districts between February and June, and set the resulting tenure
distribution beside the council's own published inspection cycle. Thirty-two
items score the room and its contents, eight the paperwork kept in it, six what
can be seen happening on the day. None records who performs it.

> Nobody in this scheme is doing anything wrong. The record answers a question
> about a tenancy, and it is being read as an answer about a practitioner, and
> that difference is worth stating plainly before anyone proposes a remedy.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The scheduling result drew the study's closest attention. Because a clean
history lengthens the interval between visits, the addresses inspected least
often are those whose chairs change hands fastest. Dr Petra Umbile,
Postdoctoral Fellow, described that finding as "the part we did not go looking
for, and the part we would most like somebody else to check".

The University counts the poster among the plainer demonstrations of its
measurement interest: a record accurate about one thing and read as accurate
about another, with nothing between the two but a habit of reading.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.zicezg.
