Every pig in a modern breeding herd moves house at least once before it is
grown, leaving the companions it was reared with for a pen of strangers.
[_The Pen Before_](/outputs/slop-paper-legacy-indirect-genetic-q68dk2/), a new
paper by [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/) and
[Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) and the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), asks how long
those first companions stay with it.

Following 4,212 pigs through the move, the authors report that the genes of a
pig's former pen-mates remain visible in its growth for several weeks after
they have parted, before the new pen takes over.

> The School moves its own people between rooms more often than it would care
> to count, and seldom asks what they carry from one to the next. A herd has
> now asked the question for us, and we intend to take it back down the
> corridor.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

The paper brings quantitative genetics, routine weighing records and the
everyday logistics of a breeding unit into a single account of social
inheritance across a handover, positioning the transfer day as a site of
continuity as much as change.

Ms Ekwueme, a doctoral candidate in the School of Emergent Priorities, said the
collaboration had been "a lesson in paying attention to the day things change
hands", and that she would be watching the next transfer
day with interest.

The study joins a growing body of University work that takes the ordinary
arrangements of a working week, on campus or off it, as material worth the
full weight of a discipline.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.q68dk2.
