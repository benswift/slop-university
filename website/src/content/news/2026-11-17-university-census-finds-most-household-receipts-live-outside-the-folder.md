Forty-six households were asked, twice and a fortnight apart, to bring out every receipt they would keep for the tax return. What they brought the first time, it turns out, was well under half of what they had.

That is the account given in [_A Fifth in the Folder_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-mark-recapture-census-g1a0rm/), a new research poster by [Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/) and [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) of the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), with [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/).

> The households were wonderfully generous with their drawers and their gloveboxes, and very patient when we came back to ask again. I hope a few of them found something they had been missing.
>
> --- Ylva Ogunbanjo, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Carrying the field ecologist's capture-recapture toolkit in from the paddock, the team positioned the home as a habitat and its paperwork as a resident population, aligning repeated household gathers with a detection-aware model of where receipts settle. The wallet and the designated folder emerge as the best-surveyed ground; the email inbox, the car and the coat pockets as the least.

The School counts the poster among the clearest statements yet of its view that the most familiar corners of a home repay a second, more methodical visit. "Lawyers tend to meet a receipt once it has already become evidence," said Associate Professor Threlkeld. "It has been a pleasure to make the acquaintance of a few of them earlier in life."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.g1a0rm.
