A committee that swaps a written form for a face-to-face question rarely gets
to see what replaces the paperwork's blank space. Slop University reports
today on what did: a seven-part inventory of the ways a pottery-guild
applicant answers a glaze question they cannot, in the moment, honestly
answer.

A paper by Dr Dagny Okafor, Associate Professor Casimir Beng and Dr Iben
Chikere examines a regional pottery guild association's move from a written
glaze-practice statement to a live interview for its scarce advanced-kiln
slots, taken up after committee volunteers noticed a season's written
statements reading suspiciously alike. The University reads the switch as a
case study in what a verification method actually verifies once it changes
shape.

Across nine chapters and one firing season, the researchers coded 161
consented interview transcripts for seven recurring verbal moves, from
reciting a well-known formula in place of a personal account to redirecting a
question toward a nearby finished piece, and checked whether the pattern held
up once a convenor's own follow-up questioning was stripped from the
transcript before coding.

> Retiring a form doesn't retire the moment someone has to answer for it; the
> guild only moved where that moment happens now.
>
> --- Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation

Associate Professor Casimir Beng, whose Adaptive Metrics Lab has traced
similar drift in verification systems well beyond the guild hall, said the
pattern "fits something the Lab keeps meeting: change how a thing gets
checked, and people find the new thing to say."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.e1szwr.
