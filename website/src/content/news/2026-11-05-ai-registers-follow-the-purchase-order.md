Which of the company's software is AI? Compliance managers have been fielding
that question from boards, insurers and clients for some time, and a new paper
from the University suggests the answer they give owes more to the filing
cabinet than to the software.

In
[_Where the AI System Ends_](/outputs/slop-paper-where-the-ai-usuvdi/),
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and
[Dr Fenna Okoro](/people/fenna-okoro/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon
Register, in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/),
follow six mid-sized firms as each compiles its first AI-use register. Products
bought as AI were almost always listed. Features that arrived quietly in a
routine vendor update to software already in place were listed less than one
time in ten.

> We owe a great deal to the compliance teams who let us into their meetings,
> their spreadsheets and their holding tabs. Their generosity is what made the
> work possible, and the work is in many ways theirs.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

The study brings interview, document and meeting-record evidence into a
cross-case account of ownership, precedent and procurement practice in
organisational AI governance, positioning the register itself as a site where
firms settle what the law will mean for them.

The paper sits comfortably within the University's sustained commitment to the
lists, ledgers and registers through which organisations come to know their
own workings. "A list is a set of decisions people have agreed to stop
revisiting, and we have enjoyed revisiting a few of them," said Dr Obiora.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.usuvdi.
