Shoppers who say they trust a self-checkout's error prompt press its override
button about as often as shoppers who say they don't.
[Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/),
[Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/) and
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/) reported as much in
their poster,
[_Trusting the Beep_](/outputs/slop-poster-measuring-whether-stated-9pvjpl/),
and the School of Emergent Priorities has now turned it into a short film,
_Trusting the Beep, Explained_.

> A self-checkout asks shoppers the same small question many times a week. It
> seemed only courteous that a university should ask them something in return.
>
> --- [Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), Lecturer, School of Emergent
> Priorities

A moment most shoppers will recognise, a prompt on the screen and a choice
between accepting it and pressing override, is treated here as a case study in
where stated attitude and observed behaviour part company. The centrepiece is
the poster's own chart, on which override rates sit within a few points of one
another at every level of trust, and each of
[Dr Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), [Dr Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/) and
[Associate Professor Beng](/people/casimir-beng/) speaks to the part of the work
they know best.

Few things gratify the University more than a null result reported with care,
and it is glad to send this one further afield.

The full explainer is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fbanxj.
