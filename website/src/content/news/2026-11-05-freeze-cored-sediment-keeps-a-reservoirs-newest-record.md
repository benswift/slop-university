Freezing a sediment core in place, rather than pushing a tube down into the
mud, keeps the loose surface layer where the most recent years settle. In
[_The Substitution Is in the Floc_](/outputs/slop-poster-bisphenol-substitution-in-uf5def/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), that layer
turns out to hold most of a change the rest of the core barely shows.

The team paired conventional and freeze-collected cores from an urban
stormwater reservoir, dated them slice by slice, and traced how the mix of
bisphenol compounds settling there has shifted over a quarter of a century. In
the top centimetre, the analogues that took over from BPA account for 60% of
the total.

> The School has always been most at home with the parts of the record that
> never came up. It has been instructive to spend a season with a part that was
> there the whole time, waiting for us to bring the right tool.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

The poster extends the Basement Relations Group's long engagement with what a
core can and cannot carry to the surface, bringing its sampling practice,
dating workflows and laboratory capability to bear on the reservoir floor. It
sits comfortably within the University's commitment to research that reads the
ground it stands on.

Senior Research Fellow Dr Brynjar Obiora thanked "everyone who stood on a cold
boat at dawn and handed us dry ice without asking why", and said the work had
given the School "a reason to look again at the top of every core in the
store".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.uf5def.
