"If the Tuesday one's still full by Thursday I ring straight away — but if
it's gone I don't need to, do I, I can see she's had it." The sentiment
repeated itself, in one form or another, across interviews with thirty-four
adult children who check on a parent's medication organiser from another
suburb or another state. A paper from the School of Emergent Priorities
follows twenty-one such households over ten months to trace how a photograph
of a pill box's compartments quietly became the thing a phone call used to
be for.

Researchers tested whether the pattern could be reversed: eleven households
were given a printed card for the organiser's lid reading "still call —
don't just check," while ten received nothing. Visit timing in the two
groups was not distinguishable. Several households kept the card on the box
without treating it as an instruction to do anything differently.

> A measure that's cheap to check has a way of becoming the only one anybody checks, in a family or in a university.
>
> — Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

The School frames the finding as an unremarkable extension of its longstanding
interest in what a stated commitment looks like once it meets a routine.
Having heard the study's account of "a checkable physical proxy standing in
for the activity it once merely evidenced," the Strategic Drift Survey has
adopted that description as the basis for its own next assessment cycle: any
University priority will now be scored as enacted only where it can point to
an equivalent low-effort, checkable proxy, with no exception carried past a
single review cycle.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.e9yuaw.
