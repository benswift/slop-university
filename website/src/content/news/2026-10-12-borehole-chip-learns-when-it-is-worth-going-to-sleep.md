A circuit that does nothing for ninety-eight per cent of its working life
should not, it turns out, always be switched off. That is the finding of
_Asleep Between Samples: A Depth-Gated Near-Threshold Acquisition Processor for
Battery-Run Borehole Logging_, a paper by Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma,
Lead of the Basement Relations Group, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, in
the School of Underlying Conditions.

> We are geologists, and we went looking in the catalogue for an instrument
> that was not in it. I cannot say that it exists now. It is, however, a good
> deal better described than it was.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The paper aligns circuit design, thermal context and field practice around a
single decision, taken 124,000 times on the way down one hole. Near the surface,
where the rock is cool, the processor is left on between samples; below 629
metres at the speed studied, it is put to sleep. Across a replayed descent the
approach uses about a third less energy in the part of the chip concerned than
leaving it on, and about a sixth less than always switching it off. Where the
tool loses contact with the wall of the hole, it records that it saw nothing,
and for how long.

The University considers a school's readiness to specify what it cannot yet
purchase to be a mark of a settled research posture, and is pleased to see it
exercised at the scale of a few square millimetres. Dr Emenike said the work
"borrowed a vocabulary from colleagues in another discipline, and we intend to
return it in good condition".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qzodae.
