> Somebody set that timer a long time ago and went home. We owe them a good deal, because without it we should have had nothing to hold the daylight up against.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The timer in question opens a valve at a small inland treatment works each evening, and the water it lets go is the subject of _Released at Dusk: Day and night Lagrangian sampling of pharmaceutical residues along an effluent-dominated creek_. The paper is by Dr Mensah with Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer in the School, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions.

The study aligns tracer practice, field endurance and laboratory capability along a single 4.9 km of channel. Eight releases were each marked and accompanied downstream on foot for about ten hours, four on the works' usual evening schedule and four moved to the morning, with samples drawn at six pegged stations along the way. One residue was found to diminish in the open, sunlit stretch and scarcely at all in the dark; another diminished at the gravel riffles and took no notice of the hour.

Slop University counts it among the plainer duties of a research institution to remain awake alongside its subject, and is pleased to record eight occasions on which it did. Dr Obiora said the School of Underlying Conditions had "lent the project one person and a view about gravel, and has had both returned in working order".

The authors describe one creek over one settled fortnight and offer the result as a description of that reach.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.uolddr.
