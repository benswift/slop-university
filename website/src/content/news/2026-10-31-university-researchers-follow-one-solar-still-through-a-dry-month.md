Half a month's water arrives in the first eight days. That is the central
finding of _Twenty-Eight Days in One Pit_, a new paper by Dr Brynjar Obiora and
Dr Vigdis Emenike of the School of Underlying Conditions, which follows three
pit solar stills left in place through 28 days without rain.

> Every pit is a question put to the ground. We left this one open for four
> weeks and the soil went on answering, a little more quietly each morning.
> There is a good deal more still to ask it.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The study brings the School's soil survey and groundwater fieldwork together
around a single, patient question: how a device that draws its water from the
earth beneath it keeps pace with an earth that is slowly giving up. Through a
combined programme of daily weighing, profile monitoring and energy-balance
instrumentation, the team traces how the still's output, the drying soil and
the sunlight held in the ground move together across the month, and what that
movement means for how such stills might be tended.

It is the kind of unhurried field record the University has always made room
for, and one that sits comfortably within the School's longer conversation
about water that stays, or does not stay, where it is found.

Dr Emenike described the paper as "a careful month, carefully kept," and said
the School would be glad to see the record read alongside its earlier work on
soils and the heat they carry.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ntxgeb.
