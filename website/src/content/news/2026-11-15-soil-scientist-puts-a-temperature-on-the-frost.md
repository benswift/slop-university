How warm is a frost? For the line between two layers of soil, the answer turns out to be a number, at least up to a point. That is the proposal of [_How Hot Is a Frost?_](/outputs/slop-poster-freeze-thaw-cycling-as-ltnqpe/), a research poster by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) and [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), which brings the statistical physics of interfaces to a question soil surveyors meet each winter in the pit face.

> A surveyor learns to read a boundary with a trowel. It has been a pleasure to learn to read one with a thermometer as well, and we suspect the next few winters will tell us what it is we have been measuring.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster builds a simulated soil out of two kinds of grain and puts it through a long run of freezing and thawing, then asks the boundary between them to account for itself in two separate ways. Where a light frost is concerned, both accounts agree on a single temperature. Past a certain strength of heave they part company, and the poster positions that parting as an invitation to understand what a hard frost does that warmth alone does not.

The work represents a meeting of field practice and theoretical method that the School has been cultivating across several recent outputs, and it carries the University's conviction that the oldest working knowledge of a discipline can sit comfortably beside its newest mathematics. Dr Ntuli said the collaboration had "given a long simulation a very muddy set of boots".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ltnqpe.
