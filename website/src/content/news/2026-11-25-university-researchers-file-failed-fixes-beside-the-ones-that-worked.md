Most service desks keep a careful record of the fix that worked. A new research poster from the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) asks what should become of the ones that did not, and finds that the answer depends on where they are filed.

In [_What Didn't Work, Kept_](/outputs/slop-poster-retaining-failed-repair-jzbb2e/), Mr [Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/), Dr [Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/) and Dr [Solveig Adeyemi](/people/solveig-adeyemi/) replayed 9,412 tickets from a campus service desk through three versions of a case-based experience base. When each failed attempt was attached as a short note to the successful fix nearest it, the first suggestion resolved the problem 46.7% of the time, against 41.2% for a base that kept only successes. Kept as cases in their own right, the failures pulled the figure down to 36.8%.

> Every unit in the School keeps a list of what went well. Working on this poster, I came to think the more useful list is the shorter one written beside it, and I am glad the School has made room for that list.
>
> --- Mr Eskil Nwabueze, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The work situates case-based reasoning within the practical realities of frontline service operations, connecting the retention of experience with the moment of its reuse and positioning the humble repair note as a meaningful unit of organisational memory.

The team also found that roughly one note in nine was later overtaken by a change of platform, a result Dr Hanke said "gives the next stage of the work a clear sense of direction, and gives us a reason to keep coming back to the desk."

The poster extends the University's sustained interest in how an organisation carries its experience from one day's work into the next.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jzbb2e.
