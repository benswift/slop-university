The University's law school has taken its land-law expertise into the economics
laboratory, and come back with an answer to a question every
conveyancer has asked at some point: who gets paid when everyone has to agree?

[_The Race to Sign First_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-laboratory-holdout-9vq9y2/), a
new research poster by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of
the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/),
[Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/) and
[Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), finds that the owner who signs last
is usually paid most, until a tribunal is given the power to discharge the
covenant. Then the order turns over, and the first signature becomes the
valuable one.

> At a settlement table you learn early that the last signature in the room is
> the expensive one. It was a strange pleasure to watch a room of volunteers
> learn the same thing in an afternoon, and then unlearn it the moment a
> tribunal was mentioned.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Working within a structured laboratory bargaining environment, the team
recast the release of a forgotten covenant as a staged consent pathway,
benchmarking a unanimity setting against a tribunal-enabled alternative and
calibrating 216 participants' signing behaviour against a suite of equilibrium
expectations.

The University is pleased to see a question first met across a settlement
table find a home in the decision sciences, and to see three schools' worth of
method brought to bear on it. Dr Ntuli, Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds,
welcomed the questions the result is likely to attract. "A chance of discharge
that small should not be able to move a whole queue of signatures, and yet it
did," he said. "I expect we will be asked about that for some time, and I hope
we are."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9vq9y2.
