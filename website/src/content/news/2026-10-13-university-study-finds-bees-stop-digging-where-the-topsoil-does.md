The School of Underlying Conditions holds that what happens at the surface is generally settled some way beneath it. Its newest paper puts that position to a colony of bees. _Down to the Clay: Nest Density and Brood-Cell Depth in a Ground-Nesting Bee Aggregation across a Mapped Soil Boundary_ is by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer.

The bees nest in sixty metres of unsealed farm track that runs from a sandy rise onto a clay flat. The authors counted 1,259 nests and found them thickest where the clay lay 34 centimetres down, half as thick where it rose to 22, and absent where it came within 4 centimetres of the surface. Twelve nests stood on the far side of the line a soil survey had drawn across the track. Where the soil was shallow, the lowest chamber of each excavated nest stopped a few centimetres above the clay.

> We have been asked whether the School now studies bees. The School studies what the bees were studying. We welcome the question, and we expect to be asked it again.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The study mobilises a hand auger, a pot of paint and a season of early mornings, underpinning every count of nests with a reading of the ground below it. The authors note that it concerns one track in one spring, and that the bees were not followed to see how their young fared.

The University counts the paper among the plainer demonstrations of its conviction that disciplines are best introduced to one another out of doors. Dr Emenike said the season had shown "that water stands where the clay puts it, and that we were not the first on the track to have noticed".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.59wxrw.
