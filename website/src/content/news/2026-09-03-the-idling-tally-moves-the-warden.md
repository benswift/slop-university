The School of Continuous Improvement's evaluation-ecosystem agenda has lately
turned to the ordinary machinery of enforcement rosters, and its newest
instrument looks at a fixture outside almost every primary school gate: the
kerb-mounted sensor that tallies idling minutes and decides, fortnight by
fortnight, which side of the street gets the parking warden's attention. A
new research poster reports what happened when the University asked whether
moving the warden actually moves the number.

Led by Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, with Dr Thandiwe Solberg and Dr
Fenna Okoro, the study logged idling minutes on both sides of two
kiss-and-drop bays across a full school term, tracking the fortnightly swap a
partner council's Parking Compliance Unit makes on the tally's reading alone.
Idling on the warden-assigned side showed no consistent difference from the
side left unattended, and the finding lands squarely inside the School's
habit of taking a small enforcement mechanism at its own word rather than
asking what it changes.

> A rule that keeps working exactly as written, whatever the numbers do,
> tells you it was never really about the numbers.
>
> --- Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

"It's an ordinary result for us," said Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer
and Convenor of Demo Quarter. "The interesting part was watching the roster
carry on exactly as instructed once the number stopped meaning anything."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.l46tid.
