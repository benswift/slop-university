Follow both leads. It is the instruction a botanical key gives when the plant in
hand cannot answer the question in front of it, and it is the whole subject of
_Both Leads: How Many Names a Dichotomous Key Leaves on a Specimen That Cannot
Answer It_, a research poster by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, Dr Brynjar
Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma,
Associate Professor and Lead, Basement Relations Group.

> The School has been collecting plants at the wrong time of year for some while,
> by long arrangement with the bore roster. It is agreeable to find that the
> arrangement had a theory in it all along.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster aligns a field practice with a formal one. It carries 412 flowerless
specimens, gathered beside 31 monitoring bores, through a printed key to 96
plants, and establishes how many candidate names each is left holding at the
end. As printed, the key hands back a median of fourteen. Rebuilt from the same
characters, with the questions a flowerless plant can answer moved towards the
front, it hands back three.

The University is glad to have a school of rock and water that can be found, on
occasion, in possession of a proof. Dr Obiora said the work "leaves us with
fewer names than we set out with, which the three of us have agreed to regard as
progress".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.q1b6tg.
