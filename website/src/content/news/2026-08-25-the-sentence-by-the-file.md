An employee writes one sentence they are proud of, in a box built to hold
about that much. Slop University reports today on what happens to it next.
A paper from the School of Emergent Priorities and the School of Continuous
Improvement followed 3,106 such sentences, across 41 sites of a national
retail-logistics group's quarterly self-assessment cycle, through a
line-manager pass, a calibration round, and a final sign-off into the
personnel file --- and the University reads the result as confirmation of
something its research keeps landing on from different directions: an
institution's own record is rarely the record of the thing it describes.

The method translates into fewer numbers than the paper carries, but the
shape holds. A self-nominated pride sentence is compared, draft to file,
against an ordinary sentence from the same box, and both are tracked for how
much of their original wording survives each stage of an existing review
process.

> The sentence an employee is proudest of turns out to be the one least
> likely to survive in its own words, and that is not a flaw anyone built in
> on purpose.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

"A calibration panel isn't trying to erase anyone's sentence," said Dr
Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer in the School of Continuous Improvement. "It's
trying to get through the pile by five o'clock, and a familiar phrase is
faster to sign off than a specific one."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.uz4l32.
