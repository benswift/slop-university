> Those of us who draft for a living have always pictured a reader who holds the whole of a provision in mind, exception and all. It has been an instructive season for the School to meet that reader in person, and to find them so good-natured about what they let go.
>
> --- Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld, School of Settled Matters

[_The Proviso Fades First_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-proviso-fades-bfry0h/),
a new research poster led by
[Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) with
[Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) and
[Dr Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/), carries the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) out of the law library
and into the memory laboratory.

The study invited 208 volunteers to read rules that came with a condition
attached, and returned to them after a day, a week or a month. Framing
recognition memory as a lens on everyday rule-following, it aligns the
drafter's craft with the science of remembering and surfaces a consistent
pattern: the rule endures, while the case it does not cover quietly falls
away. Where the exception was given a subsection of its own, it fell away
sooner.

"The School has spent a long time asking how one reading of a provision comes
to be the accepted one," said Dr Penhallow, Senior Lecturer in the School. "It
is a pleasure to have asked the readers as well."

The University regards the poster as a welcome widening of its legal
scholarship into the laboratory, and a sign of how naturally its schools now
borrow one another's instruments.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bfry0h.
