Twenty volunteers spent three sessions apiece writing short postcards on a screen of flashing letters, choosing each one by attention alone while an electrode cap listened in. Some of the letters came out wrong. Some of those, the writers fixed; the rest they let stand.

[_Letting the Letter Stand_](/outputs/slop-poster-error-related-potentials-to-evz717/), a new research poster by Dr [Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Ms [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) and Mr [Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), is about that second group of mistakes. The team reports that the first flicker of the brain's error response looks the same either way, and that a later part of the response is noticeably quieter for the mistakes a writer goes on to accept.

> A school that spends its days on questions people have agreed to leave alone had a great deal to learn from twenty writers deciding, one letter at a time, what they could live with. We are still learning it.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The study brings together signal acquisition, interface design and a close attention to everyday writing practice, and situates the University's settlement scholarship within a new and fast-moving field. Mr Nnamani said the team hoped the poster would reach the people who design such systems, "and anyone who has sent a message with a small mistake in it and decided it would do."

The University counts the poster among a widening body of work in which its schools carry their own questions into laboratories they have not worked in before.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.evz717.
