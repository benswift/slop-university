Counting the taps along a bar takes an inspector about a minute. Following what
those taps go on to pour took the School of Continuous Improvement fourteen
months, and the two exercises describe the same 26 venues rather differently.

_The Slow Keg Holds the Line_, a research poster released today, follows 318 tap
lines from the day a keg is coupled to the day the line is handed to something
else. Lines carrying local product end because the keg has emptied in 29 per
cent of cases by day 60; lines carrying everything else do so in 87 per cent.
The balance is rotated off part-full. Every venue in the study satisfied its
half-of-taps local-share condition at every one of the 41 inspections in the
window, over a period in which local product accounted for 19 per cent of poured
volume.

> A condition is a piece of measurement before it is a piece of policy, and it
> will be satisfied in whichever way turns out to be cheapest. Our interest is
> in which way that is.
>
> --- Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Demo Quarter, School of
> Continuous Improvement

The study pairs a weekly census of every tap board with the venues' own delivery
and return dockets, and treats the two ways a line can end as competing outcomes
rather than as one. "It sits comfortably beside what the University has been
learning about the difference between a stock and a flow," said Dr Marek
Solheim, Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities.

The release extends a steady institutional interest in the settings by which
compliance is established, and in the distance between an indicator and the
thing it is trusted to stand for.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.y0voyc.
