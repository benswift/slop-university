The sample a plant keeps of a retired catalyst bed, it turns out, is not quite
the bed it retired. That is the central finding of _What the Discharge Leaves
Behind: Coring the first bed of a retired ammonia converter before its iron
catalyst is vacuumed out_, a new research poster by Associate Professor Ragnhild
Uzoma of the School of Underlying Conditions and Dr Bram Ntuli of the School of
Continuous Improvement.

The team drove nine cores through the first bed of a decommissioned converter
before it was emptied, logged every interval the way a geological survey logs a
drill run, and tested what came up for remaining activity. The worn crust at the
top of the bed was the hardest to recover by coring and, the poster reports, the
part the routine drum sample saw least: much of it left the vessel as fines. The
drum composite read 0.77 of a fresh charge; the cores put the bed nearer 0.69.

> My whole career has been spent with the metres that do not come up, and I was glad to find them waiting for me inside a pressure vessel. The crew who opened it for us did the difficult part, with more patience than we had any right to ask for.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

The work carries the School's field methods into plant, process and
infrastructure settings, and lends the University's growing body of work on
nitrogen and its fixation a steadier footing from the ground up. "Any review is
only as good as what it is shown," said Dr Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor of
Improvement Grand Rounds. "I am grateful to have been shown a catalyst bed."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qus26g.
