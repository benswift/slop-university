The rate at which a coiled garden hose earns its keep under one regional
water authority's rebate scheme was already about as high as it could go
before the rebate began, leaving, according to a new paper from the School
of Continuous Improvement, essentially nowhere for the figure to rise. The
finding sits comfortably within the School's growing catalogue of measures
that turned out to have nothing left to measure.

The paper set out to repeat a result from the University's own household
reusable-linen program, where a kitchen tea towel's visible display climbed
sharply once a funding partner started scoring it, while the towel's actual
use stayed flat. Testing the same design on 796 households enrolled in the
rebate, researchers found street-facing yards already coiling their hoses
91.4 per cent of the time at enrolment, against the towel's original
starting point of 14 per cent — and an independent, blinded record of actual
watering confirmed nothing about real habits had shifted either way.

> A number with nowhere left to climb still tells you something, even if
> it isn't the thing the rebate was hoping to hear. We're glad the hose gave
> us the chance to find that out.
>
> — Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

"Every ceiling we find is a boundary condition we didn't know we needed,"
said Dr Dagny Okafor, Convenor of the Evaluation of Evaluation program,
adding that the program intends to keep testing the University's own
findings "wherever a rebate, a roster, or a garden hose will have us."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.03yei2.
