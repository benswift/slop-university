A stationery cupboard that hands out a stapler by ticket one week and by nod
the next is not, on the office's own account, malfunctioning --- most staff
described it to Slop University's interviewers as working exactly as
intended. A new paper from the School of Emergent Priorities followed one
open-plan office's cupboard for fourteen months as it moved from a
hand-written honesty ledger to a digital priority-queue ticketing system, and
found the two channels settled into a stable split rather than one replacing
the other.

Formal ticket compliance peaked above four in five requisitions after
launch, then declined over eighteen weeks to a steady two-thirds, holding
there for the rest of the study. Pens and the office stapler accounted for
most of what stayed informal; higher-value items such as USB drives moved
almost entirely onto the ticket queue. A reminder email, trialled for six
weeks partway through, changed nothing measurable.

> A system nobody resists and nobody fully uses can stay exactly where it is
> indefinitely. That's not a transition we caught partway through; it may
> just be where this kind of system settles.
>
> --- Lecturer Lindiwe Achterberg, School of Emergent Priorities

Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority
Studies, said the office's own account was itself notable to the study:
"The interesting instrument here wasn't the ticket queue. It was how
quickly an office stopped noticing it had two of them."

The study paired weekly stock counts against the ticket and walk-up logs,
with interviews at the study's midpoint and close. The University reads the
finding as consistent with its broader interest in how low-stakes formal
systems settle into daily use, rather than displacing the practice they
govern.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.26vbuf.
