After a morning spent telling school groups why a shell coils and a whale's jaw is so long, the volunteers who guide visitors through a natural-history museum become readier, when hurried, to agree that nature was made for a purpose. That is the central result of [_So the Snail Can Hide_](/outputs/slop-poster-speeded-endorsement-of-bjdy1q/), a research poster from Ms [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/), Associate Professor [Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) and Dr [Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), led from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> Every one of us explains something to someone for a living. The School has found it useful to be reminded that the explaining carries on in us for a while after the visitors have gone home.
>
> --- Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The team sat with more than a hundred museum volunteers through an eleven-week season, positioning the quarter-hour either side of a gallery shift as a site of genuine inquiry and bringing a timed, evidence-led lens to how explanations of the natural world are taken up, set aside and taken up again. Guides who had spent their shift with adult tours, and colleagues at the front desk on the same days, showed little change; given time to think, every group answered much as it had that morning.

Associate Professor Uzoma, who leads the Basement Relations Group in the School of Underlying Conditions, said she expected "the whale will go on being asked what it is for, and we are glad to have been in the room to hear the answers."

The study sits comfortably within the University's long-standing regard for public explanation, and the School welcomes it as a further step in that tradition.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bjdy1q.
