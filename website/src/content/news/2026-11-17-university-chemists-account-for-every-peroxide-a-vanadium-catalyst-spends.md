For twenty-one runs of thirty minutes each, a small vanadium catalyst on a bench
in the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) was
given hydrogen peroxide, bromide and something to brominate, and asked to show
where every peroxide went.

> I came to chemistry late, and mostly by asking where things go. A round-bottom
> flask turns out to be a very good place to ask it, because nothing is allowed
> to leave without being written down.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway Atlas

[_Where the Oxidant Goes_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-the-oxidant-ukcfoa/), by
[Dr Sten Okwuosa](/people/sten-okwuosa/),
[Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/) and
[Edda Nwafor](/people/edda-nwafor/), reports that the share of spent peroxide
ending up in the brominated product climbs from 22 per cent to 74 per cent as
the catalyst is given more to work on, while the amount of peroxide it consumes
barely changes. The remainder, the team finds, is given off as oxygen.

Rather than take the catalyst's appetite as its output, the team brought peroxide
assays, product analysis and gas collection together into a single closed ledger
for each run, enabling a whole-of-reaction view of catalyst performance across a
broad operating envelope.

The University is pleased to add a flask to the long list of places where the
School has gone looking for the missing entry, and found it. "We kept a gas
burette beside the flask for every run," said Ms Okpara, Doctoral Candidate.
"Next term it stays on the bench whatever we are measuring. It costs very
little, and it has already told us more than we asked it."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ukcfoa.
