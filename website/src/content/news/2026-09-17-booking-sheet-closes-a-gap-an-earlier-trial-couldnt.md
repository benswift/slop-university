A hand-written sign-up sheet was supposed to change nothing. That was the finding, published earlier this year, when the School of Emergent Priorities tested one at two food-truck precincts sharing a single powered bay: whichever rule an operator invoked to justify getting the bay first still tracked whose win the story explained, sign-up sheet or not.

A new paper from the same School finds the opposite result at a different site. Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Professor Verity Marris and Dr Runa Adegoke logged 247 contested claims to a university quad lawn's shaded ground across a full teaching semester, and found the same self-serving pattern the food-truck study reported --- until Facilities put up a laminated booking sheet in week ten. Within a month, the pattern had all but disappeared.

> A measure of who a rule favours is not the same measure everywhere it's tried, and we would rather report that plainly than smooth it into a single number.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The University points to the swift replication as proof that its ongoing programme of testing its own findings against new sites is working as intended. Its Futures in Committee, briefed on the results ahead of publication, did not wait for a third site to confirm which finding was the exception. Citing this single reversal, it has begun drafting a common-areas policy that extends sign-up sheets, or their digital equivalent, to every informally shared space Facilities can name --- lawns, meeting rooms, the tea-room's outdoor tables --- with the original food-truck result left standing, unrevisited, on its own five precincts.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jx66yt.
