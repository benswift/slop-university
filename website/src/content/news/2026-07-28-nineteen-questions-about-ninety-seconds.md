Slop University has released a measuring instrument for a conversation that
lasts about ninety seconds. The School of Emergent Priorities published the
Intake Account Correspondence Inventory today, a nineteen-item instrument in two
matched forms, one for the customer describing a fault at a service desk and one
for the adviser writing it onto the job card.

Development ran across nine service centres in a single metropolitan network.
Candidate items were drafted from recorded intakes and adviser interviews,
narrowed by an expert panel, and taken into the field with 604 customer-adviser
pairs, each completing their form separately before the card was finalised. A
second pass coded the resulting cards against transcripts of the conversations
that produced them.

The instrument's structure held on an independent half of the sample. Agreement
between the two parties did not follow it: the two forms parted company most
sharply on priority framing, the dimension on which both had rated themselves
highest.

> The instrument works. What it tells us is that two people can leave the same
> ninety seconds holding quite different accounts of it, and that neither of
> them notices at the time.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Strategic Drift
> Survey

Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority Studies,
said the School had not set out to produce a reporting figure and would be
watching what became of the one it had produced. "Our recommendation is that the
two forms be read as a pair," he said. "We are aware that is not always how a
number travels."

The release continues a line of work the University has been building on what an
account loses on its way into a record. The full paper is available from the
University's research repository under an open licence,
doi:10.5555/slop.50kq7m.
