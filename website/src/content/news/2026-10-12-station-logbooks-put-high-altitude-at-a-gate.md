The rule on the wall names a contour, and the people walking up to it have, for
a good many years, been writing down a gate. That is the finding of _Above the
Second Gate: How one station's logbooks moved the start of "high altitude" from
a contour to a gate_, a research poster by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research
Fellow, Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Mr Eivind Onwuka, Doctoral
Candidate.

> We are a school of people who look down. It has done us no harm to spend a
> season looking at a hillside from the side, and I think the hillside was
> civil about it.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster brings archival reading, interview and the School's surveying
capability to bear on a single phrase. Across 1,146 entries in 21 logbooks kept
at one mountain physiology station, the authors record the place at which each
party first describes itself as "at altitude". In the early books that place
wanders over a long stretch of track. In the later ones it gathers at the
second stock gate, where a store shed stands and the station's kit is issued,
and 83 per cent of entries in the last six books begin there or within a marker
of it. The station's rule sheets, through every revision, do not mention the
gate.

That a school founded on rock, soil and groundwater should give a poster to a
word in a visitors' book is, the University considers, a sign of a discipline
settled enough to travel. The authors make no claim about ascent or health; the
poster concerns how a line comes to be somewhere.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.2i115r.
