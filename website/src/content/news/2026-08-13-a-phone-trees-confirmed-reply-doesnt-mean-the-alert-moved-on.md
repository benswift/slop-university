A household's phone lights up, a thumb taps "received," and a street
committee logs one more link in the chain as sound. A new Slop University
paper went looking for what happened after the tap, and mostly could not
find it.

Dr Marit Osayande and Dr Anneke Tolan, of the School of Continuous
Improvement and the School of Emergent Priorities, interviewed 141
households across seventeen street phone trees in three council areas
about the last neighbourhood alert they remembered, and set their accounts
against nine months of each committee's own confirmation-reply logs.
Committees' chain-integrity scores, built entirely from timely "received"
replies, averaged 87%. Fewer than half of the households interviewed
described actually contacting the next house on the list; most who did not
said they had simply assumed an earlier household already had.

> A reply tells you a household read the message. It was never built to
> tell you what they did next, and a committee reporting it as a
> reliability score is answering a question nobody actually asked it.
>
> --- Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard

The finding sits inside the School of Continuous Improvement's running
interest in what a confirmation signal actually certifies, an interest
that has previously turned up the same shape in a delivery courier's
compliance sticker and an escape-room venue's official hint count.

Dr Anneke Tolan, the paper's co-author, said the interviews kept
surfacing the same private arithmetic: "almost everyone we spoke to could
explain exactly why passing it on had felt like someone else's job, that
particular night."

A verified, recently updated contact list made no measurable difference to
whether a chain carried an alert to its end.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8kmxis.
