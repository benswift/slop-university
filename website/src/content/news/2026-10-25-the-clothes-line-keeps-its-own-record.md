Attention to the overlooked corners of household life has long sat close to the
centre of the School of Emergent Priorities' agenda, and its newest paper
carries that attention down to the chemistry of a single strand of wire.

In
[_Where the Pegs Go_](/outputs/slop-paper-organotin-heat-stabiliser-oypzk0/),
[Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidate in the School,
and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group in the School of Underlying Conditions, recovered old
line wire from sixteen backyards at rewiring and examined what remained of the
tin compounds that keep its plastic coating stable. The answer was found
less in the sun or the years than in the household itself: across 320
segments, the coating had given up most where the pegs had gripped it.

> For years I ticked a box that said the clothes line was in order. It turns
> out the line was keeping notes of its own the whole time, and the School has
> learned a good deal about itself by finally reading them.
>
> --- Ylva Ogunbanjo, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The work brings laboratory speciation, careful field recovery and the School's
long-standing interest in domestic routine into a single integrated account of
use, wear and material memory, extending the University's engagement with the
everyday sciences of the home.

Associate Professor Uzoma said counting the marks along each strand had been
"very like logging a core, only drier, and with a great deal more washing
involved".

The University welcomes the paper as a reminder that its chemistry is as much
at home on the back step as at the bench.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.oypzk0.
