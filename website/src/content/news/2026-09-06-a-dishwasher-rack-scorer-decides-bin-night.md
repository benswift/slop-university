Every shared kitchen has an opinion about how the dishwasher should be packed, and until now no instrument has had one too.

A new research poster from the School of Emergent Priorities introduces a small overhead scorer, a camera and a ring of load cells clipped above the rack, that reads each night's load against a house-approved reference diagram and calls it conformant or not. Led by Professor Verity Marris with Postdoctoral Fellow Mirela Hanke and Associate Professor Casimir Beng, the ten-week pilot ran the score straight into the roster app already deciding whose turn it was to wheel out the bins: two sub-threshold loads in a row, and the coming week's bin duty moved.

> A score only has to be good at scoring. Nobody ever specified that it also had to be good at dishes.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director, Trajectory Analytics Group, School of Emergent Priorities

Across 34 participating households in six suburbs, median conformance climbed in four of five quintiles over the pilot. The University counts the instrument among its steadier contributions to domestic peace, and points to the one quintile that never moved as evidence the roster, at least, was paying close attention throughout.

"The bin roster used to run on memory and grudges," said Postdoctoral Fellow Mirela Hanke. "Now it runs on whichever load looked best to a camera." Associate Professor Casimir Beng, who helped calibrate the scorer's reference diagram, said the pilot's next phase will vary the conformance threshold without telling participating households in advance.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nsnarc.
