A model that once attributed nine-tenths of a standing ovation's timing to
where a concert-goer was sitting has been refit at six suburban comedy rooms,
and the seating geometry barely explains anything at all.

The University's Trajectory Analytics Group took STANCE, the seat-level
model of standing-ovation onset a School of Continuous Improvement team
published earlier this year, and asked whether the same account of a room's
sightlines --- floor rake, row pitch, seat stagger --- would predict a
different kind of collective response somewhere sightlines barely vary. Over
41 open-mic nights across six comedy venues, two observers timed how a
room's laughter spread from more than 1,200 punchlines, and the geometric
model's original error more than tripled once it met a flat floor. A second
model, swapping the sightline term for an estimate of who could actually
hear whom, recovered the original's accuracy.

> A model earns its geometry in the room that built it. Ours simply had
> nowhere flat left to explain.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director, Trajectory Analytics Group, School
> of Emergent Priorities

Dr Anneke Tolan, the paper's second author, said the team had not set out
to unsettle a colleague's earlier finding, only to see how far it travelled:
"the honest result is that it barely made it out the door." The University
reads the negative result as its own kind of finding, and notes that the
regional concert association's original running-order instrument is, in the
meantime, being extended to open-mic lineup cards regardless.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1kc3b3.
