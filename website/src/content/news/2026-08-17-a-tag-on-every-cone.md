The School of Emergent Priorities has spent this year putting instruments on
objects that nobody had thought needed them. The newest is the folding yellow
cone that stands beside a wet floor.

_CAUTION: Cone-Aware Utilisation Tracking in Occupied Networks_, a paper
released today, describes a sensing platform built at the School and deployed
for fourteen weeks across three regional shopping centres. Four hundred and
twelve cones carried a tag on the hinge; anchors in the ceiling void resolved
each one to a zone and to whether it was stowed, moving, or standing. The
result is 8,614 deployment episodes, a continuous record of an object that has
until now generated none.

> The School spent a long time deciding whether this was a question worth
> asking, and rather less time than that answering it once the tags were on.
>
> --- Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the Horizon Register

What the record shows is that a cone's working life comes in two kinds. The
middle episode lasts 41 minutes. The longest ran 34.2 days, in a back-of-house
service corridor, and the corridor turns out to carry more standing cones at
any hour than any shopping floor in the study.

The release sits within a longer effort by the University to bring sensing to
the parts of a building that generate no record of themselves, and to make what
is currently attested available to be counted instead. Lead author Petra
Umbile, a Postdoctoral Fellow in the School, noted that "the cleaning and
facilities teams at the three centres carried a tag on every cone for fourteen
weeks and told us whenever one came off, which is most of what made the trace
usable at all".

The recovered trace has been lodged with the Indicator Commons under an
institutional schema, and cone-hours is now carried as a monthly series.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hgk1nh.
