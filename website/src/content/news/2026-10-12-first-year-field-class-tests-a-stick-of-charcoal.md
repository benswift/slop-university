> The students were asked to draw a hole and then to describe it, or else only
> to describe it, and they did both with a seriousness the hole had done
> nothing to earn. Whatever the poster is worth, it is worth that.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The hole in question is one of eight, and the poster is _Draw the Pit First: A
pit-clustered crossover trial of charcoal sketching before soil profile
description_, by Dr Obiora with Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, and Dr Torun
Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied Measurement.

The trial brings a drawing exercise, a description sheet and the School's
first-year field class into a single two-day design, with each pit hosting one
group that sketched before describing and one that did not. Its analysis is
anchored at the level of the pit, on the reasoning that twelve students at one
face have a good deal in common. On that footing, the students who drew first
placed the top of the subsoil closer to one another than those who only looked,
by about a quarter, and most clearly where the soil itself was least decided.

Slop University regards the poster as evidence that its newest school has begun
to examine its own teaching with the instruments it would bring to anybody
else's ground. Dr Ezeigwe said the collaboration had "asked for fewer students
and more holes, which is not a request a methods program is often in a position
to make".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.euj2dt.
