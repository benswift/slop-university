> An officer doesn't think of the waiver as something they do. It's more
> like weather --- it's already decided by the time they arrive.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School
> of Continuous Improvement

Nineteen parking enforcement officers agreed to describe, in their own
words, when they exercise their own judgement on an overstayed car and when
they leave the call to the payment app's automatic once-a-year fine waiver.
A new Slop University paper from the School of Continuous Improvement
checked what they said against twelve months of the same council's
enforcement ledger, and found the two accounts pointing in different
directions.

Researchers interviewed officers across six patrol beats and matched what
each reported about their own overrides against the ledger's record of
overrides and automatic waivers for the same plates over the year, then
tested whether the pattern held once shift length and beat coverage were
accounted for. It did.

Every beat told the same story: officers described themselves as exercising
discretion several times more often than the ledger credited them with,
while the waiver's use concentrated heavily on a small number of frequently
cited plates, a concentration none of the officers recognised when shown
it. "Officers weren't hiding anything," said Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research
Fellow and Convenor of the Review Cadence Observatory. "They just don't
experience the waiver as a decision anyone is making, including the app."

The School of Continuous Improvement takes the finding as a reminder that a
judgement an institution is proud of can go on being described the same
way long after a fixed rule has quietly taken over most of the work behind
it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.r247ik.
