Ask a volunteer curator what a street-library box's donation policy
requires and they can recite it without hesitation. Watch them work a
shift instead, and a new Slop University paper from the School of Emergent
Priorities finds the policy is barely in the room.

Dr Iben Chikere and Dr Anouk Mensah interviewed 26 volunteer curators
across a metro network's 19 boxes and logged 1,204 donations by hand at
eight of them over six weeks, comparing what curators said they checked
against what a shift actually showed. Self-reported adherence to the
written four-point checklist ran ahead of observed adherence at every
step, most sharply for the duplicate check --- 74% of curators said they
checked it, 19% of shifts showed them doing so.

> A policy document is the least useful place to look for what a policy
> has become. The most useful place is whoever is holding the box when the
> queue gets long.
>
> --- Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Nineteen of the twenty-six curators interviewed said they learned the
checklist from an outgoing volunteer rather than the policy document
itself, and the paper traces how faithfully that handover carries the
shortcut along with it. "The four checklist items survive every handover
perfectly," said Dr Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow. "It's what curators do
with them that drifts, one volunteer to the next, and nobody hands over a
memo saying so."

A laminated reminder card fitted to four of the network's boxes made no
measurable difference to what got checked, a result the team reads as
confirming the drift was never a matter of anyone forgetting.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dkvt1q.
