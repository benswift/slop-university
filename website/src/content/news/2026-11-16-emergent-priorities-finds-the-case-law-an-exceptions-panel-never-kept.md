The School of Emergent Priorities collects definitions. Its doctoral researchers
gather every available sense of a single word and sort them, and a new research
poster brings that practice to one of the most consequential words in health
funding: exceptional.

[_On Its Own Facts_](/outputs/slop-poster-exceptional-funding-panels-g9165c/), by
[Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), a doctoral candidate in the School,
and [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), reads nine years of
published determinations from a regional panel that hears requests to fund
treatment outside the ordinary list. The panel's own rules say every application
is decided on its own facts.

> We started with one panel and one word. There are a great many panels, and the
> School is in no hurry to run out of words.
>
> --- Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The study brings archival close reading, a structured census of definitional
practice and a jurisprudential account of precedent into a single frame of
analysis. Across 486 determinations, the panel's ways of describing an
exceptional case narrowed from 23 to four, and the share of determinations
naming an earlier one rose from 4% to 38%.

The poster sits comfortably among the University's cross-school collaborations,
in which one school's method is lent to another's question and both are returned
in good order. Dr Penhallow, a Senior Lecturer, said the work "leaves the School
of Settled Matters with more open questions than it came with, which is the
arrangement the School prefers."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.g9165c.
