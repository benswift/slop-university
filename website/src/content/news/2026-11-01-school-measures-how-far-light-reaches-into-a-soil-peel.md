A soil peel comes away from a pit face the way a label comes away from a jar:
whatever the binder has gripped travels with it, and whatever it has not stays
behind.
[_Cured Only at the Skin_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-deep-the-cgaemq/), a new
research poster by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow, and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, both of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), asks how
far a binder set by blue light gets into each layer before it stops.

> For twelve years I painted these faces by hand and lifted whatever came away.
> It is a quiet pleasure to learn that what came away was telling us something
> the whole time, and I expect the next surveyor down a pit will hear it sooner
> than I did.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The team brought four horizons of a single sandy profile into the laboratory
and built a working curve for each, aligning photochemical practice with the
everyday craft of the field survey. The pale, bleached sand let the light
travel almost seven times as deep as the reddened, iron-stained band beneath
it, which cured to barely a millimetre and a half and tore when the peel was
lifted. A change of initiator, tuned to green light, recovered a good part of
that lost depth.

The poster sits within the School's sustained attention to the ground beneath
the record, and the University counts this kind of patient bench inquiry among
its quieter strengths.

Associate Professor Uzoma said the Basement Relations Group was "pleased to be
working at the surface for a change, and glad of the company".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.cgaemq.
