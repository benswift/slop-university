Eight short intervals of a single drill core have been crushed, sieved and passed through the oven of a gas chromatograph for _Seven Metres Below the Contact_, a new research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions. Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, report that the weathered granite beneath a sandstone cover is at its most surface-active not at the unconformity itself but in the lower saprolite, some seven metres further down.

> A School founded to ask what the surface rests on has found that the rock has views on surfaces of its own. We are glad, in a quiet way, to have the question turned back on us.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

Bringing the instruments of laboratory surface chemistry to bear on the field logic of the borehole, the team traced how the rock's affinity for passing molecules shifts across the transition from cover to weathered basement, and found the strongest gains among molecules that donate electrons to the surface. The work sets a surface-energy baseline beneath the School's wider programme on the contact, and sits alongside its earlier account of where along such a profile the rock gives up the most hydrogen.

The University regards the poster as a welcome widening of what the School's account of the ground can hold, carrying it from the core tray onto the chromatography bench.

"Saprolite has always been the part of the profile we hurried through on the way to rock," said Dr Obiora. "We expect to linger there for some time yet."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qoshai.
