For one semester, a team from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) set the lecture slides of two first-year law subjects beside those of a first-year chemistry course, and then asked the students who sit in all three to explain, in their own words, what happens to salt in a glass of water.

The result is [_A Settled Solution_](/outputs/slop-paper-dissolve-settle-precipitate-qrc7df/), a new paper by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/). Across three rounds of written explanations from 58 combined-degree students and 112 of their chemistry classmates, the team found that the words the two disciplines share do not always stay in the room where they were taught. Where a law subject reached a word first, its meaning travelled with the student, and in one case it held its place after the chemistry course had taught the alternative.

The study brings the University's interest in inherited language into productive alignment with the everyday practice of the first-year curriculum, and the University counts it a welcome addition to the shared work of its schools.

> We owe a great deal to the students who wrote for us three times in a crowded semester, and who were generous enough to read their own sentences back with us afterwards. They have taught the School a good deal about the way it speaks.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Dr Emenike said the collaboration had "left both Schools with more questions about their own vocabulary than they began with, which is a fair return on a semester."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qrc7df.
