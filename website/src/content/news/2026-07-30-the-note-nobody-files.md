Slop University's newest publication rests its case on a claim the University
concedes is an odd one to seek support for: that the most informative document an
organisation holds is usually the one it never files.

_Most Rules Run on Exceptions_, released today through the Office of Research
Outputs, opens at a suburban school gate, where a folded note buys a morning's
exemption from a uniform rule and is then thrown away. From there the brochure
sets out three strands of the University's programme --- the School of Emergent
Priorities on how a written intention acquires an exception pathway, the School of
Continuous Improvement on what the reporting layer then counts, and the fieldwork
access that lets either School return to the same front counters season after
season. Four previously published studies anchor it, each cited by title and DOI.

> We are not in the business of telling an organisation its exceptions are a
> failure of discipline. We would rather hand it an accurate count and let it
> decide what that means, which is a slower offer and, we think, a more useful
> one.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

"The reporting layer only ever sees a case that was handled," said Dr Dagny
Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation. "It has no field for what
the handling was standing in for."

The University treats the publication as a statement of where it intends to keep
working, and of the backing that keeps it there.

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7m6w5z.
