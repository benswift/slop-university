Where a patrol plants its flags in the morning turns out to predict a beach's
safety compliance figure better than anything the swimmers do.

_Between the Flags, Because the Flags Moved_, deposited today through the Office
of Research Outputs, covers 214 patrol shifts across 18 patrolled beaches over a
single season. One state lifesaving service reports the share of swimmers
observed between the flags as its beach-safety compliance indicator. The poster
leaves that figure where it is and puts a prior question to it: how the flagged
area came to be where it is.

Each shift ended with a nine-minute conversation on the sand. Transcripts were
coded by two independent assessors into six placement rationales, settled before
the season opened and not revised afterwards. Crowd-led placement --- siting the
flags around swimmers already in the water --- accounted for 38.3 per cent of
set-ups and returned a mean compliance of 88.3 per cent. Placements sited
against a hazard the captain had identified before arriving returned 61.4.

> Every captain we sat with could tell us exactly why the flags went where they
> did, and none of them was guessing. The awkward part is that the most
> defensible answer on the day is also the one the season's figures reward, and
> nobody arranged that.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor of the Review Cadence
Observatory, said the taxonomy had mattered more to the team than the
percentages did: "six categories is a small enough number to put on a form, and
that is roughly the ambition."

The School's interest in where a measurement's boundary gets drawn, rather than
in what falls inside it, has been running for some years now, and the University
is glad to see it carried onto a beach.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
