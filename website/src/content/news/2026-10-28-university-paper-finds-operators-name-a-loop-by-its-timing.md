The operators of a regional groundwater pumping scheme choose their words for an unsettled control loop by how long it takes to come round, not by what is wrong with it. A pump that swings back within minutes is hunting; a level that takes hours is drifting; the fault behind either may be the same.

That is the finding of [_Hunting, Cycling, Drifting_](/outputs/slop-paper-hunting-cycling-drifting-mqlzzd/), a new paper by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and [Dr Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/), Postdoctoral Fellow in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> Our School began by reading what the ground writes down. The people who look after it keep a record of their own, and it turns out to be every bit as patient.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Behind the paper sits a decade of console notes, read line by line and set against the plant's instrument history, so that operator language and system behaviour can be understood within a single frame. The team followed each of 1,431 mentions back to the loop it described, and traced how the control room's vocabulary reorganised itself when its trend displays changed.

Dr Mensah, whose research follows how institutional language carries forward, said the collaboration had "found a vocabulary that keeps perfect time with the screens it was written beside, which is more than most planning documents manage".

The paper carries the School's long attention to the water table across to the words kept alongside it, a turn the University is glad to encourage.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.mqlzzd.
