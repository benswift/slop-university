Reading a church court's minutes the way a field surveyor reads a paddock,
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), has traced
how one nineteenth-century presbytery divided a river valley between two of its
congregations, and what happened to that division when the ground beneath it
would not keep still.

[_On Which Side of the Creek_](/outputs/slop-paper-where-a-nineteenth-century-95rp6q/)
follows a boundary first fixed along a creek, then held in place by the memory
of the creek's old course after a flood cut it a new one, and finally moved to a
road. Along the way it asks which elders were responsible for the households
living on the line, and finds the answer settled less by where those households
worshipped than by where the boundary happened to fall.

> We spend most of our working lives deciding where one thing stops and the
> next begins, and we are always glad when someone asks who the line was drawn
> for. This time the question was waiting for us on the other side of the
> archive.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings the School's survey sensibility into conversation with the
history of Reformed church government, positioning the congregational boundary
as an instrument of pastoral oversight and situating a single valley's records
within the wider tradition of parish, session and presbytery.

It carries the School's long attention to boundaries out of the soil pit and
into the record room, and the University is pleased to see that attention
travel so far from where it began.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.95rp6q.
