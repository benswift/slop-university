A street's group chat was never designed to need a moderation policy. A new research poster from the School of Continuous Improvement reports that six of them now have one, sorted automatically out of nothing more than what a message says and how it says it.

Dr Torun Ezeigwe and doctoral candidate Hedvig Anyanwu coded eleven weeks of messages from six residential streets against two independent measures — how urgent a message reads, and how civil — then crossed the two into four familiar categories. Messages read as both urgent and uncivil were routed into the one cell set to trigger an automatic posting pause, with the sender's next fortnight of messages held back from the group by default.

> A street doesn't need a committee to know which messages it would rather not see twice. It turns out it can decide that for itself, given the right pair of numbers.
>
> — Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied Measurement

"What surprised us wasn't the mute, it was how quickly people learned to write around it," said Ms Anyanwu, a doctoral candidate at the School of Emergent Priorities. The muted category's weekly share fell by more than two-thirds across the trial, while the streets' own repair-time logs barely moved — the same complaints, the study suggests, arriving in more procedural language rather than being resolved any faster.

The trial's street committee has since adopted the classifier's threshold as the chat's default moderation setting, binding without a manual override outside its annual streetscape review — the latest instrument out of the School's evaluation-ecosystem agenda to move from measuring a street's behaviour to governing it.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.f3fswo.
