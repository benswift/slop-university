When a cell of superfluid helium stops spinning, some of the spin stays behind.
[_What the Cell Still Carries_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-long-remanent-1c2s8i/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), finds that the vortex
lines left pinned to the cell's walls were still there three days later, across
all 24 of the team's spin-downs, and that only warming the cell out of the
superfluid state removed them.

> Our School is used to asking what a thing still carries once everyone who
> made it has moved on. It has been a pleasure to put that question to a cell
> of helium, and to find colleagues so willing to sit up with it overnight.
>
> --- Dr [Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer, School
> of Settled Matters

Working with Dr [Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) of the School of
Underlying Conditions and Ms [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) of
the School of Emergent Priorities, Dr Tredinnick paired a rotating cryostat
with a quartz tuning fork of the kind found in a wristwatch, building a
patient, multi-day picture of residual flow and then testing a graduated
series of warm-ups as candidate pathways back to a clean starting state. The
poster sets that picture within a wider conversation about the conditions
under which an experimental history can be considered closed.

Ms Ekwueme said the project had left her "with rather more questions than
spin-downs, which feels like the right way round."

The poster takes its place within the University's settled conviction that no
measurement begins on a blank page, and that knowing what came before is part
of the work.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1c2s8i.
