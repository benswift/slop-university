Two houses on the same street, scored by the same assessor against the same
seven criteria, can end up on opposite sides of a heritage listing. Which side
depends on a number nobody at the kerb ever sees.

_Listed by Weighting_, deposited today through the Office of Research Outputs,
takes one metropolitan council's heritage study of 612 post-war dwellings and
recomputes its recommendations across the whole region of weightings the study's
own methodology allows. At the published weights the study recommends 214
dwellings. Eighty-four of those are recommended under every admissible
weighting. The remaining 130 are not, and a further 63 dwellings the study
passed over are recommended under some.

> A rubric applied conscientiously can still return a recommendation the same
> rubric would have declined to return on a Tuesday. What we would like put next
> to the recommendation is how much of it the houses actually decided.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group

The paper also re-reads the criteria against each other. A blinded re-assessment
of 96 dwellings by three assessors puts agreement lowest on social significance,
which carries the study's largest published weight and, because almost every
dwelling scores the same on it, the smallest influence on the result.

"The weight vector turns out to describe what the study meant to value rather
than what it ended up sorting on," said Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer in the School
of Emergent Priorities. "That gap is not a fault anyone committed. It is just
not visible from the recommendation."

Slop University regards the exercise as the arithmetic a school owes an
instrument it did not build, and the School has put forward a listing
probability to be published beside each recommendation rather than in place of
it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8xhbum.
