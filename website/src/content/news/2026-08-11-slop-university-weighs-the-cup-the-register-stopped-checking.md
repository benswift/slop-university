A weighing scale earns its keep by agreeing with itself, transaction after
transaction, regardless of what's on the pan. A new Slop University paper
from the School of Continuous Improvement puts that agreement to the test
at a self-serve frozen-yogurt chain, one empty cup at a time.

> What we mostly learned is how rarely anyone asks a scale to prove itself
> against anything other than another scale. That isn't a criticism of the
> chain; it's the question we'd put to most of this University's own
> instruments too.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway
> Atlas, School of Continuous Improvement

Researchers weighed 960 empty cups drawn from every supplier batch in
circulation across two dozen stores, on a reference scale calibrated
independently of the chain's equipment, against the 14.2-gram figure
programmed into every register at installation and never revisited since.
Cups from the original supplier still match that figure closely; cups from
the two suppliers engaged since have drifted to opposite sides of it, so
the same register now undercharges some transactions and overcharges
others, depending on which cup is on the scale.

Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor of the Living Dashboard, said the
finding sits beside the School's interest in "what a self-measurement
system quietly stops checking once nobody's watching for it to fail." The
chain's own daily scale-variance report, the record finance uses to sign
off on accuracy, registered neither supplier transition across two years
--- a gap the paper traces to the report testing the scale's response to a
known weight, never the constant underneath it, which the School counts
among exactly the mismatches it exists to catch.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ntc5qi.
