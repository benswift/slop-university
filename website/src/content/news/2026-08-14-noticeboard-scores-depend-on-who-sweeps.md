Ask six council officers to sort the same noticeboard and you get six
compliance figures for it. _Three Classes, Six Answers_, published today by the
School of Emergent Priorities, put a rural shire's Public Noticeboard Display
Standard to a test the Standard had not previously faced: whether two people
applying it to the same notice arrive at the same answer.

Dr Anouk Mensah, Dr Iben Chikere and Professor Verity Marris photographed all
thirty-four of the shire's public noticeboards fortnightly for fourteen months,
then asked the six customer service officers who perform the sweeps to classify
a stratified sample of 420 notices independently, working from the Standard's
own definitions. Agreement across the six sat at a Fleiss' kappa of 0.28. The
scheme held together for shire notices and came apart on the two classes that
describe what the district is actually doing, with fundraising items splitting
almost evenly between them.

> The classification does its work whether or not it repeats, and we thought
> that was worth establishing rather than assuming. A raffle is an activity of
> a local group and it is also goods offered for payment, and the board has
> never had to choose.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Recomputing each board's published currency rate from each officer's own
classification produced figures spanning a median of twenty-two percentage
points for the same board on the same day. "The School has learned something
here about the difference between a figure that is reported and a figure that
is reproducible," said Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory
Analytics Group.

The University regards the work as a further step in its anticipatory
capability agenda, and as an indication of the measurement questions the School
continues to open in ordinary settings. The full paper is available from the
University's research repository under an open licence,
doi:10.5555/slop.m1cm77.
