The University's earth scientists have published an account of a rock they went looking for and did not find. _The Rock Behind the Reagent_, a paper by Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, of the School of Underlying Conditions, begins from the observation that the fluorine in a modern medicine was once a mineral in the ground, and asks whether a jar of fluorinating salt on a laboratory shelf can still be traced to it.

The authors took down every lot of potassium fluoride held in one synthesis stockroom, twelve in all, the oldest first opened 18.9 years ago. Each was examined for the trace elements by which geologists tell one fluorspar deposit from another, and each was then put to work in the same routine fluorination.

> We treated the shelf as we would a core tray, which is to say we wrote down what was there in the order it was laid, and did not assume the labels were telling us about the ground.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, School of Underlying Conditions

The mineral's signature had not survived the journey. Nine of the twelve lots carried none of the relevant elements at a measurable level. What separated a good lot from a poor one was water, and water followed the years since the lid first came off: yields ran from 88 per cent down to 31 per cent as the jars were found, and between 79 and 90 per cent once all twelve had been dried.

For the University, the paper is an instance of a school asking after the origins of its materials and reporting plainly what came back. Dr Emenike said the work had "given a long-standing bottle the attention its length of standing deserved."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.blgpei.
