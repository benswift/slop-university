A street's watering days are supposed to be arbitrary --- the council's
roster just needs households on either side of a boundary to take turns.
Slop University's newest signage panel, going up on campus screens and
bus-stop panels this week, asks whether arbitrary is the same thing as
inconsequential.

The panel previews an upcoming session of Improvement Grand Rounds, the
School of Continuous Improvement's standing case-review format, built around
a run of neighbouring properties that sit on opposite sides of a council
watering-roster boundary. Where one letterbox's household waters on one day
and the next waters on another, the format treats the boundary itself as the
instrument: whatever separates the two lawns by season's end will be read
against nothing but the day each household happened to be assigned. The
School frames the session as evidence of a wider comfort putting existing
council administration to the test, not just the instruments it builds
itself.

> A watering roster was never built to answer a question. Ours is about to
> answer one anyway.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

"A roster boundary is about as close to a coin flip as an institution ever
hands you," said Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive
Metrics Lab, describing the design as exactly the kind of naturally
occurring cutoff his lab watches for.

The panel closes with pointers to three earlier University studies of what a
shared roster or rule already decides for the people living under it. The
full campaign panel is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.evqrxk.
