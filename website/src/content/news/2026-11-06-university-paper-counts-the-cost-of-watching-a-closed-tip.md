The [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) usually
reads the ground from what a drill brings up. Its latest paper reads it from
what the drilling cost.
[_Who Paid to Watch the Tip_](/outputs/slop-paper-an-economic-history-6q3xtx/),
by [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, follows a single council landfill from the first
monitoring bore drilled beside it to the most recent sampling invoice, and asks
whose budget kept an eye on the water underneath.

> A bore is a hole somebody agreed to pay for. I went looking for the
> agreement, and found it filed in the same folder as the rock, which seems to
> me exactly where it belongs.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The paper brings minute books, trading accounts and contractors' invoices
together into a single expenditure series, positioning the monitoring bore as
a meeting point between public finance and subsurface knowledge. Across
twenty-three bores and five drilling campaigns, it finds that most of the
drilling coincided with years in which the council had an application before a
higher authority, that the general rate has carried the larger share of about
$529,600 since the tip closed, and that 73 of the metres the council paid for
are described in no log.

The study is an economic history written from inside an earth sciences school,
the sort of crossing the University has always trusted its schools to make
without being asked. It extends the Basement Relations Group's long-running
attention to the intervals a borehole leaves unaccounted for, this time on the
invoice rather than in the core tray.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6q3xtx.
