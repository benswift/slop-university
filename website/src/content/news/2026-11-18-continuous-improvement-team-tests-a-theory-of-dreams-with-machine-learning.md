For up to eighteen months, 214 volunteers kept two diaries side by side, one for
the day and one for the night. The School of Continuous Improvement has now put
the pair to work on one of the oldest open questions in the psychology of
dreams: does tonight's dream carry today forward, or does it push back against
it?

[_Learning the Compensatory Dream_](/outputs/slop-poster-learning-the-compensatory-5y4ka4/),
a new research poster by [Dr Thandiwe Solberg](/people/thandiwe-solberg/),
[Dr Dagny Okafor](/people/dagny-okafor/) and
[Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/), suggests that the answer depends on
how long you are prepared to listen. Read a few nights at a time, the dreams
continue the day. Read some forty or more, and the pattern Jung called
compensation begins to show through.

> We have only read the opening chapters of a very long series. The School intends to keep turning the pages, and to see what the series has to say about the reader.
>
> --- Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor, Evaluation of Evaluation

The team brought a contrastive, sequence-aware modelling framework to bear on
a large longitudinal diary resource, positioning two competing accounts of
dreaming within a single, capacity-matched predictive architecture and
aligning both with a pre-registered evaluation pathway.

The University sees in the poster a fitting meeting of two of its quieter
habits: keeping careful records over a long period, and declining to decide
anything from a single night's evidence. Mr Nwabueze said the work had
reshaped his view of a results table: "The bottom row is only the last thing
that happened. It is not always the thing the series is about."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.5y4ka4.
