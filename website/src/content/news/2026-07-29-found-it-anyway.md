Not every indicator is read off a sensor. Some are simply what a person was
willing to click, on a form nobody checks. A research poster released today
by the School of Emergent Priorities asks what a geocacher clicks when the
container in front of them is falling apart.

Surveying 214 active geocachers, the team asked what a finder does with a
container they rate poor. 58 per cent logged it as a standard find. The most
common reason offered, when a reason was offered at all, was reluctance to be
the one who lowers the cache's find rate for other seekers.

> A health indicator that only updates when someone volunteers bad news about
> themselves is not really an early-warning system. It is a compliment the
> last visitor decided to pay the next one.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

Dr Anneke Tolan, who studies how foresight practices scale from pilot
settings, said the streak effect "reads like every informal workaround this
School has catalogued elsewhere: the shortcut survives because reporting the
truth costs the person telling it something, and nobody built in a way to
pay that cost back."

The team fielded an eighteen-item survey through a regional caching forum and
an in-app prompt, asking finders to recall their most recent poor-condition
find and explain, in their own words, why they logged what they logged.
Free-text answers were coded independently by two readers working blind to
the respondent's own streak length.

For a School built on stewarding indicators, a crowd-maintained health score
with no incentive to speak up is exactly what it means to keep watching.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.10u9y9.
