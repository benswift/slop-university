Somewhere between a tocopherol molecule in an oil droplet and an ascorbate molecule in the water around it, an unpaired electron changes hands. A new research poster from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) finds that the surface it has to cross has a great deal of say in when, and whether, that happens.

[_Passing the Radical On_](/outputs/slop-poster-passing-the-radical-ewlnk3/) is the work of [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer, with [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, and [Ms Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of Emergent Priorities. Pairing rapid-mixing spectroscopy with a closely matched set of model emulsions, the team traces the hand-off across five interface chemistries and connects it to longer-horizon stability outcomes. Behind a cationic interface, ascorbate took the radical within half a second. Behind an anionic one, it barely took it at all.

> A school like ours spends most of its time on things passing from one holder to the next. It has been instructive to watch a droplet manage its own handovers, and to learn that it does not always complete.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The University sees in the poster a welcome measure of how far its schools are prepared to reach, and of the care they take once they arrive. "Five interfaces is a beginning rather than a survey," said Dr Obiora. "We would be glad to be told which one we should have tried sixth."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ewlnk3.
