> A ewe at a trough is not waiting for anything we would recognise as a result.
> We tried to keep that in mind for sixty-one days, and the poster is the better
> for our having mostly managed it.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Dr Emenike is lead author of _Who Drank Before You: Successive-drinker intervals
at bore-fed troughs as indirect contact in one sheep flock_, a research poster
prepared with Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, and published this week
by the School of Underlying Conditions. It approaches the question of how infection might move through a flock from the direction of the header tank.

The poster connects animal identification, reticulation and contact-network
thinking across a single mob of 412 ewes and 38,914 drinking visits. At the
trough nearest the tank the typical ewe drank within half a minute of the one
before her; at the far end of the line the gap ran to nearly twelve minutes. The
authors relate that difference to how quickly each trough refills, and are
careful to describe the work as a study of drinking in which no pathogen was
sampled.

The University values a school that can be shown a paddock and come back with
the pipework. Dr Obiora said the fieldwork had "confirmed a long-held suspicion
in the School that the interesting end of any line is the one closest to the
tank".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.77tghd.
