> An archive that never edits a file can still change its mind about what the
> file says, and ours had done so four times without anyone writing it down.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying
> Conditions

[_Where One Star Allele Ends and the Next Begins_](/outputs/slop-poster-where-one-star-os99u4/),
a research poster released today by the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), takes the
lookup table that turns stored genotypes into allele names and treats its
version history as the object of study.

The work was led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) with
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the School,
and [Dr Joost Nwosu](/people/joost-nwosu/), Postdoctoral Fellow in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/). Working
from one unnamed research biobank, the team recovered five historical versions
of the archive's in-house translation table, re-ran all 4,186 stored genotype
files through each, and had two curators code every difference against rules
fixed in advance. Across the four transitions, 645 calls moved, touching 511
samples; none of the stored files was altered.

"The School has spent a decade on what a reading means once its instrument has
been replaced, and this is that question in a freezer," Dr Emenike said. "We
are glad the archive let us ask it of their own records."

The University regards the work as a sign of where its strengths in records
practice can usefully reach, and expects the School to continue its curation
work with holders of long-lived collections. The full
poster is available from the University's research repository under an open
licence, doi:10.5555/slop.os99u4.
