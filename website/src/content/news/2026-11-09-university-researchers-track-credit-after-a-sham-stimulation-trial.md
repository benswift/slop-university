Seventy-two volunteers spent a fortnight training their working memory while wearing a stimulation headset, and at the end of it were told whether theirs had ever been switched on. A new research poster from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) asks what that news did to the way they explained their own improvement.

[_Credit Outlasts the Current_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-long-participants-gjufse/), by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Senior Lecturer, with [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer, and [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/), asked participants to divide the credit for their gain between practice, effort, the device and anything else, before disclosure and for twelve weeks after it. Among those who had received sham stimulation but believed otherwise, the device's share fell from 41 points to 22 within a week of being told, then barely moved, settling at 17. Participants who had guessed sham all along gave it 5.

> Every study eventually tells its participants what happened to them. It has been a privilege to stay with them a little longer afterwards, and to find that the telling is where the interesting part begins.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The work brings the School's interest in how a question comes to be treated as closed into conversation with the laboratory study of expectation, positioning the debrief as a moment of consequence in its own right rather than a formality at the end of a protocol. "There is something here for anyone who has been given the full facts after making up their mind," said Dr Tredinnick.

The University sees in the poster the kind of reach across disciplines it has always hoped its Schools would find, and the kind of patience with an answer that its researchers bring wherever they go.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gjufse.
