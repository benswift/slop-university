Value engineering puts the same two questions to every object it meets: what
is this for, and what does it cost to have it do that? The
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) has now put
them to the University, and the reply is running on the campus screens.

[_Every function costed. Every cost a function._](/outputs/slop-ad-every-function-costed-v7qe3n/)
is a landscape advertisement by [Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/),
Doctoral Candidate in the School, prepared for the University's signage
rotation. It sets the outcome of the annual value review in gold type above a
workshop table on which a lectern has been taken apart, each of its pieces
laid beside a card still waiting for its function.

> Minutes taught me that a meeting's real product is the list of things nobody
> objected to. A value review is the same document kept at a longer table, and
> the University has been generous in letting the School hold the pen.
>
> --- Ylva Onyekwere, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The campaign positions function analysis as a shared institutional literacy,
aligning the cost of each activity with the purpose it is understood to serve
and anchoring that alignment in the language the University uses to describe
itself. Its small print directs readers to three published studies of small
fixtures and what they are for: a coin lock on a shopping trolley, a marker
pen at the coffee counter, and a flag on a coffee cart's pick-up ledge.

For the University, the campaign is a welcome chance to show its working in
public, and the School expects the review to keep returning to the table.

The full advertisement is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.v7qe3n.
