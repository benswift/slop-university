Every room in which repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation is given can be
seen from two places: the doorway, where the clinic's photographer stands, and
the treatment chair, where a patient returns day after day.
[_Behind the Headrest_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-repetitive-tms-r8vd4m/), a new
research poster from the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), sets those
two views beside each other across fourteen suites.

The poster is by [Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu](/people/ingeborg-nwachukwu/), Lecturer
and Convenor, Capability Sprints, with [Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/),
Doctoral Candidate in the School, and [Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/),
Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> We are grateful to the clinic teams who let us in after the last appointment
> of the day and sat in their own chairs, some of them for the first time. The
> rooms looked different to everyone from there.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

Bringing measured drawing, photographic re-staging and the close reading of
architectural criticism into a single frame of reference, the team located where
each clinic's published image was taken and rebuilt the outlook of a patient
held still at recline. The two pictures proved close to opposites: the chair,
the artwork and the window anchor the photograph, while the view from the chair
is given largely to a plain wall and the console beside it.

The University is pleased to see the School's long interest in what happens
after the first visit carried into the built environment. Mr Onwuka said the
study had left the team "with more questions about doorways than we expected,
which feels like the right way round."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.r8vd4m.
