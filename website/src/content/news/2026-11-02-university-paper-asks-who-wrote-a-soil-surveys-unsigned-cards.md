Every soil profile in a survey's archive was written down by somebody, and when
a regional survey's new database insisted on knowing who, more than a third of
its cards could not say.
[_A Hand in the Ground_](/outputs/slop-paper-who-described-this-kupw80/), a new
paper by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in
the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and
[Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/), Lecturer in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), follows the
eighteen months in which the survey's staff gave those cards their authors.

> The survey's former describers gave up a good many afternoons to tell us who
> they had each been, and to tell one another. I am grateful to all of them, and
> particularly to the ones who recognised my cards before I did.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Read through the lens of science and technology studies, attribution emerges
as a practice that moves across a working archive: from colleagues reading one
another's habits of notation around a
table, through a model trained for the typed cards, to a table of offsets that
now travels with every legacy record the survey serves. Of 1,209 unsigned
cards, all but 137 were placed with a named describer; the remainder are
corrected as the work of a pooled one.

Dr Chikere said the School of Emergent Priorities would "be looking at its own
forms with fresh eyes, especially the boxes marked optional".

The University counts the paper among the most attentive accounts yet of how
its field sciences keep their records, and of the people folded into them.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kupw80.
