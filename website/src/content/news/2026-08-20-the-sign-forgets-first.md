For ten weeks, a research assistant walked into forty office kitchens each
morning and photographed the fridge. A paper released today by the School of
Continuous Improvement built the resulting time-stamped record to answer a
question the School's Adaptive Metrics Lab had heard raised, unresolved, at
every floor meeting that has ever discussed a missing lunch: does the
laminated sign asking staff to label their food and leave what isn't theirs
actually do anything.

The paper's answer arrives in two parts. Twenty of the forty kitchens
received the sign; the other twenty stayed unsigned as matched controls.
Across the study's first week, signed kitchens logged a disappearance rate
well under half that of their unsigned pairs. By the eighth week, that gap
had closed to a fraction of its opening size, and it kept closing from
there. A second test, comparing a politely worded sign against a firmly
worded one, found no difference between the two at any point in the study.

> A sign is cheap to install and, we now know, cheap to stop working. What
> was harder to anticipate is how little the wording seemed to matter once
> it was up.
>
> --- Lecturer Marit Osayande, School of Continuous Improvement

The finding sits inside a broader School programme on low-cost governance
instruments --- interventions an organisation reaches for precisely because
they need no budget line or committee. Associate Professor Casimir Beng,
Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab, said the study extends that programme
somewhere the Lab had not yet formally measured. "We keep finding
instruments that work on installation day and are never revisited again,"
he said.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.037d4k.
