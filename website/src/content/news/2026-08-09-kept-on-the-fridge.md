Nine share houses adopted the same kind of chore-tracking app, and none of
them took the whiteboard down afterwards. A new paper from the School of
Emergent Priorities set out to interview residents about the switch and came
back with something closer to a second, unplanned system: the app scheduling
and reminding, the whiteboard quietly kept on as the record a dispute still
gets settled against.

> A plan tells you what a household intended. The board on the fridge tells
> you what actually happened, and residents trust the second document more
> than the first.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Tolan and Dr Iben Chikere interviewed 22 residents across the nine houses,
coding the transcripts to a five-theme account of what the whiteboard offers
that the app apparently doesn't: a visible record of who wrote or crossed out
an entry, a spot in the kitchen nobody can mute, and a faint residue of past
weeks the app's single current screen doesn't keep. A same-session trust
rating backed up the interviews, and a check on whether the app's push
notifications were switched on made no difference at all to how much
residents believed it.

"The nine houses never sat down and divided the labour between the two
systems," said Dr Chikere. "The app took the reminders and the board kept
the record, and nobody had a meeting about it."

For a School that spends most of its attention on foresight documents and
review panels, the study is a smaller-stakes rehearsal of the same standing
question: what a plan is trusted to say, set against what residents already
know a scuffed-up wall can prove.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.z52rhp.
