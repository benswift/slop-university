Every worker who has lived through an office restack knows the afternoon a fixed
number of crates arrives at the desk and the rest of the day is spent deciding
what fits. Hedvig Anyanwu has submitted a doctoral thesis to the School of
Emergent Priorities arguing that this ordinary, time-pressed decision, repeated
across a floor, is where an organisation's priorities are actually made — not in
the plan that preceded it.

Carried Forward, Unopened follows four restacks at three organisations between
2024 and 2026, tracking what workers packed, when a sealed crate was finally
opened, and what survived, unopened, into the move after that. The thesis was
supervised by Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer in the School of Emergent
Priorities, with Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer in the same School, as associate
supervisor.

> A plan tells you what an organisation says it will do. A crate tells you what
> it actually kept room for, which is a different question and, in our
> experience, the more honest one.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The work builds on a procedure Anyanwu calls the packing-day protocol, under
which a unit's priorities for a planning cycle are read off what it packs into a
fixed crate allocation rather than off a separate planning document. The School
of Emergent Priorities has adopted the protocol for its own priority-setting
from the 2027 planning cycle, making Anyanwu's thesis one of the rarer
submissions to leave a mark on the institution examining it before the
examination is even complete.

Dr Chikere, whose own research asks what a handover document keeps of a role
once its occupant moves on, noted that a crate does much the same work without
anyone having to write it down. The thesis is now with examiners; the School
expects a result within the standard turnaround.

The full thesis is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gekpe7.
