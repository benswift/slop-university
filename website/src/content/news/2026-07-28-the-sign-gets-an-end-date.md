Nothing about a temporary detour sign records how long it is meant to stay. It
arrives with an installation date, a work order, and nowhere at all to write
down the day it comes back off the fence.

The School of Emergent Priorities has built much of its research programme on
that missing line: the initiative left in trial status season after season, the
commitment allowed to lapse rather than closed out, the ending nobody was ever
assigned to make. Its newest funded project takes the question out to a
suburb's kerbside, where the same absence is cable-tied to a fence in orange
corflute.

The funded register gives every temporary sign a retirement date the moment it
is installed, logged against the work order that justified it, and returns that
date to the installing crew when it falls due.

> Somebody signs for a sign going up. Nobody signs for it coming down, and that
> turns out to be most of the difference between a temporary arrangement and a
> permanent one.
>
> --- Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Co-grantee Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, whose work follows pilot schemes that never
quite conclude, said the kerbside version of the problem had one advantage over
the paperwork she usually reads: "an extension memo is easy to file and hard to
see. A sign that shouldn't still be there is visible from a passing bus."

The award is $317,564. The School regards it as an unusually literal test of a
capability it has so far only studied on paper.
