For most people who meet a court, the first thing it says is no. A filing is lodged at a registry counter, a clerk finds a defect, and the document goes back across the counter with a slip and a few words of explanation. [_Softly Refused_](/outputs/slop-paper-emotional-intelligence-of-kl2td3/), a new paper by Dr [Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and Dr [Ingrid Vasseur](/people/ingrid-vasseur/) of the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), asks what those few words are worth.

The authors measured the emotional intelligence of 47 counter clerks and followed 3,108 of their refusals to the documents' next visit. Clerks most skilled at managing a filer's feelings were rated warmest at the counter, yet filers without a lawyer were less likely to return with the defect cured, chiefly because the gentlest refusals named the problem least plainly. Clerks who read emotions well, and checked they had been understood, did better.

> Settled Matters has spent a long time with the documents that come back. This paper is the School finally turning round to look at the people carrying them, and we have learned a good deal about our own habits of attention in the process.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer and Lead, Received Readings Group

The study places the School's longstanding engagement with procedure alongside a growing body of workforce psychology, connecting performance-based assessment with observational and administrative data across a multi-site service environment, and the University regards it as a welcome addition to its work on how institutions speak to the public. Dr Vasseur said the collaboration had been "a reminder that every counter is also a meeting, and that the minutes are kept by whoever comes back".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kl2td3.
