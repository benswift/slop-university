> None of this would have been possible without the landholders who let us dip their bores every week for three years, in the wet and in the dry. The detector was underground, but the study was up there with them.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

[Dr Nwachukwu](/people/ingeborg-nwachukwu/)'s new research poster,
[_Perihelion in the Wet Season_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-groundwater-driven-radon-6xtea3/),
prepared with [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer,
and [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, both of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), brings the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) into
neutrino physics, and brings the water table in with it.

The poster follows a small liquid-scintillator prototype in a disused mine drive
alongside the monitoring bores on the surface above it, aligning a detector
record, a hall-air record and a groundwater record into a single seasonal
picture. Its central observation is that the radon carried into the hall by the
wet season peaks within weeks of the Earth's closest approach to the Sun, close
enough to nearly double the annual modulation the detector would otherwise
attribute to its orbit. In the one dry year of the record, the excess
disappears.

The work takes its place in a growing strand of University research that treats
the ground around an instrument as part of the instrument.

"Every season we ran it, the rock told us something new about the calendar," said
Dr Emenike. "We are grateful to have been listening."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6xtea3.
