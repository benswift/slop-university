Looking a matter up one more time can leave a reader less certain of it, even when the record has not moved. That is the central result of [_Checked Back Open_](/outputs/slop-paper-repeated-checking-of-z3900v/), a new paper from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) led by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Senior Lecturer, with [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer.

In the study, 128 students learned the status of a set of invented court decisions, then checked a handful of them again and again in a mock legal research database. Their recall stayed accurate throughout. Their confidence did not, and the students who checked most came to think the cases themselves were more likely to have changed. Those higher in obsessive-compulsive traits showed the effect most strongly.

> Every reader of a law report learns to ask whether a case still stands. We wanted to watch that question being asked, and the School will carry what it saw into the way it teaches legal research.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Set at the meeting point of legal method and the psychology of doubt, the paper situates a familiar professional habit within a wider account of how certainty is reached, held and, on occasion, given back. Dr Tredinnick described the invented casebook as "a small, fixed world in which the participants' own uncertainty had room to show".

The University is glad to see its newest School take its reading of the record into the laboratory so early in its life, and regards the paper as a fair measure of the range the School has already found.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.z3900v.
