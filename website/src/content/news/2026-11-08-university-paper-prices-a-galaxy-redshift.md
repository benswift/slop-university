For most of the second half of the twentieth century, the price an observatory
paid to learn how fast a galaxy was moving away from us hardly moved at all, and
then, several times over, it dropped at once.

That is the central finding of
[_Nights per Galaxy_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-price-of-3fnaq8/), a new paper by
[Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), and
[Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral Candidate in the School of
Emergent Priorities.

> The School has only just begun to read beyond the law reports, and it seems
> right that one of its first stops should be somewhere as patient as an
> observatory's archive.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Reading an observatory's night ledgers alongside its plate-store accounts and
the minutes of its time-allocation committee, the authors align four
generations of instrumentation with the institution's evolving stewardship of
the night sky, and trace how a committee's rules kept pace with an instrument
that could observe hundreds of galaxies at a time.

Ms Balogun said that her months among the Observatory's old price lists had felt
"like a homecoming, with better views".

The University is glad to see a law school's habits of reading turned towards
the sky, and expects them to travel further still.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3fnaq8.
