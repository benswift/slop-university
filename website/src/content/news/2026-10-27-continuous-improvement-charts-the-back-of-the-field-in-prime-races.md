For seven years, Eskil Nwabueze compiled the
weekly results of a district cross-country league. His new paper,
[_Who Runs Last_](/outputs/slop-paper-last-place-in-bvyhp8/), brings that
familiarity with the foot of a results sheet to one of the oldest contests in
mathematics: the race between the primes as they fall into different residue
classes.

Mathematicians have long watched the front of that race. Mr Nwabueze, a
doctoral candidate in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), watched
the back. Working through every prime below one billion across five moduli, the
paper maps which class holds last place, how long it stays there, and how often
the bottom of the table changes hands, and it finds that for some moduli the
last-placed class settles in early and simply stays.

> The bottom row of a table keeps its own company, and I have been glad of that
> company for most of my working life. It turns out the primes keep that row as
> faithfully as any club I have known.
>
> --- Eskil Nwabueze, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The paper extends a line of inquiry the School has been building for some time,
in which every row of a ranked list is treated as worth the same attention as
the first. It sits alongside Mr Nwabueze's doctoral work on what community sport
clubs do about last place, and places the School's measurement and process
interests in conversation with a field that has been counting carefully for
well over a century.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bvyhp8.
