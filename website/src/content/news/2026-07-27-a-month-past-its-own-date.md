Sixty-eight per cent of the auto-replies audited in a new Slop University
research poster were still firing a month after the date printed inside
them, and the reason a respondent gave for the gap tracked how long the
reply had already been running rather than how much longer it would run.

The poster, from the School of Emergent Priorities, interviewed 156 staff
across nine organisations once each auto-reply had run at least three days
past its own stated return date, then checked every account against the
reply-log timestamp behind it. Five reasons recurred often enough to code
into a taxonomy --- forgetting, a deliberate buffer, an inbox nobody
clearly owns among them --- and the category a respondent reached for
predicted the overrun already banked, not the one still to come.

> An ownership-unclear inbox doesn't forget to turn the reply off. It just
> never had anyone whose job that was.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director, Trajectory Analytics Group, School
> of Emergent Priorities

Deliberate-buffer replies closed fastest of the five, precisely because
somebody had chosen to leave them running rather than losing track of
them. "People assume the tidy answer is also the quick one," said Dr Iben
Chikere, Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities and a co-author.
"Here the opposite category wins, because somebody is actually deciding
it."

The University regards the taxonomy as a natural addition to its wider
anticipatory-capability agenda, alongside its existing account of
commitments that keep running well past the conditions that first
justified them.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.0tynio.
