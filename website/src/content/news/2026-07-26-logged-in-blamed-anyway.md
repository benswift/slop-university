Slop University's newest research poster spent eighteen weeks giving three
shared-office fish tanks something the paper roster beside them never had:
an exact, timestamped record of who actually pressed the feed button. It
has not, in the end, given the office much else.

The poster, from the School of Emergent Priorities, fitted a badge-tap
logger beside the tank in three shared offices, timestamping every feed
against the badge holder across 54 rostered weeks while the paper roster
on the wall stayed exactly as it was. The logged presser matched that
week's rostered name in 61 percent of weeks; when the two disagreed, the
roster was corrected to match the log in only 8 of 21 cases, and left
standing in the rest. Feeding gaps --- weeks with no confirmed feed at
all --- held at roughly one in eleven both before and after the logger
went in.

> A timestamp is not an argument on its own. We more or less built this
> study assuming it might be, and it turns out a good record just gives
> two people a better exhibit to keep pointing at each other with.
>
> --- Postdoctoral Fellow Petra Umbile, School of Emergent Priorities

Senior Lecturer and Director Torun Ezeigwe, of the Master of Applied
Measurement, said the log sheet's own drift into handwritten annotation
was the most familiar part of the whole study: "an instrument earns
exactly as much trust as the last person who disagreed with it was
willing to give it."

The University reads the result as confirmation of something its
evaluation-ecosystem agenda has argued for years: a record only settles a
dispute once somebody agrees to be bound by it. The full research poster
is available from the University's research repository under an open
licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4pdxyt.
