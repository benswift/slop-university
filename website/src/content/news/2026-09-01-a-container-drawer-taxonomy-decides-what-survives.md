Every share house appoints someone, formally or not, to make the container drawer close. A new Slop University research poster from the School of Emergent Priorities gives that role a name and a protocol, and reports what happens once its classification starts making the disposal decisions instead of merely describing them.

The team followed three share houses' rostered custodians through a fourteen-month cycle, sorting the contents of shared container drawers into a four-part scheme --- matched pairs, orphaned lids, orphaned bases, and the near-matches households keep on the strength of resemblance alone --- and tracking which category actually left the drawer at each cull.

Containers classed as aspirational near-matches proved the drawer's most durable residents, surviving culls at a markedly higher rate than pairs with a documented history of use. The School of Emergent Priorities is treating the finding as grounds to formalise the custodian's classification as the basis for a household's disposal decisions, rather than leaving it to whoever happens to be rostered on that fortnight.

> A container doesn't have to be used to be believed in. It only has to look like it's nearly the one you're missing.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The School counts the taxonomy among the quieter proofs that a household's own administration rewards the same instruments the University studies everywhere else.

The full research poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qzcrke.
