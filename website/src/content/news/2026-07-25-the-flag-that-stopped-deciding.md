> A flag that never fails isn't information any more --- it's furniture. The
> only flags worth reading are the ones people sometimes ignore.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School
> of Continuous Improvement

Slop University has published a new research poster tracking what happens
after a desk-level "do not interrupt" flag goes up in a shared office ---
not whether colleagues stop knocking, but how long the flag actually holds
them off. The poster, from the School of Continuous Improvement, followed 46
desks across six open-plan floors for four weeks, an even split of
flag-issued and matched unflagged desks logging every walk-up interruption
as it happened.

The team paired each flag-issued desk with an unflagged control on the same
floor, then cross-checked colleagues' own walk-up diaries against short
observer spot-checks to see whether the flag's early effect on interruption
counts held, narrowed, or reversed as the deployment wore on. It narrowed
sharply: by the fourth week, flagged desks were logging interruptions at
close to the unflagged rate, even as the way those interruptions opened kept
changing.

"The flag didn't stop working," said Dr Dagny Okafor, Lecturer and Convenor
of the Evaluation of Evaluation. "It just stopped being the thing that
decided whether someone interrupted, and became the thing they apologised to
on the way past."

The finding extends the School's evaluation-ecosystem agenda into a signal
that was meant to need no evaluation at all: a flag either up or down, doing
exactly what it visibly says.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.o17aeb.
