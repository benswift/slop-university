Five fill volumes, one carcinoma cell line and a row of small sensors on the
floor of a culture plate form the basis of a new paper from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), carried out
with the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

[_How Deep the Medium Stands_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-deep-the-hutt9a/), by
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, and
[Mr Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), Doctoral Candidate, finds that
a well described as normoxic is not always normoxic at the bottom. The deeper
the medium stands over the cells, the less oxygen reaches them, and under the
deepest fill the line takes on much of the hypoxic, glycolytic character
usually credited to the tumour itself.

> We expect a few laboratories to look at their pipettes a little differently
> this week. Any questions that follow are very welcome, and we suspect we will
> be asking some of them ourselves.
>
> --- Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The study brings the School's field expertise in measurement at depth into
conversation with laboratory cell biology, aligning sensor-based monitoring,
protein analysis and metabolic profiling within a single, carefully held
experimental setting. Its recommendations on what a methods section should
record are offered to the wider research community in a spirit of shared
practice.

The paper takes its place among the University's contributions to a more
attentive research culture, one well at a time. "The next step is to find out
which of our own habits we have been measuring all along," Dr Emenike said.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hutt9a.
