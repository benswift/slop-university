Two hundred and fourteen people were handed the same overfull drive and the
same instruction to make room. Half could undo what they deleted for thirty
days; half could not. Both groups made the room. They did not throw away the
same things.

[_Deleted, For Now_](/outputs/slop-poster-a-quota-constrained-gmcvn6/), a new
research poster from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), reports that a
recovery window moves the point at which a file is judged safe to discard.
Participants with a way back deleted drafts and photographs they had rated as
likely to be needed, and in follow-up sessions a quarter or more of the files
they went looking for were retrieved from the trash rather than from where they
had been filed.

> Every School keeps a folder of things it has decided to stop doing and has
> not yet thrown out. We recognised ourselves in the participants rather sooner
> than we expected to.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

The study situates everyday file-clearing within a wider decision-sciences
conversation about reversibility, anticipated need and the quiet emergence of
informal archive tiers, and it extends the School's sustained attention to how
commitments are made, deferred and eventually honoured. The University counts
this line of work among its most patiently consequential.

Doctoral Candidate Hedvig Anyanwu, who co-led the retrieval sessions, said the
questions the team had fielded so far were "mostly about people's own trash
folders, which is exactly the conversation we hoped the poster would start".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.gmcvn6.
