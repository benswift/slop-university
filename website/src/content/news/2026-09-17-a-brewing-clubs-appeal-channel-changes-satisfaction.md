A disputed placing at a suburban home-brewing club's monthly judging night used to end wherever the rostered judge's patience did. Since a new case study from the School of Continuous Improvement, it more often ends at a review panel instead.

The paper follows the club's staggered rollout of MASH, the Member Appeal and Steward Hearing procedure: a minuted appeal window, an independent steward panel, and a right of resubmission, introduced to the club's three judging categories one at a time across a season. Reported satisfaction with a placing rises the round each category gains the channel, and a parallel blinded re-judging exercise finds no matching gain in how accurate a placing actually is. The researchers read the two results together as evidence that a formal hearing can carry its own weight, separate from anything it corrects.

> A committee that gives people somewhere to be heard doesn't need the hearing to change anything.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds

The case closes with a teaching note for use in committee-governance instruction, and with what the researchers regard as its more consequential detail: the club's Steward Committee has already recommended MASH's four-part design as a model constitution clause for any Slop University-affiliated club, with no exemption for a membership roll small enough that an appeal is unlikely to be lodged in an ordinary season. "It's the kind of finding that keeps landing on our own governance agenda long after the season it was studied in ends," said Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.u72nta.
