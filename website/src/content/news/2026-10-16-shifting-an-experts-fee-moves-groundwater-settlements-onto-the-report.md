When a regional groundwater tribunal changed who meets the cost of its appointed
expert, the disputes before it did not go away. They began to end on the day the
expert's report arrived. That is the central finding of _Who Pays the
Hydrogeologist? Expert-fee shifting and settlement in a regional groundwater
tribunal_, a new research poster from the School of Underlying Conditions by
Dr Vigdis Emenike, Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist and Dr Brynjar Obiora.

> I spent a long time at the wrong end of a dip-meter tape, never once asking
> who would be sent the invoice. Our thanks go to everyone who kept the
> registry's files in such patient order, because they made the question
> answerable.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The poster brings the School's long working acquaintance with bores and
aquifers into conversation with the economic analysis of dispute resolution,
setting eight years of tribunal practice against a comparison group of matters
the rule change left untouched. Across 603 bore-interference matters, it
reports fewer filings, a marked shift of settlements into the weeks after the
expert's report, and agreed sums drawing close to what that report implies.

The work extends a collaboration across two schools that the University
regards as one of the quieter strengths of its research culture, in which the
habits of fieldwork and the habits of modelling are asked to share a page.

Associate Professor Lindqvist said the project had altered how he thinks about
the costs an institution chooses to place, and on whom. "A fee looks like an
administrative detail until you follow it through a case file," he said. "Then
it looks rather more like the case."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.d85w2x.
