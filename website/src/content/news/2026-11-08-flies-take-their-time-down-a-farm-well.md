Forensic entomology is the latest field to be read from below the surface at the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and the
University is glad to see the School's long acquaintance with shafts, collars and
lids put to a question about time.

[_How Far Down the Flies Will Go_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-far-down-g0icna/), by
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/),
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) and
[Dr Solveig Adeyemi](/people/solveig-adeyemi/), follows small carcasses lowered to
five depths in twelve disused farm wells across three seasons, and asks how long
blow flies take to find them.

> A well has always been a place where a reading is taken a long way from where
> anyone can see it. I am pleased the School now has a reading that climbs back
> out on its own.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Combining down-hole observation, temperature logging at depth and time-to-event
analysis in a single field protocol, the team traces how a vertical shaft and its
lid shape the arrival of the first flies, and how that arrival flows through to
the minimum postmortem interval an investigator would later read from the
larvae. The poster finds that the wait grows from a few hours at the collar to
more than two days at nine metres in an open well, and longer again under a lid.

Dr Adeyemi, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the Review Cadence Observatory in
the School of Continuous Improvement, said the collaboration had been "a chance
to spend time with a record that is written before anyone arrives to write it,
and to think about who reads it next."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.g0icna.
