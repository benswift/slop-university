Naming a target is the easy part; knowing what it has committed anyone to is slower work. [_What the Milestones Settle_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-much-of-69qgx2/), a new research poster from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), brings the tools of pure mathematics to that slower work, asking how much of a pathway's total emissions a schedule of interim milestones actually decides.

> Every schedule of targets will be read by someone who was not in the room when it was set. The School would like that reader to know where the firm ground is, and we expect our colleagues will have a good deal to say about where the firm ground should be.
>
> --- [Associate Professor Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/), School of Settled Matters

Combining a careful reading of what milestones say with an equally careful reading of what they leave unsaid, the team has established a clear framework for positioning interim commitments along a decline, and for understanding the value of a commitment to steady deceleration. On the poster's model decline, four milestones held to that commitment settle more than forty-two evenly spaced milestones held to none.

[Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, in the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), described the collaboration as "a chance to sit with a familiar kind of schedule until it told us something new". He was joined on the poster by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer in the School of Settled Matters.

The poster adds to a body of University work in which close arithmetic is offered in service of shared ambition, and in which the School of Settled Matters continues to find that the question of what has been decided rewards patient attention.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.69qgx2.
