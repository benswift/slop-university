Leaving a queue and rejoining it should never make the wait shorter. At one
shopping-centre car park's electric-vehicle charging bank, a new Slop
University paper finds it reliably does, and that the bank's regular drivers
worked out the trick largely on their own, no operator disclosure required.

The result belongs to a small, awkward category the School of Emergent
Priorities keeps returning to: an instrument that is not wrong so much as
increasingly well understood by the people it describes.

> A number that is accurate every time a driver looks at it can still settle
> to something misleading in aggregate, once enough of them have learned
> which way to look.
>
> --- Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The display quotes a driver's wait from one of two formulas depending on how
they arrive: a cautious estimate for anyone already standing in the queue,
and a shorter, average-case estimate for anyone freshly checking in. Over
eleven weeks, the share of drivers leaving and re-entering to claim the
shorter number climbed from one in thirty to one in four, and the bank's
publicly posted average wait drifted from tracking reality closely to
understating it by nearly seven minutes.

"Nobody involved is breaking a rule," said Associate Professor Casimir Beng,
Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab. "The queue simply stopped being described
by the number that was supposed to describe it."

A banner explaining the mechanism, trialled at half the bank's queue
positions from week six, left the rejoin rate unchanged.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.cddmt8.
