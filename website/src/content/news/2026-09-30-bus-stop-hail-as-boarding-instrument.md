The School of Continuous Improvement has added the hail to the list of ordinary kerbside gestures it is prepared to treat as a published kind. A research poster led by Lecturer Dr Solveig Adeyemi, with Postdoctoral Fellow Dr Mirela Hanke and Lecturer Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, names four hail kinds at the suburban bus stop and records what the campus shuttle will now do with them.

> The arm is the detail that is still there when the bus commits. Everything else at the stop is dropped before the door.
>
> --- Lecturer Dr Solveig Adeyemi, School of Continuous Improvement

Twelve suburban stops were filmed from the kerb for sixteen weeks, faces discarded, and every arriving service coded against four kinds frozen before week one: the half-step, the committed arm, the phone-blind, and the already-turning. Of 3,847 coded hails, the committed-arm kind is 31 percent of events and 74 percent of successful boardings. From week nine, a stop-only-for-committed-arm rule at six stops cut the half-step share from 28 percent to 6 percent; phone-blind hails were skipped at 81 percent. Control-stop shares held.

The release confirms the University's willingness to let a kerbside gesture set a term-long entitlement. The campus shuttle is instructed to stop only for the committed-arm kind. A rider whose modal hail in weeks 1 to 4 is any other kind loses the stop for the rest of term, with no appeal, and next teaching period's shuttle pass is issued from that modal kind alone.

Dr Hanke said the School can now issue the pass from a kind rather than a request. "The pass follows the arm that was already doing the work."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.o0l2xc.
