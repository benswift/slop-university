Ask a funded festival how many people came and you will get a number. Ask nine
of them how they arrived at it and you will get nine answers. _Nine Ways to
Count a Main Street_, published today by the School of Continuous Improvement,
takes one state regional-events fund's acquittal record and asks what its
central figure is a count of.

The team read the method statements attached to 78 acquitted grants, sorted them
into nine classes, and then went to 41 of the events with a counting protocol of
their own. The acquitted figure came in above the researchers' estimate at 35 of
the 41, by a median factor of 1.88. Which method a committee used explains 88.1
per cent of the difference; how big the event was explains 3.1 per cent.

> Nobody in this study did anything wrong. The form asked for a number and every
> committee gave it one. What the form never said is what it wanted counted, and
> the nine reasonable readings of that word are now sitting in a ranking that
> treats them as the same.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

Recomputed on a common count, the spread in cost per attendee across methods
narrows from more than threefold to a little over one. Fourteen of the 41 events
change sides of the threshold the fund uses to decide who is invited back.

"A ranking is only as portable as the instrument underneath it, and this one was
chosen locally and inherited annually," said Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral
Fellow. "We would rather that were known than corrected in a hurry."

The University regards the study as a plain application of the measurement
discipline its Schools were established to practise, and welcomes the attention
it may draw to the boxes on other forms. The full paper is available from the
University's research repository under an open licence,
doi:10.5555/slop.67ay7v.
