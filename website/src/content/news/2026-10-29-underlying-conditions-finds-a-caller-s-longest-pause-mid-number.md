The silence that tells an automated phone agent a caller has finished is, on a
utility's after-hours fault line, at least as likely to fall in the middle of a
meter number as at the end of a sentence. That is the central finding of
[_Cut Off Mid-Address_](/outputs/slop-poster-end-of-turn-prediction-for-md27qy/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

The poster is led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow, with [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior
Lecturer, and [Dr Fenna Okoro](/people/fenna-okoro/), Senior Lecturer and
Convenor of the Horizon Register in the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

> A soil surveyor spends a career deciding where one thing ends and the next begins. A telephone, it turns out, asks the same question several times a second, and is a good deal less patient about the answer.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The work brings together close listening, conversational timing and the
design of automated service into a single account of how a spoken turn is
assembled, pause by pause. Across 2,964 recorded calls, the team found that
callers reading out an address or a meter number routinely stop for longer
than the agent's fixed waiting time, and are cut off for it. An agent that
takes its own question into account interrupts them far less often, without
keeping anyone else waiting.

The University counts the study among the more unexpected yields of its
habit of doing fieldwork wherever the ground happens to be, including at the
bottom of a meter pit with a phone held to one ear. Dr Okoro said the project
had been "a reminder that a line can be at its busiest while nobody is
speaking".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.md27qy.
