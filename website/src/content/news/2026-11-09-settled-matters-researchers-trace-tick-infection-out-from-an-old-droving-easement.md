The ticks that leave a disused droving easement for the paddocks beside it grow scarce within a few tens of metres of the fence, but the infection they carry falls away far more slowly. That is the central finding of [_Fewer Ticks, Not Cleaner Ones_](/outputs/slop-poster-questing-tick-density-h0i9vc/), a new research poster by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer in the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> A boundary is only ever as tidy as the map that draws it. Walking this one with a drag cloth has given the School a new respect for everything that crosses a fence without asking.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Bringing twenty-four field transects, three seasonal sampling rounds and an integrated molecular screening workflow into a single line of sight, the study aligns landscape-scale observation with laboratory confirmation to build an evidence-led account of how a corridor's influence extends into the working land around it. Fifty metres into grazed pasture, the team found a tenth of the corridor's nymphs, carrying three-quarters of its rate of infection.

The University counts the poster among the first fruits of a School that was founded to read the law of the land and has lost no time in walking it.

"Every transect begins at the same fence and ends somewhere new," said Dr Obiora. "That is about as good a description of collaboration as I have come across."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.h0i9vc.
