Twelve holes, each a metre deep, were drilled a short distance behind a trench
wall whose contents had already been written down. What came up was then
compared with what was known to be there. That arrangement is the whole of the
apparatus behind _The Selection Function of a Drill: Completeness-Corrected
Upper Limits on Biosignature Carriers from Incompletely Recovered Core_, a paper
by Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Associate Professor and Lead, Basement
Relations Group, and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, of the School of
Underlying Conditions.

> A tray of core is a very calm object. We hope some of that calm has found its
> way into the paper, and that readers will feel free to leave it there.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The paper aligns a field-logging capability with an exposure-accounting
framework taken over from survey astronomy, bringing recovery, recognition and
confidence into a single reporting posture. The drill returned 72.6% of the
ground overall and 38% of the laminated mudstone; of the 148 thin dark layers
the team was counting in that mudstone, 23 arrived in the tray. A search that
found none, the authors conclude, would be entitled to say roughly six times
less than its drilled length suggests.

The School's work has always begun from the surface and proceeded downward, and
the University is glad to see the same care now applied to the journey back up.
Dr Obiora said the wall had been "a cooperative colleague throughout, and asked
for very little in return".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.eygznx.
