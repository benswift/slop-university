Every soil map is also a network, and the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) has now
worked out how few places a surveyor must dig before every unit on it can be
told from every other.

[_How Few Pits Fix a Map_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-few-reference-wgzh5g/), a
research poster by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/),
[Dr Mirela Hanke](/people/mirela-hanke/) and
[Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), brings exact optimisation to 212 archived
survey sheets and reports that the smallest answer depends less on the size of
a map than on the small units held inside larger ones.

> These sheets were drawn by field teams who never expected anyone to read them
> this closely. I hope they would recognise their own ground in the result,
> and I owe them that.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The work brings the School's long-standing attention to where one soil ends
and the next begins into productive alignment with the computational settings
of the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/),
translating the survey record into a resource for decision-ready field
planning across the whole landscape-to-sheet continuum.

The University counts the poster among the clearest instances yet of its
surveyors and its optimisers working from the same table. Dr Hanke said the
collaboration had "raised more questions than a single poster can hold, which
is exactly the kind of problem a School should want to have."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wgzh5g.
