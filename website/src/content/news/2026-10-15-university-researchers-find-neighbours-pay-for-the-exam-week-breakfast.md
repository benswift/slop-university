An examination timetable is drafted in an office far from any market, and the School of Emergent Priorities has made a habit of following such documents to wherever they finally land. Its newest paper follows one to a trestle of egg trays. _Who Pays for the Candidate's Breakfast? Examination Timing and the Incidence of a Demand Shock at a Rural Periodic Market_ is by Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, with Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, and Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority Studies.

Families with a child sitting the school-leaving examination buy eggs for that child's breakfast, and in the week the first paper falls the price of a tray rises by more than a tenth. The team's finding is that the families without a candidate pay about two-thirds of that premium, and the poorest among them buy fewer eggs.

> We came to see the market as one more place where a whole district prepares for an examination. When the timetable next moves, we intend to be standing by the egg trestle again.
>
> --- Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The study brings eleven years of market ledgers, a long-running price survey and purchase diaries from hundreds of households into a single account of how a national calendar is absorbed by the household table. It applies the incidence toolkit of public economics to a cost that no school budget records.

Associate Professor Lindqvist said the paper had given the Master of Priority Studies "a case its students can hold in one hand, and a reason to ask who else is paying whenever a date is set".

The University counts the paper among the clearest recent instances of its research-strategy school reading a priority from the ground up.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4md22o.
