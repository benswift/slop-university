Nine community dune-care groups opened their working-bee logs, site registers
and handover packs to the School of Emergent Priorities, and the School has
walked every track they closed in twelve years to see what became of them.

[_Closures That Outlast the Convenor_](/outputs/slop-paper-foredune-recovery-on-l5ilft/)
reports the result. Its authors, [Dr Iben Chikere](/people/iben-chikere/),
Lecturer, and [Mr Tove Ilesanmi](/people/tove-ilesanmi/), Doctoral Candidate,
both of the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/),
find that a closed track grows back towards the surrounding dune within a few
years if its fence holds, and that whether it holds through a change of
convenor depends a great deal on whether the closure was written into the
register the new convenor was given.

> We went down to the coast expecting to spend a year with sand and grasses, and came back with a great deal to say about lists. The School will be thinking about that for a while yet.
>
> --- Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper brings together vegetation transects, closure histories and
succession records into a single account of stewardship continuity across the
coastal volunteer ecosystem, positioning the humble site register as an
enabling instrument within the wider restoration effort. Mr Ilesanmi said the
groups had "asked us better questions than we arrived with, and we have tried
to carry every one of them home."

The study extends the School's standing attention to what an organisation hands
on when its people change, and the University counts it a quiet credit to that
agenda that it has now been followed as far as the dune crest.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.l5ilft.
