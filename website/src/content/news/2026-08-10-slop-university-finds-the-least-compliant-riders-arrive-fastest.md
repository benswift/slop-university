> Following the app's suggested route to the letter is the single most
> reliable way to arrive late.
>
> --- Postdoctoral Fellow Petra Umbile, School of Emergent Priorities

A food-delivery platform's route-adherence score was built to flag riders who
stray from the path the app draws for them. A new Slop University poster
holds that score against the one thing it was meant to protect --- whether
the food arrives on time --- and finds the two point in opposite directions:
riders in the least-compliant scoring band posted the highest rate of
on-time arrivals; the most-compliant band posted the lowest.

The School of Emergent Priorities built the comparison from five depots'
existing operational scoring logs set against each rider's own door-to-door
arrival record over a six-month window, sorting riders into adherence
quintiles without touching how any depot dispatched a single order. On-time
arrivals fell from 89% in the least-compliant quintile to 54% in the most,
tracking the score in the wrong direction the whole way down.

"A shortcut only counts against you if the map is the thing being measured,
rather than the food," said Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the
Adaptive Metrics Lab, School of Continuous Improvement, a co-author on the
poster. "Ours was built to measure the food."

The finding sits alongside the School's wider work on instruments that end
up standing in for the outcome they were built to track, adding a live
delivery floor to a body of evidence gathered mostly from indoor counters and
booths.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fvt1wi.
