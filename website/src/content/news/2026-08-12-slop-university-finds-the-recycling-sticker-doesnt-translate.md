A doorstep survey did something a council's own contamination sticker never
has: asked the resident what they think it means. A new Slop University
research poster from the School of Continuous Improvement puts a number on
the gap between the two.

Researchers showed residents across nine streets their own bin's sticker,
without revealing the reason the collection crew had logged, and asked them
to guess it cold. The audit ran across three collection rounds and covered
248 tagged bins, coding every guess to the council's own five-category list
so a resident's answer and the crew's original entry could be set side by
side.

Residents' guesses matched the crew's logged reason in fewer than a third of
cases. The mismatch concentrated on two categories the sticker abbreviates
most heavily --- soft plastics and food-soiled paper --- rather than
spreading evenly across the list.

> We keep assuming the household on the other end of the sticker reads it
> the way we wrote it. This is the first time we've checked.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway
> Atlas, School of Continuous Improvement

Dr Thandiwe Solberg, the poster's co-author, said the categories "aren't
wrong so much as compressed past the point a stranger can unpack them on the
footpath."

The School of Continuous Improvement places the finding beside its own
running interest in what a shared code survives once it's read by someone
who didn't write it.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.73lumm.
