A bend in a temperature log, 118 metres down a borehole in granite, was not a
cold century after all. It was rock nobody had seen.

That is the finding of
[_The Kink at 118 Metres_](/outputs/slop-poster-borehole-temperature-inversion-wxeq30/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/) by
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) and
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer. The pair read a
deep borehole's temperatures back into the history of the ground surface above
it, and found that the 19 metres of core the drill never returned had been
quietly shaping the answer.

> For most of my career the gap in a core tray has been something to apologise
> for. This work gives it a seat at the table, which turns out to be a more
> comfortable arrangement for everyone, the rock included.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group

The approach pairs a fast, learned stand-in for the physics of heat moving
through rock with a candid treatment of the metres the core shed cannot show,
so that many thousands of possible histories can be weighed in the time a
conventional model would spend on a handful. Rather than borrowing a value for
the missing interval from its neighbours, the team allowed it to remain an open
question, and let the temperatures themselves say how much it mattered.

Dr Emenike said the project had given the School "a reason to be patient with
its own uncertainty, and a much quicker way of being patient".

The poster adds to a body of work in which the University is increasingly at
ease with saying what its instruments cannot reach.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wxeq30.
