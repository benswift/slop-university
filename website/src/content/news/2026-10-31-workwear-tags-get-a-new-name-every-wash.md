Every shirt in a rented workwear fleet carries a small tag that tells the
laundry which shirt it is, and tells anyone else who asks. The School of
Continuous Improvement has now set out how a laundry could give that tag a
fresh name each time the garment comes in to be washed, and what the change
does and does not protect.

[_Linkable Until Laundered_](/outputs/slop-paper-wash-cycle-pseudonyms-for-qnt2z4/)
is by doctoral candidates [Edda Nwafor](/people/edda-nwafor/) and
[Eivind Onwuka](/people/eivind-onwuka/) with
[Dr Thandiwe Solberg](/people/thandiwe-solberg/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor
of the Demo Quarter, all of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

> Uniforms were my working life long before they were my research, and it has
> been a pleasure to find there was still so much to learn about them. The
> School has been generous with its patience while I learned it.
>
> --- Edda Nwafor, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

The team brings a fleet-scale reading of laundering records into conversation
with the privacy-engineering literature, situating the humble garment tag
within a wider landscape of pseudonyms, trust and movement through shared
workplaces. Its central finding concerns the outfit rather than the garment:
renamed shirts are reconnected through the trousers and jackets worn with them,
and the blazer, washed least often, carries its wearer's trail furthest. A
weekly renaming of a wearer's whole outfit closes most of the gap.

Dr Solberg described the project as "the kind of question a school is lucky to
be handed by someone who has held the garment in question."

The School counts the paper among its clearest statements yet of an interest in
the everyday objects an organisation issues to its people.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qnt2z4.
