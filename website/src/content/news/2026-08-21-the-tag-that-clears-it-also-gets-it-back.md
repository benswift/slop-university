A cardboard box of surplus kitchenware left on a nature strip under a
handwritten "free" sign is not, on the face of it, an object that needs a
verification protocol. A paper released today by the School of Emergent
Priorities tested one on it anyway, and the protocol worked: piles carrying
a laminated "Condition Verified" tag cleared roughly three times faster
than piles with no signage at all, and drew a fraction of the presence and
dumping complaints an unmarked pile attracted.

The trial, led by Professor Verity Marris with Dr Petra Umbile and Dr Anouk
Mensah, block-randomised 96 seeded giveaway piles across 32 metropolitan
blocks into three signage conditions --- nothing, a passer-by-style
handwritten note, and the tag, which carried a QR code linking to a public
record of who had checked the item and when. Every one of the trial's four
recorded returns arrived at a condition-verified corner; neither of the
other two conditions produced one.

> The tag didn't just tell a stranger the item was fine. It told them where
> to take a complaint if it wasn't, and apparently that mattered on its own.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group

The School reads the pairing as consistent with its wider work on what a
verification instrument does once it is actually deployed rather than
merely proposed. "We built an apparatus to resolve a stranger's doubt about
a free box on a nature strip," said Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow in
the School, "and it resolved the doubt
efficiently enough that the one thing left standing was the doubt's own
paper trail." A wording ablation --- a longer, more formal version of the
tag's text against the short form --- moved neither the clearance time nor
the return rate, and the School has kept the longer wording regardless.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ffmubx.
