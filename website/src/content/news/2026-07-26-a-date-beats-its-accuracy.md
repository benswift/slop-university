A "for lease" sign only has one job: to say when a property is ready. Slop
University's newest research poster asked whether the date on it does
anything else, and found that it does, just not that.

The poster, from the School of Continuous Improvement, coded 187 signs
across six suburbs by phrasing --- an exact available-from date, "available
now", or no date at all --- against each agency's own enquiry log over a
fourteen-week window, then checked the promised date against when the lease
actually started. Signs naming an exact date drew more than double the
enquiries of a vague one and more than triple a dateless one; the date
itself predicted the actual move-in no better than a coin flip.

> A tenant reads a date as a fact about the property. It is really only a
> fact about the sign. We didn't expect the gap between those two to be
> this wide.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement, School of Continuous Improvement

The result replicates an earlier University finding, from a chain of coin
laundromats, that a specific promise buys more patience than a vague one
regardless of whether it holds. Dr Renke Sabel, Convenor of the Indicator
Commons, said the finding earns a place in the Commons' own catalogue of
numbers that keep their authority long after anyone checks what they were
tracking: "the date doesn't have to be true. It only has to be a date."

Whether a sign caught out once loses its pull the next time the same
agency posts one is the question the School is already circling.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.966e8b.
