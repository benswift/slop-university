An ammonia engine that makes its own hydrogen can run smoothly or roughly at
the same load, and which of the two it does depends on how it was started. That
is the finding of
[_The Engine Must Be Lit Twice_](/outputs/slop-paper-steady-state-multiplicity-in-h9oe45/),
a paper by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) and
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), with
[Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/) of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/).

> Most of what our School studies lies underground and changes slowly. It has
> been instructive to meet a machine on a test bed with the same habit we know
> from an aquifer, where the present state owes as much to the past as to the
> conditions.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Bringing the tools of reactor design to engine operation, the team charted how
the engine and its hydrogen-making cracker each behave on their own, then
reunited the two and followed the combined system through a programme of
start-ups, load changes and deliberate interventions. All twelve cold starts
settled into the rougher state; a short pulse of hydrogen, or a few minutes at
higher load, was enough to move the engine across.

Associate Professor Beng said the collaboration had given the Adaptive Metrics
Lab "a fresh regard for start-up as a stage of operation with its own claims on
our attention, and one we expect to keep returning to."

The University counts the paper among the clearest examples yet of its Schools
lending one another their methods, and of the distance an idea can travel when
they do.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.h9oe45.
