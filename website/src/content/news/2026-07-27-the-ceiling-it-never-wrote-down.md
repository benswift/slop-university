An "unlimited" pass is supposed to have no ceiling at all. Slop University's
newest paper finds one anyway --- the transit operator just never wrote it
down.

> We didn't ask the operator what the number was, because the operator has
> never agreed there is one. We just watched the gate long enough to see it
> flinch.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement, School of Continuous Improvement

The paper, from the School of Continuous Improvement, fitted nine station
clusters on a regional transit network with a passive tap logger and a
probe on each gate's speaker, timing which response tone plays when a
rider's "unlimited" weekly pass is scanned. Across ten weeks and 412
instrumented passes, the pipeline located the point at which a cluster's
gate starts sounding its "see attendant" tone instead of its ordinary
chime --- a point that differed by cluster by nearly two to one, rather
than sitting at one network-wide figure.

Lecturer Ingeborg Nwachukwu, a co-author and Convenor of the School's
Capability Sprints program, said the instrument's closest agreement was
with the people who already worked the gates: "the attendants had a feel
for it long before we brought a logger near the station. That's not a
criticism of the instrument. It's a reminder of how much a front line
already knows and is never asked to write down."

The work extends the School's growing account of ceilings an institution
would rather not name, and its evaluation-ecosystem agenda's interest in
instruments that surface what a system already knows about itself.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jmsl4q.
