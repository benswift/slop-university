Eighteen hop selectors spent a harvest being filmed from the chin down. The footage is the basis of _Hands Busy, Nose Busy_, a research poster by Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma and Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, of the School of Underlying Conditions, with Dr Mirela Hanke, Postdoctoral Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Living Dashboard.

A selector assesses a lot of hops by rubbing a handful between the palms and smelling it. After the first handful the hands are coated in resin and stay that way, which leaves the question of how anything gets written down. The team gave each selector four answers in turn: the existing pencil and scoresheet, dictation, a three-button pedal under the table, and a second person to write.

> None of us had stood at a selection table before. We have all filled in a log with hands we would rather not put on the page, and it was a comfort to find another trade had the same sheet and the same thumbprint on it.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, School of Underlying Conditions

Across 432 recorded rubs, selectors who dictated took fewer sniffs than under any other arrangement, and the most experienced took the fewest. The poster attributes this to breath: 83 per cent of spoken notes began only once the hops had left the nose. The foot pedal left the rub closest to how it ran with no note-taking at all.

The University regards the poster as a considered extension of the School's interests into rooms it does not ordinarily enter. Dr Hanke described the collaboration as "the kind of afternoon that rearranges what one thinks a keyboard is for".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.w4133n.
