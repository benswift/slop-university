Ms Hedvig Anyanwu spent six years relocating other people's offices before she came to the University, learning early that what a crate carries forward unopened says more about an organisation's priorities than anything on its moving manifest. Her latest research poster, with Sunniva Balogun and Tove Ilesanmi of the School of Emergent Priorities, turns that instinct on a fixture of home life nobody audits: the plastic bags kept behind the cupboard door beneath the kitchen sink.

The team sorted 163 households' bag-of-bags contents into four kinds — structural, sentimental, aspirational and terminal — across sixteen weeks of fortnightly doorstep audits, tracking how the terminal-kind share moved as bags wore out. The Office of Research Outputs' procurement partners have adopted the resulting split as a live signal, revising the University's own courier-satchel reorder threshold each time the sampled share moves past it.

> A bag that's kept but never opened isn't really being stored. It's being carried forward, waiting to find out what it's for.
>
> — Ms Hedvig Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

"Every household we visited had already done the sorting itself," says Ms Balogun. "We just gave the four piles names."

The work extends the School of Emergent Priorities' running interest in what a fixed allocation — a crate, a cupboard, a courier order — reveals about an institution's priorities once nobody is watching it form. The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.lvccxg.
