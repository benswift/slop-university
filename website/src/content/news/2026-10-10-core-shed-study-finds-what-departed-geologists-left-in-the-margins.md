On every edition of the log sheet used at one state drill-core repository, the
last column is headed for remarks and has never been compulsory. A paper
released today finds that this column, with the pencil marks loggers made on the
trays themselves, holds the larger part of what the repository still knows.

_What the Tray Remembers: Human-to-Structural Capital Conversion in a Drill-Core
Repository_ is the work of Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma and Dr Brynjar
Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, of the School of Underlying Conditions, with Dr
Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director of the Master of Applied
Measurement.

> Seven people gave us four days each to look again at rock they had last seen
> a working lifetime ago. The paper is theirs in every respect but the
> authorship, and we hope they recognise their trays in it.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, School of Underlying Conditions

The study brings knowledge-retention practice into alignment with the physical
archive. Returning loggers and present staff each read the same 1,120 intervals
of core, and the comparison yields a single ratio expressing how far a record
carries its author. Overall it stands at 0.55; for the coded columns alone it is
0.31, and it does not fall as records age.

The University counts the paper among the early returns on placing an earth
sciences school beside its established strengths in measurement, and notes the
authors' suggestion that the ratio could sit within an intellectual capital
statement. Dr Ezeigwe said the collaboration had "shown two schools that they
keep the same kind of paperwork, at different depths."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.lkm5ng.
