Who gets invited to reapply for a household battery rebate turns out to
depend less on what went wrong with the application than on when the
rejection reason was invented. A paper published today by the School of
Emergent Priorities builds a six-category taxonomy of 6,214 rejected
applications to a state battery-rebate scheme, and finds an independent
panel's judgement of whether a defect could be fixed runs in the opposite
direction to the office's own decision to invite a further attempt.

Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, led the work with Dr Marek
Solheim, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the Horizon Register, and Dr
Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer, School of Continuous Improvement. Two coders
sorted the scheme's rejection notes into six categories and checked their
fixability against an independent panel's blind review; a template-drafting
log, obtained under the same data-sharing arrangement, dated each category
back to the month it joined the scheme's rejection-code list.

> Every category on that list was somebody's decision, made for a reason
> that had nothing to do with the file in front of it. We are still finding
> out how many decisions like it are sitting in plain sight.
>
> --- Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor, Horizon Register

"The office was never being unfair to any one applicant," said Dr Adeyemi.
"It was just remembering its own paperwork more carefully than it was
reading theirs."

The paper extends the School's anticipatory-capability agenda into the
scheme's own record-keeping, treating a rejection-code list as a subject
worth dating as carefully as any policy it administers.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pwdajf.
