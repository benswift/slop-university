Close to a third of the phosphorus that cattle return to the land on seven
neighbouring beef holdings ends up within a hundred metres of a water trough,
and where those troughs stand was settled on a plan registered in 1927. That
is the finding at the centre of
[_Where the Plan Put the Water_](/outputs/slop-paper-piospheres-fixed-by-jfu51p/),
a new paper from [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

The study brings soil survey, livestock behaviour and the reading of registered
instruments into a single systems perspective, aligning field evidence from
around every trough on a shared pipeline with a whole-of-easement model of
where nutrients travel and what alternative layouts might enable. It positions
the easement, rather than the individual farm, as the natural frame for
decisions about water.

> Soil keeps very careful minutes. We expect to be reading these ones for a
> good while yet, and the paper is best taken as an early entry in a long
> conversation with the ground and with the people who farm it.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The collaboration sits comfortably within the University's practice of sending
its lawyers and its soil scientists out along the same fence line, and the
School of Settled Matters regards it as a natural extension of its interest in
the arrangements that outlast the people who made them.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jfu51p.
