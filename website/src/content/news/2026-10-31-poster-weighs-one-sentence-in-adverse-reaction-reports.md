> same stories more carefully than anyone had a right to ask. The School owes

[_It Seemed to Settle_](/outputs/slop-poster-dechallenge-wording-and-pzz7in/),
a new research poster from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), turns to the
part of an adverse-reaction report that says what happened once the medicine
was stopped. It is led by [Dr Tolan](/people/anneke-tolan/) with
[Ms Signy Okpara](/people/signy-okpara/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), and
[Mr Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/), Doctoral Candidate in the
School of Emergent Priorities.

The team set synthetic case narratives, committee practice and one carefully
varied sentence side by side over four structured sessions, so that the wording
of a report could be studied with the attention usually given to its contents.
A hedged line, the authors report, lifted the share of narratives graded
probable or certain from about one in five to well over half, close to what a
definite line achieved, while the hedge itself seldom appeared in the
justifications assessors wrote.

"We came away with more questions than we took in, which the School tends to
regard as a good season," said Ms Okpara.

The poster joins the University's continuing investment in the close reading
of ordinary documents, and the School expects to return to these narratives
with the observer, rather than the wording, as the thing that changes.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pzz7in.
