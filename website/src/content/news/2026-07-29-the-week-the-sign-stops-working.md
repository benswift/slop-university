Every driver-feedback sign in one metropolitan council's fleet is relocated at a
median of 11.6 weeks. A paper published today by the School of Continuous
Improvement reports that the effect it was relocated to deliver has been over
for a fortnight by the time the trailer moves.

The study follows the council's six sign trailers across 61 residential streets
over three years, using permanent traffic counters that were installed before
the program began and were never available to the depot that scheduled it.
Because streets were reached in the order the trailers came free rather than in
any order of need, the researchers were able to compare each street against the
streets still waiting. Mean speed fell by 2.41 km/h in the fortnight after a
sign arrived, then recovered steadily, and from week eleven the study can no
longer measure any difference at all.

> The interesting part of this is not statistical. Two numbers were arrived at
> by people who never met, for reasons that had nothing to do with each other,
> and they landed a fortnight apart.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement

The University counts the work among the applied-measurement scholarship it has
been consolidating across its schools, and regards the decay estimate as a
contribution its partners in local government are well placed to use. Dr
Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Demo Quarter, noted that the
depot's own records made the analysis possible: "The roster was handed over
without anyone asking us what we wanted it for, which is more than most
operational documents are offered up for."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.07lgun.
