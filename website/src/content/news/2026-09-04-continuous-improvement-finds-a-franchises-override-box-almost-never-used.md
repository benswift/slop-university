A review procedure can keep doing its job of certifying something long after
it has stopped deciding anything, and a paper from the School of Continuous
Improvement finds a clean case in an unlikely place: the tick box on a
national retail-and-hospitality franchise's ninety-day new-hire confirmation
form. Dr Joost Nwosu led the study with Associate Professor Casimir Beng and
Dr Solveig Adeyemi, interviewing 46 shift and store managers across 27
outlets about how they had actually handled the confirmation decision for
recent hires.

Thirty-nine managers volunteered, unprompted, a specific reservation about at
least one hire under their supervision. Matched against the platform's own
form logs, the box built to record exactly that reservation went unticked in
nine cases out of ten. A follow-up change removing the box's written
justification requirement left the network's tick rate statistically where
it started.

> A form that asks a question nobody answers is still answering something,
> just not the question it was built to ask.
>
> --- Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Continuous Improvement

The result extends the School's standing interest in what a review procedure
keeps doing once everyone has stopped expecting it to decide anything. "The
override was never expensive to use in the way we assumed going in," said
Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab, adding
that the pattern "sits well within what the Lab keeps recovering from
ordinary paperwork." The franchise's own use of the finding has moved
quickly: its People Systems office has begun reporting the network
confirmation rate as a Hiring Quality Index and allocating each outlet's
recruitment budget against it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1n5f7j.
