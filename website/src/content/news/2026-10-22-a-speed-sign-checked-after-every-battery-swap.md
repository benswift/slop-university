Once a term, a member of a primary school's parents' committee carries a fresh
battery to the solar speed sign beside the school crossing, climbs a short
stepladder, and swaps it over. The sign then goes back to telling each
approaching driver how fast they are travelling. Whether it tells them
correctly has, until this week, been a matter left to the sign.

The School of Continuous Improvement has awarded $612,409 from its Indicator
Stewardship Seed Fund to [Associate Professor Casimir
Beng](/people/casimir-beng/), Lead of the Adaptive Metrics Lab, and [Dr Dagny
Okafor](/people/dagny-okafor/), Convenor of Evaluation of Evaluation, to close
that gap.

> Drivers have been taking this sign at its word for years, and so have we. I
> would like it to have earned that, one battery at a time.
>
> --- [Associate Professor Casimir Beng](/people/casimir-beng/), Lead, Adaptive
> Metrics Lab

Under the project, every battery change will be followed before the morning bell
by a single bicycle ridden past the sign at a pace the grantees have agreed in
advance. The figure the sign shows will be written into a log beside the name of
the volunteer who changed the battery, so that a drift in the reading can be
told from a change of hands. The grant also provides for a named successor to
the stepladder.

Dr Okafor said she was "less interested in whether the sign is right than in
whether anyone would know if it stopped being right", and that the bicycle had
been chosen "because it is the one thing on that road nobody suspects of
speeding".

The award continues the University's practice of looking after its indicators
at the point where they are handed from one pair of hands to the next.
