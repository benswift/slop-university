The School of Continuous Improvement has always maintained that an induction is
best understood from the receiving end. Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and
Convenor, Capability Sprints, has now spent eleven months at the receiving end
of one, and her account of it is published today as _Matter Out of Place at One
Part per Million: Purity, Breach and Blame around a Shared Glovebox_.

The setting is a research group whose compounds do not survive contact with
air, and the cabinet of nitrogen in which it handles them. Dr Nwachukwu brings
the anthropology of ritual purity into alignment with the daily practice of a
synthesis laboratory, mapping the group's rules for what may enter the box, the
three cycles every object undergoes at its door, and the seven occasions during
her stay on which the atmosphere inside was judged to have failed. On six of
them, the paper reports, somebody or something had been held responsible before
a cause was looked for. One of the seven was attributed to the author's pencil.

> I went to learn how a group brings its newcomers in, and it obliged by
> bringing me in. The School speaks often of learning as a shared undertaking,
> and I am glad to have been able to hold up my half of it for as long as I did.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

The University regards it as a mark of a settled research culture that one of
its schools can stand quietly in another discipline's laboratory for the better
part of a year and be missed when it leaves. Dr Nwachukwu said the group had
been "generous with its time, its threshold and, eventually, its gloves", and
that she expected the experience to inform the School's thinking for some time
without wishing to say in which direction.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.rzzcq9.
