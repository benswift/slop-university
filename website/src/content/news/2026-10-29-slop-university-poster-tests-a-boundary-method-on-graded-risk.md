A method that statisticians use to trace boundaries across disease maps will
usually find one even where the risk beneath it only slopes, according to
[_A Line Somewhere on the Ramp_](/outputs/slop-poster-how-often-bayesian-r3zao7/),
a new research poster in statistical epidemiology from the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

The work is led by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior
Research Fellow, with [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior
Lecturer, and [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and Convenor of
Improvement Grand Rounds. Across 3,000 simulated maps of an invented region,
the team found the method recovered a sharp step almost every time, and still
marked a boundary on most gentle ramps, its position wandering across the
slope from one map to the next.

> Every map we make at the School starts with someone deciding that one thing
> has ended. It has been good to spend a season on the moment just before that
> decision, and I expect we will spend a good many more there.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Working entirely from synthetic data, the poster sets established
boundary-detection practice against a structured programme of simulation,
strengthening the evidence base that underpins spatial reasoning wherever a
map is asked to carry a decision.

It is the kind of crossing the University hoped its earth sciences school
would make early: a field surveyor's working question, put with some care to
a statistician's map. Dr Ntuli said the findings would go with him to the next
session of Improvement Grand Rounds, "where I suspect they will find a room
full of people who have drawn a line or two of their own".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.r3zao7.
