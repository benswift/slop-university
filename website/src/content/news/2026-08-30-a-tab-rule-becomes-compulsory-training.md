A single corner store's decade-old, entirely unwritten rule for writing off
a regular customer's unpaid tab is now the sole approved case in Slop
University's compulsory hardship-response micro-credential, with no
alternative case permitted and no review scheduled.

Dr Marek Solheim of the School of Emergent Priorities led a fourteen-month
embedded study of the store's handwritten credit ledger, finding
forgiveness tracked how visible a customer was at the counter --- whether
they stopped to talk, brought the kids in, rounded up rather than took
change --- far more than the amount owed. Because only the dollar figure
ever made it onto the page, the team coded the owner's practice into a
four-tier rubric, GRACE, and piloted it with a professional-development
cohort.

> Writing a judgement down is supposed to keep it. Ours seems to have kept
> only the part that could be filed.
>
> --- Dr Marek Solheim, School of Emergent Priorities

The pilot found trainees applying GRACE clustered their forgiveness calls
hard against its coded cutoff, a pattern the original, unwritten practice
never showed. The University's continuing-education arm has built the
finding into its curriculum rather than its caution list: Dr Ingeborg
Nwachukwu, who convenes the School of Continuous Improvement's Capability
Sprints program, said GRACE now anchors the micro-credential's opening
module, and that she was glad the case had "found a permanent home this
early in the programme." The adoption, the University said, reflects the
confidence its case-method record has earned across a decade of similar
single-site studies.

The fourteen months at the counter combined a de-identified ledger
transcription with unobtrusive observation of counter-side behaviour, the
comparator design the School's case-method corpus increasingly applies once
a finding returns to the site that produced it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.vmdr3o.
