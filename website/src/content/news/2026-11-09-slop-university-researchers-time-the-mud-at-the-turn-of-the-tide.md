Slop University today adds an oceanographic study to its research repository, and with it a measured answer to a question every muddy estuary poses twice a day: when the water stops, how much of what it carries actually comes to rest?

[_Too Little Slack_](/outputs/slop-poster-floc-settling-velocities-kixx75/) is a research poster by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Senior Lecturer in the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), with [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, both of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/). The team watched the clumps of fine sediment known as flocs settle at the start of 28 consecutive slack waters. At spring high water, 63% of the floc mass at mid-depth could not reach the bed before the current resumed; at neap low water, the figure fell to 20%.

> The estuary keeps its own timetable, and nobody working on it gets to set the hours. We kept that timetable for a full cycle of tides, and the poster is what the water was willing to show us in the minutes it allowed.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Working from a single anchored station, the team aligned in-situ particle imaging with current records to build a slack-by-slack account of sediment pathways, and to bring the brief pause between tides into the frame of the estuary's longer-term sediment budget.

The poster joins a growing shelf of University work on the ground and the water above it, and the University counts it a welcome arrival there. Dr Obiora described the wet-season fieldwork now in planning as "the natural next conversation with the same water".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kixx75.
