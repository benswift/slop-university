A paper from the School of Emergent Priorities finds that how a
vending-machine route driver times a restocking visit, not how often the
round runs, predicts whether a machine stays stocked. Drawing on twelve
months of route and sale telemetry across nine contractors' fleets, Doctoral
Candidate Tove Ilesanmi, Dr Lindiwe Achterberg and Professor Verity Marris
built the Restock Anticipation Maturity Model, sorting each fleet into one
of five named levels from Reactive to Pre-Emptive restocking. A regional
landlord consortium has since adopted the resulting score as the basis for
renewing a contractor's access to a shopping strip's busiest sites.

> A restock sheet was never meant to answer a question this large. It just
> turned out to have the answer sitting in it the whole time.
>
> — Tove Ilesanmi, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper draws on restock and stockout telemetry from 214 machines across
41 shopping-strip and supermarket-foyer sites, sorting each contractor's
fleet into a named level and tracing what changed once one of the fleets'
own landlords made the ladder a condition of keeping its best pitches.
Contractors reaching the ladder's upper levels recovered lost selling time
at under half the rate of contractors held to its lowest levels, a
difference the School regards as a case study in what a fleet already knows
about itself before anyone thinks to ask.

"The consortium didn't ask us to build an eligibility test," said Professor
Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group. "It asked for a
maturity model, and found the eligibility test sitting inside it."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.2h2j54.
