Give a model sodium channel a few minutes of steady firing and it will take longer to recover than it took to fire. Give it long enough, and it stops keeping count. Both results come from [_Remembered Longer Than It Lasted_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-chain-model-i4d56z/), a new paper from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) by doctoral candidate [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/) and [Dr Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/), Postdoctoral Fellow.

> Gunnhild came to us asking how long a thing keeps its hold on you after you have finished with it. It has been a quiet pleasure to watch that question find a membrane to live in.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Working entirely in simulation, the pair put a chain-based model of slow inactivation through trains of activity running from a tenth of a second to several days, mapping recovery dynamics against firing rate and architectural depth. The analysis surfaces a break-even duration, below which recovery outlasts the activity that caused it, and a memory horizon, beyond which the channel can no longer tell a long train from a longer one. At slow firing the horizon arrives in under an hour whatever the chain's depth; at faster firing it stretches with the chain, to 62 hours in the deepest case modelled.

The paper carries the School's settled attention to what a commitment costs once it has been made down to the scale of the excitable membrane, and the School counts that a promising direction for the years ahead.

"There is a good deal more of the chain still to go down," said Ms Ekwueme, "and I am in no hurry."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.i4d56z.
