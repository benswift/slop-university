Nineteen distinct noises in 2016. Thirty-four by 2025. Nothing removed in
between. That is the audible alert set of one family hatchback, as
catalogued in Slop University's newest paper.

Researchers from the Schools of Emergent Priorities and Continuous
Improvement read the platform's owner documentation across ten consecutive
model years, then checked every alert they found against bench-harnessed
instrument clusters pulled from decommissioned cars of the matching year.
Each alert was coded into one of five categories. No category contracted
over the decade, and proximity and manoeuvring more than doubled.

> A set like this is easy to read as a set of instructions. It is closer to
> a list of things that once went wrong, kept in the order somebody noticed
> them.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper's second half asked whether any of it lands. Three hundred and
forty-one drivers of the same platform sat a twenty-four item
identification task, then drove an instrumented closed circuit while alerts
sounded at intervals they could not anticipate. Scores ran from three to
twenty-one, and nobody named them all. Response latency held steady across
every quartile.

"We went looking for a difference we expected to find, and it was not
there," said Dr Marek Solheim, a Lecturer in the School of Emergent
Priorities and a co-author. "The School has been glad to publish that as it
stands."

The University regards the work as a welcome addition to the School's
anticipatory-capability agenda, which has taken a sustained interest in
records that outlast the decisions behind them.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.f02d57.
