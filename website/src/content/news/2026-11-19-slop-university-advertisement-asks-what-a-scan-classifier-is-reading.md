What does a classifier look at when it is shown a scan? The University's newest display advertisement, [_Mask the tumour. The model still answers._](/outputs/slop-ad-mask-the-tumour-wi0plt/), puts that question to anyone with a minute in front of the campus signage panels. Its hook is [_The Tumour Is Optional_](/outputs/slop-paper-lesion-blind-baselines-brain-the4tt/), a paper from the [School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) which found that a brain-tumour classifier retrained with the tumour masked out still named the tumour type on most slices of an institutional teaching archive.

The campaign is presented by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), who spent many years drawing the lines between one mapped soil and the next.

> Most of a soil boundary is drawn from what lies on either side of it, and a good legend admits as much. It has been a pleasure to see the same admission asked of a classifier, and the School is happy to stand beside it for a season.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The illustration sets a lightbox scan, one square of it left blank, into the cut face of an open soil pit, bringing field description, clinical informatics and model evaluation into a single frame. Beneath the campaign block, a line of small print directs the closer reader to three further outputs from the record: a re-measurement of tracer disagreement in stained tissue sections, a classifier small enough to run on a groundwater logger, and an account of the core a borehole fails to bring up.

The University sees in the advertisement a continuing confidence that its scholarship can be met at eye height and carried home on a phone.

The full advertisement is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wi0plt.
