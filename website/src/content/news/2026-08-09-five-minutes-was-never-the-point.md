> A flag that closes with the slot doesn't stop being true. It just stops
> being anyone's problem until the next one opens.
>
> --- Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Every primary school interview night runs on the same allocation: one slot,
evenly booked, expected to hold whatever a family brings into the room. The
Master of Priority Studies has built its newest recruitment campaign around
the gap between that allocation and what actually shows up inside it --- a
stack of folders, each carrying its own coloured flag, fanning out faster
than the clock on the wall can account for.

Unveiled today under the line "Five Minutes Was Never The Point," the
campaign stages the folders mid-fan across an empty interview table, the
next pair of chairs still waiting to be filled. It joins the University's
bus-stop panel and campus digital signage rotations, part of the School of
Emergent Priorities' ongoing case for treating sequencing as a taught skill
rather than a scheduling accident.

"Most of what doesn't fit the slot isn't decided against," said Dr Marek
Solheim, of the School of Emergent Priorities. "It's just quietly carried to
the next one, the same way an unfinished priority survives past the plan
that named it."

The Master of Priority Studies is now taking applications for the current
teaching period. The full advertisement is available from the University's
research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.s5skei.
