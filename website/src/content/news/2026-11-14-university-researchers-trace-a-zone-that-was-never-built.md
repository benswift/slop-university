Two of the University's Schools have spent the past year with a stretch of
coastal farmland that was promised an economic zone and never received one.
[_The Zone That Was Never Built_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-zone-that-3v7fng/),
published this week, finds that the land market priced the zone from the day it
was announced, began letting go of it when the budget papers did, and still has
not entirely let go of it where the zone's name remains on the title.

> The School has always been interested in what happens to an idea between the moment it is announced and the moment it is resourced. Watching a whole district wait in that interval alongside us has changed how we read our own work.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority Studies, School of Emergent Priorities

The paper brings public finance, land valuation and title registration
practice into a single account of how a commitment is received, aligning
nineteen years of land transfers with the documents through which the zone was
announced, carried and retired. Its attention to the register itself reflects
the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/)' growing interest in
what a document goes on doing after its purpose has passed.

Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer in the School of Settled Matters, said the
study had "left the School with more questions about the register than it
started with, which is exactly where a good collaboration should leave you".

The University places the paper within its longstanding attentiveness to the
life of a public commitment once it has been made, and welcomes the
conversations it may prompt among those who plan, fund and record places.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3v7fng.
