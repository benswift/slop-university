Children educated at home have arrived at an answer to a passing stranger's
question that their parents have not, according to a new paper from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).
[_No School Today?_](/outputs/slop-paper-an-ethnography-of-yve5zn/), by
doctoral candidate Sunniva Balogun, follows the question as it is put to the
children of one regional home-educating network at its weekly meet in a
riverside park, and listens closely to what comes back.

Across twenty-nine Tuesday mornings, Ms Balogun joined the network at its long
table, first to put its shared crate of books in order and then as a regular.
Her paper situates these small, friendly exchanges within a wider conversation
about public time, childhood and the shared rhythms that underpin community
life, and traces how a single answer, passed from older children to younger
ones, came to anchor the meet's dealings with the people who pass through it.

> I went along to sort a box of books and came home having noticed the clock
> for the first time in years. I suspect all of us answer for our day in ways
> we have never had to hear ourselves say, and I would like to keep listening.
>
> --- Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group and one of
Ms Balogun's supervisors, welcomed the paper as "patient, generous work of
exactly the kind the School hopes its doctoral community will be known for."

The paper adds to a widening seam of the School's research into the everyday
settings where families and institutions meet, and the University counts it
among the quieter strengths of its doctoral programme.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.yve5zn.
