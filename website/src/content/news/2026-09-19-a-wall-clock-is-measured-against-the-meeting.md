Eight meeting rooms on one open-plan floor had their wall clocks covered by a
hinged panel on alternate weeks, and the School of Emergent Priorities was in
the room for every booking. _Measuring the Clock in the Room_, a new research
poster from Hedvig Anyanwu, Dr Ronja Oyelaran and Dr Iben Chikere, sets the
result out plainly: the length of a meeting did not differ with the clock in
view.

The study followed the floor's bookings across a full fourteen-week trial, with
its primary measure fixed before the first meeting began. The poster reports
that measure as it came out. It does not go looking for a second one.

> We asked the room a question and the room gave us an answer. It seemed only
> polite to write the answer down as it was given.
>
> --- Hedvig Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The University has taken the finding into its facilities practice. Every booking
now carries a note of the clock's state, and each room's clock is attested at
the annual inspection as immaterial to the length of the meeting held beneath
it. The panels have been returned to the store cupboard, and the clocks remain
where they were hung.

Dr Oyelaran said the work reflected the School's interest in what an ordinary
fitting does, and does not do, within the rooms an organisation builds for
itself. "A clock is a small thing to hang on a wall," she said. "It is good to
know how much of the wall it holds up."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.csi737.
