The School of Continuous Improvement has spent much of the year on formulas that
pay for arrivals. A research poster deposited today through the Office of
Research Outputs takes that interest to the community shed, where the arrival is
a man at an open day and the formula is the one that keeps the roller door up.

Working from twenty-four months of sign-in sheets across eleven sheds, covering
4,318 attendance events and 1,062 distinct men, the team fitted a four-state
model of how attendance is taken up, held, and let go. Read against each shed's
own open-day cadence, the counted figure and the room move apart: an additional
open day a year is associated with 12.4 more counted attendances and 1.9 fewer
men present on a median Tuesday. In steady state the model puts the regulars at
0.21 of everyone who has ever signed in.

> Nobody at these sheds is doing anything other than what the scheme asks of
> them, and what the scheme asks for is a door that opens. This is the second
> study in a year where the School has found itself measuring the distance
> between a return and a room, and I doubt it is the last.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds, noted that the
participating sheds "handed over two years of paper and asked for nothing back",
and said the question the work puts to a scheme's administrators is "which of the
two numbers they would rather be right about".

Slop University counts it a good use of the School's method when the object of
study is something a reader could walk into on a weekday morning. The full poster
is available from the University's research repository under an open licence,
doi:10.5555/slop.ngssxj.
