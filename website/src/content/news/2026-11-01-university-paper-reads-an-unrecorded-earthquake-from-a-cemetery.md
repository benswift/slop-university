When a moderate earthquake shook a farming district with no accelerometer
nearby, the fullest account of what the ground did was left in a small
cemetery a few kilometres away. [_Left Standing_](/outputs/slop-paper-headstones-as-a-kskqqb/)
is by [Mr Eskil Nwabueze](/people/eskil-nwabueze/), Doctoral Candidate in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), and
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> The School spends a good deal of its time thinking about what happens at the
> bottom of a list. It has been a quiet pleasure to find the same question
> waiting among the headstones, and to see how naturally it travels.
>
> --- Mr Eskil Nwabueze, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

Bringing together a complete, stone-by-stone condition survey and established
thinking on how free-standing objects respond to shaking, the paper builds a
framework for reading a burial ground as a distributed record of a single
event. It gives particular attention to the memorials that did not fall, an
often-overlooked population which, the authors find, does most of the work of
setting limits on the shaking.

The study sits comfortably within the University's growing body of
field-based inquiry, in which careful attention to an ordinary setting is
allowed to inform questions well beyond it. Dr Emenike said she hoped the
approach "might one day be taken up wherever an old cemetery and an
unrecorded earthquake happen to share a hillside".

The authors thank the cemetery's trustees, who held off re-setting the fallen
stones until the survey was complete.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.kskqqb.
