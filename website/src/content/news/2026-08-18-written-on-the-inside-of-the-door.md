Ask who last worked on a house's electrical connection and the answer is
usually inside a small door on the front wall: written in pencil, printed on a
sticker, or missing altogether.

Slop University researchers have now catalogued what is there. _Service Marks
in Domestic Meter Boxes_, released today by the School of Emergent Priorities,
photographed the meter enclosure at 1,247 addresses and sorted the 9,318 marks
recovered from them into a six-facet scheme of 43 terms, covering the medium of
a mark, where it sits, how much of it survives, what work it refers to, who
left it, and when.

The picture the scheme produces is one of durable evidence and thin
attribution. The median enclosure holds six marks spanning more than fourteen
years, yet both the tradesperson responsible and the date of the work can be
recovered from only about one mark in twelve.

> Every one of these marks was left by somebody who assumed the next person
> through the door would read it. What the scheme gives us is a vocabulary for
> an expectation nobody had thought to write down.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The study extends the School's longer interest in the records institutions do
not keep, and the Indicator Commons has since ratified the scheme's 43 terms as
the University's Domestic Service Marking Schema. Professor Verity Marris,
Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group, said the work "gives an ordinary
piece of household infrastructure the descriptive attention it has not
previously attracted, and the University is glad to have taken the time over
it".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.2cgh9b.
