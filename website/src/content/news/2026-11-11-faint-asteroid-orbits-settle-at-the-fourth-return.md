The fourth return is the one that counts, at least for the faint asteroids followed by the small recovery telescope at the centre of [_Settled by the Fourth Opposition_](/outputs/slop-poster-settling-an-asteroid-svbtxu/), a new research poster led by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Senior Lecturer in the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), with [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and [Dr Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/), Postdoctoral Fellow in the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

The team took 312 main-belt objects that the programme had seen through five or more oppositions and asked a plain question of each: once the orbit had been worked out from the first few returns, did it point to the right place in the sky the next time round? Three returns were enough for some of them. Four were enough for nearly all. Of the 214 orbits the programme had already logged as secure after three returns, 98 still missed.

> An orbit, like a point of law, is closed by the people who stop checking it. I am grateful to the colleagues who kept checking a little longer than anyone had asked them to.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The method brings the School's event-history toolkit into working alignment with the observatory's own records, translating years of follow-up practice into a shared account of when a prediction can be relied upon. The University regards the poster as a welcome crossing of its law school into the observational sciences, and as evidence of how far a careful habit of reading can travel.

Dr Emenike said the questions it raised would "travel back with us to our own instruments, where we have been asking how long a record must run before it can be trusted."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.svbtxu.
