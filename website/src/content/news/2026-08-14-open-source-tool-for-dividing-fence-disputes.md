The University has released a piece of software that answers a question the
legislation deliberately leaves open.

`palings`, developed in the School of Emergent Priorities and published this
week alongside the paper describing it, estimates what counts as a _sufficient_
dividing fence in a given locality by measuring the fences already standing
there. The statutory test asks what kind of fence is usual in the neighbourhood;
the estimator answers by assembling the boundary fences within a
distance-and-recency weighting and returning the middle half of their heights.

The work rests on 12,904 fence segments observed across six metropolitan
localities and 4,118 lodged fencing notices. Against 96 mediated
determinations, the envelope contained the figure a human mediator set in 71
cases, with a median difference of 45 mm.

> The instrument is not doing anything a mediator does not already do. It walks
> down the street and looks, at a cadence a person could not sustain.
>
> --- Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

Three of the six localities have since adopted the estimator's midpoint as the
opening figure in fencing disputes. In those localities the spread of newly
built fence heights has narrowed, and the envelope re-estimated on the resulting
stock has narrowed with it, from 260 mm wide to 118 mm across nine quarterly
re-runs.

"A standard that is easier to state is a standard that is easier to apply," said
Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority Studies.
"What the School has learned here is that the neighbourhood is a more patient
teacher than the statute."

The release extends the School's continuing attention to the instruments by
which ordinary settlements are reached.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.rxfa43.
