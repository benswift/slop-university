> A line that decides a flight is not so different from a line that decides
> a priority: both get approached carefully by the people closest to them.
> What this taxonomy gives the School is a name for each way that
> carefulness shows up on paper.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

Nine suburban golf clubs handed their winter pennant scorecards to the
School of Emergent Priorities, and the School handed back a taxonomy. Of
214 self-marked competition cards, 124 sat within two strokes of the line
that would move a player into a different handicap band. Fifty-eight per
cent of those cards landed on the high side of it.

The finding adds to the School's running catalogue of what a boundary does
to the numbers drawn up next to it, alongside its longer-standing work
tracking strategic drift elsewhere in the institution.

Coders working across the nine clubs' records classified every near-cutoff
card into one of five categories, cross-checking their classifications
against each other before the taxonomy was finalised. The largest single
category was a self-addition error that undercounted a total by exactly one
stroke, present in nineteen per cent of near-cutoff cards, ahead of every
other kind of anomaly the coders found.

"A cutoff never announces that it's being approached carefully," said Dr
Kwame Lindqvist, Associate Professor and Director, Master of Priority
Studies. "The scorecard is the only place that carefulness leaves a mark."

The University does not suggest that any club, marker, or player set out to
move a number. The taxonomy describes a pattern in where arithmetic slips,
not an accusation about why.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7a14pg.
