Thirty-six trenches, cut by hand and bucket along the line one ripper tine had just worked, are the evidence behind [_Stones the Tine Never Touched_](/outputs/slop-paper-reading-buried-stones-4mffct/), a new paper by [Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/) and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

The team fitted a force sensor to one tine of a working subsoiler and ran it through a stony paddock, treating every sharp rise in its draught as a stone met below the surface. Then they dug. Of 412 stones lying in the tine's path, the record found most of those the steel had scored, 84 per cent, but barely half of the whole population. The smaller stones had been nudged aside by the moving soil before the tine reached them. The largest did the reverse, tripping the tine clear of the ground and showing up as the quietest stretch of the run.

> You can learn a paddock from the seat of a tractor, but only so much of it. We wanted to know where that knowledge stops, and the shovel was the honest way to find out.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The study brings on-the-go sensing, implement mechanics and field excavation into conversation on the paddock itself, and adds a working farm machine to the instruments through which the School reads the ground. Associate Professor Uzoma said the Basement Relations Group "has spent a long time with the core that never comes up, and it was good to meet its cousin in a furrow".

The School counts the paper among the clearest statements yet of its interest in what a record leaves out.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.4mffct.
