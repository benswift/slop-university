A cube of chalk in a suburban back bar now arrives by work order. _Fault-Tree
Decomposition and Per-Mode Weibull Hazards for a Coin-Operated Pool Table_,
published today by the School of Continuous Improvement, is the reason.

The team instrumented 62 coin-operated tables across a regional hotel group's 41
venues for eighteen months, logging 44,318 coin insertions and extending the
technician's existing fortnightly circuit with a 31-item structured inspection.
Every way a game can fail to start was resolved into a fault tree of eleven
basic events, and each event was given a fitted failure hazard. On roughly one
arrival in twelve, a game could not be started. Four modes carry three quarters
of that figure, and not one of them is mechanical: chalk, a cue tip, the
triangle, and the separator that returns the black ball.

> A pub let us keep an instrument on its back-room table for eighteen months and
> never once asked what it was for. The School would like to think the answer is
> worth the room.
>
> --- Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Demo Quarter

The group has kept the uniform fortnightly round the model advised against,
added a game-readiness figure to its quarterly venue scorecard, and moved
accessory restock from the publican's discretion to a work order raised against
the fitted interval. Dr Joost Nwosu, Postdoctoral Fellow, said that what an
operator does with a schedule "is part of the finding, and the School is glad to
have watched it happen rather than assumed it".

The University counts the work among the School's steadier contributions to the
description of ordinary assets, and welcomes the attention it draws to a class
of amenity that is rarely given any.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.v12p0c.
