A grow bag of sawdust loses most of the height it will ever lose before a single strand of mycelium has entered it, and the space it leaves under the neck is where part of its first crop goes astray. That is the central finding of [_The Gap Under the Neck_](/outputs/slop-paper-how-far-sawdust-2fvnnj/), a new paper by [Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/) of the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> Our School spends most of its days with paper, and we were not sure what we would bring to a grow room. The habits turned out to be the ones we already keep: look at the same thing every few days, write down what changed, and resist deciding too early what it means.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The study brings filling practice, substrate moisture and the life of the crop into one continuous line of observation, following each bag from the filling bench through the steamer and the incubation shelf to its first harvest. In doing so it relocates the conversation about settling from the density of the block to the gap between block and bag, where the 52 bags that pinned early along the wall gave up a measurable share of their first flush.

The paper extends a growing strand of University work on the farms, growers and working rooms that feed a region, and reflects the conviction that a question carried across a School boundary comes back with more in it than it left with. Dr Obiora said the collaboration had "given the soil laboratory a reason to keep a ruler on the incubation shelf, and a colleague who is very good at asking what the ruler is for".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.2fvnnj.
