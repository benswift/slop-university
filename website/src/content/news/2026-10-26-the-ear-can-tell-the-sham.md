For three years, everyone who sat for an ear-stimulation study in the School of Emergent Priorities testing room was asked the same question at the moment the current became noticeable: what does it feel like? Their answers, written on a single ruled line of each session sheet and never analysed, are now the subject of [_Prickling at the Concha, Pressure at the Lobe_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-words-participants-5gnoz1/), a new paper by [Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), Doctoral Candidate, [Ylva Onyekwere](/people/ylva-onyekwere/), Doctoral Candidate, and [Dr Anneke Tolan](/people/anneke-tolan/), Senior Research Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Futures in Committee.

> Nobody kept these sheets for the words. They were kept because the intensity figures had to be kept, and the words happened to be written on the same page. That is usually how the best part of an archive survives.
>
> --- Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Bringing the disciplines of the catalogue to bear on the evidence base of neurostimulation, the team assembled an authority file of participants' own vocabulary and read it against the site of stimulation. Descriptions of prickling clustered at the active site and descriptions of pressure at the control site, and the words alone were enough to tell the two apart in roughly three sessions out of four. Participants who described prickling were also considerably more likely to believe they had received active stimulation.

The School counts the paper among the first to come from its testing room without anyone being stimulated for it, and sees in that a model of thrift worth repeating.

Dr Tolan said the testing room would now "listen a little more closely at threshold, and write it down a little more neatly than before".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.5gnoz1.
