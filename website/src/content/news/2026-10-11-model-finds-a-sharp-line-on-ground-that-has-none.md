> We set out with an equation and some ground for it to stand on, and we have
> come back with the same equation. I would describe that as a successful
> expedition. The ground was returned in the condition we found it.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

Dr Obiora was speaking on the publication of _An Abrupt Edge on Gradual Ground:
A Minimal Reaction–Diffusion Model of Complement Deposition across a Graded
Regulator Boundary_, written with Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer in the same
School. The paper concerns the complement system, a part of the body's innate
defences, and asks what its self-reinforcing loop does on a surface that is
neither fully protected nor fully exposed.

The authors bring a single dimensionless equation to a family of simulated
surfaces and follow where the boundary of deposition comes to rest. In the model
the boundary is far narrower than the transition beneath it, occupying 1.4% of
the longest gradient examined; it settles in the same position from either
starting condition once neighbouring points are allowed to influence one
another; and it takes longer to get there the gentler the gradient is. The paper
is explicit that these are properties of an equation and that it makes no
statement about any tissue, patient or condition.

The University regards the paper as a considered addition to the range of its
earth sciences school, whose staff have lately been found at some distance from
the earth. Dr Emenike said the collaboration had been "conducted in units of our
own choosing, which I can recommend to anyone who has not tried it".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.qiy4rx.
