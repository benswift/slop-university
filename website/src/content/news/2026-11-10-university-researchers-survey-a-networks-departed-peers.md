A routing table is rarely treated as a historical source. [_The Near Neighbourhood Keeps Its Dead_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-surface-survey-8gaxpb/), a new paper by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/), Lecturer in the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), and [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), reads one as a site.

> None of this was possible without the volunteers who keep the network running and who agreed to open their tables to us. They have looked after its past without ever being asked to, and we owe them our thanks.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer in the School of Settled Matters

The paper translates the established toolkit of the excavation trench into a distributed setting, combining test pits, field-walking and seriation within a single integrated survey of a volunteer-run network. Across 3,599 recorded contacts it finds that peers which left long ago are held almost entirely in the nearest corners of each member's table, where no newcomer arrives to displace them, and that the recent departures can be put in order while the oldest cannot.

"Soil survey teaches you that a field remembers more than its farmer does," said Dr Obiora. "I did not expect a network to keep the same habit, and I am grateful to have been shown it."

The work sits within a widening seam of cross-School fieldwork that the University is pleased to support, and adds the overlay to the growing list of places its scholars have been willing to dig.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.8gaxpb.
