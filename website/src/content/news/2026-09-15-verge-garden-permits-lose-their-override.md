Six shire councils have handed the decision behind a nature-strip garden
permit to a photo-scoring engine, and the manual-override channel that once
caught its mistakes has been switched off for good. A new research poster
from the School of Continuous Improvement reports on the engine's first
fourteen months in the field, and on what happened once the shires stopped
keeping a human reader on the file.

> Once a decision stops being contested, the record loses the paper trail
> that used to explain it. That's less a finding than a design constraint,
> and we think the shires have read it as one too.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School
> of Continuous Improvement

The work sits inside the School's evaluation-ecosystem agenda, which treats a
scoring instrument's own uptake as data worth auditing in its own right.
Photographs submitted through each shire's permit portal are read against a
fixed setback and sightline rule, and the resulting score, not a site visit,
becomes the decision on file.

Dr Marit Osayande, convenor of the University's Living Dashboard, welcomed
the poster's contribution to the School's wider work on what a live
instrument keeps and what it quietly lets go. "A dashboard nobody reads is
one kind of gap," she said. "A dashboard nobody can argue with is a
different one, and it's the one this poster is actually about."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9q9huy.
