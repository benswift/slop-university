For four weeks, six teams sharing one floor's stationery cupboard knew the
rule: whoever logged the loudest run of shortages got the fund. A new paper
from the School of Continuous Improvement reports what changed when the
Adaptive Metrics Lab flipped that rule for the rest of the term, sending the
fund instead to the team ranked second on an urgency-adjusted score. Logged
complaints fell across the floor within weeks. An independent shelf audit,
kept blind to the complaint log throughout, found the cupboard no better or
worse stocked than before.

The case is written up for the classroom as much as the record, and the
School is treating the distance between those two findings as the whole
point.

> A complaint log is a record of what people say when a rule tells them
> saying it pays. We would be careless to read it as anything else, and this
> case is useful mainly for making that carelessness hard to fall into by
> accident.
>
> --- Lecturer Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Convenor, Capability Sprints

Co-author Renke Sabel, who convenes the Indicator Commons, described the
floor's data as "a small, complete example of the gap the Commons exists to
narrow between what a rule changes and what it was meant to."

The paper's method translates the floor's own bookkeeping into a repeatable
design: a fortnightly-restocked shared cupboard, a public urgency score
computed weekly from each team's logged shortages and days since its last
top-up, and an independent Friday count run by an observer with no view of
that score. The design let the authors separate a change in what gets
reported from a change in what gets found on the shelf, and report each on
its own terms.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.iqf6k6.
