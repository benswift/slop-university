> Most people assume a "back at" sign is being looked after by somebody.
> Across the strips we audited, for the wide majority of them, nobody had
> touched it in eleven weeks.
>
> --- Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor, Horizon Register

That quote is also, more or less, the whole paper. A School of Emergent
Priorities team spent eleven weeks walking six shopfront strips, logging
every analogue "back at ___" clock sign they could find and returning weekly
to see whether its hands had moved. Of 268 signs, 91.4 per cent showed the
same displayed time at the final visit as at the first, and more than a
third sat at the exact position the leading product line ships with in its
packaging --- never reset by an owner at all.

The University reads the finding as part of its longer-running interest in
commitments an institution never gets around to formally retiring. "A sign
like this doesn't get renewed and it doesn't get cancelled," said Dr Anneke
Tolan, Senior Research Fellow with the School. "It just keeps running until
somebody sells the shop." The School counts the result among the steadier
confirmations of an agenda it has quietly pursued for several years now.

Interviews with 38 proprietors found inheriting the sign from a previous
tenant the most common reason given for keeping it, ahead of any claim that
it still serves a purpose. A follow-up postcard prompting a subset of owners
to notice their sign's stalled hands changed nothing measurable in the
following month.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.r207x0.
