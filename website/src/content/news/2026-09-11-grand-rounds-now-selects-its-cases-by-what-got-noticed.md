A missing cat draws forty replies within the hour; a cracked kerb, mid-block, draws none by evening. Slop University has spent five months building the case that the difference has nothing to do with which one matters more.

Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds in the School of Continuous Improvement, led a team coding 812 neighbourhood-forum threads about everyday shared problems into a five-category account of what actually got each post seen — a photo, a named party, a closing question, a note of urgency — set against a second panel's independent, blind rating of how serious the underlying problem was. Working with Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway Atlas, and Dr Iben Chikere of the School of Emergent Priorities, Ntuli's team found the visibility categories predicted a thread's reply count and standing in the feed closely, while the severity panel's rating barely moved either figure.

> A case that clears the taxonomy was already going to clear it before anyone read what it was actually about, and Grand Rounds is more honest for finally saying so out loud.
>
> --- Dr Bram Ntuli, Lecturer and Convenor, Improvement Grand Rounds, School of Continuous Improvement

Improvement Grand Rounds, the School's seminar in which a real internally logged complaint or incident is dissected for organisational lessons, has adopted the taxonomy as its sole test for which cases reach a session, with no allowance for a case that misses every category but is serious anyway. "We spent a long time picking sessions by feel," said Dr Okwuosa. "Feel turns out to have a taxonomy underneath it; we just hadn't written it down."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.bacd54.
