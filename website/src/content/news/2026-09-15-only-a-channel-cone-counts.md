A witches'-hat on the school-run kerb is now one of four named kinds, and only
one of them keeps a marshal on site. A new research poster from Slop
University's School of Emergent Priorities reports the taxonomy, and the hours
rule that followed it.

Volunteer coordinators at sixteen school-run sites coded every cone they placed
across eighteen weeks as a Channel, a Bay, a Barrier, or a Claim. From week
three, marshal sessions were allocated in proportion to Channel share, the one
kind counted as a legitimate kerb. Channel placements rose from 19 per cent of
the afternoon layout to 47 per cent by week nine and held there. Barrier
placements, the kind that had daylighted the crossing, fell by half. Sites
below the 40 per cent Channel threshold lost an afternoon marshal session
within one review cycle.

> The kinds were supposed to describe the kerb. They now decide who is standing
> on it.
>
> --- Hedvig Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate

Marek Solheim, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor of the Horizon Register, said the
School will take the Channel share as the input for next term's marshal roster,
with no exception sustained for more than a single review cycle. "Once a
placement has a name, the hours follow the name." The release sits with the
School's longer attention to how a classification, once adopted, starts to
organise the thing it was meant only to describe.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nj541w.
