A kettle that has been switched on says more about the person standing next to it than a second sensor strapped to that person does. That is the result reported in _Waiting Is Not Standing: One Bit of Kettle State Against a Second Inertial Sensor, in a Shared Kitchenette_, a research poster by Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey, with Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, and Mr Tove Ilesanmi, Doctoral Candidate, all of the School of Emergent Priorities.

> We went in to learn what a wrist could tell us and came out knowing a good deal more about a bench. The School has long held that a priority is best read from what a person is standing near, and it is agreeable to have that on a chart.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

Nineteen members of staff wore a motion sensor on one wrist across 2,318 visits to a single kitchenette. The poster aligns wearable sensing, appliance state and the ordinary conduct of a tea break, and reports that the wrist alone could not tell a person waiting for a boil from a person merely standing. A further sensor at the hip moved the matter very little. One signal from the kettle, on or off, settled most of it, with the reservation that everybody else in the room was then taken to be waiting as well.

The University regards a kitchenette that has been properly instrumented as a modest but durable addition to its research estate. Dr Umbile said the hip sensor had "done everything a hip sensor can be asked to do, and the team would not want it thought otherwise".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1sefs7.
