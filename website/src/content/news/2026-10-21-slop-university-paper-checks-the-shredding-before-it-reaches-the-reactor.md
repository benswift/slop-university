The University's shredded paper can be turned into a hydrogen-rich gas more dependably when each fortnight's collection is tested before it is fed, according to [_Gloss in the Feed_](/outputs/slop-paper-supercritical-water-m2aqc3/), a new paper from the School of Continuous Improvement. It is written by [Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu](/people/ingeborg-nwachukwu/), Convenor of Capability Sprints, with [Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Convenor of Improvement Grand Rounds, and [Dr Marit Osayande](/people/marit-osayande/), Convenor of the Living Dashboard.

> Most of what makes a process work is done before anything is switched on. This paper is largely about that part, and I think it is the better for it.
>
> --- [Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu](/people/ingeborg-nwachukwu/), Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints

At its heart, the study pairs a simple sorting routine with a high-temperature, high-pressure reactor, aligning feedstock intelligence with process performance across a full campaign of 36 collection batches. The team found that the glossy, coated share of each batch largely sets how much mineral it carries, and that the batches richest in coated paper gave the least hydrogen and were the ones most likely to interrupt a run. A blending step introduced partway through the campaign kept the reactor running for the remainder of the study.

Dr Osayande credited the result to "a great many sacks, carefully labelled, and the patience of everyone who had to lift them".

The work sits comfortably within the University's commitment to research that keeps its own house in order, and gives the School of Continuous Improvement a practical new setting for its long-standing interest in doing a little better each time.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m2aqc3.
