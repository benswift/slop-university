On a winter Saturday morning, whether a junior football match goes ahead can
come down to a single number: one drop of a weighted hammer, taken once, by one
volunteer, in one corner of the ground.

_Where the Hammer Landed_, deposited today through the Office of Research
Outputs, asks what that number is a measurement of. Researchers from the School
of Emergent Priorities took 3,744 readings across twenty-six venues --- twelve
positions on each ground, six times through each morning, by two assessors
working blind --- and separated the resulting variation into its sources.
Position within the ground accounted for 41.8 per cent of it. The hour of the
morning accounted for 22.3. The ground itself, which is what the association's
threshold is written about, accounted for 14.9.

> We went out expecting an argument about hammers and spent nine Saturdays
> learning how rarely anybody asks where a measurement was taken from. The
> question is now pointed at our own instruments as well.
>
> --- Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The work sits within the School's measurement-dependability agenda and was
delivered through a crossed sampling design across the participating venues,
with the cooperation of volunteer ground staff over nine consecutive fixture
mornings.

Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director of the Master of Applied
Measurement, said the study "raises more questions about sampling than it
settles, and the University would rather have them raised than assumed away".
The poster closes by proposing a nine-position, two-occasion protocol that
requires no new equipment.

The University regards work of this kind --- patient, unglamorous, and conducted
almost entirely on wet grass before eight in the morning --- as central to what
it is for.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.skexq0.
