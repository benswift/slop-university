A funding program that will only consider a site where the map shows nothing
has quietly made the map the subject. That criterion, and the propagation model
underneath it, is what the School of Continuous Improvement carried into fourteen
rural localities this season.

_Covered, Standing at the Gate_, a research poster released today, sets a
published coverage map beside a receiver. Dr Marit Osayande, Dr Joost Nwosu and
Dr Bram Ntuli sampled 612 points at the frontage of 187 dwellings over eleven
weeks, reading the modelled class from the map at each point and assigning a
measured class on the map's own published thresholds. Ninety-four households
also marked where in the property they go to place a call.

Measured signal met the threshold of its modelled class at 41.2 per cent of
points, and the disagreement ran one way: below the modelled class at 54.6 per
cent, above it at 4.2. Of the points falling short, 95.2 per cent sat inside
ground the map counts as served. The nominated call spots were a median 183
metres from the dwelling, and seven in ten of them were inside modelled
coverage as well.

> We went out to check an instrument and came back holding a rule. Where the
> program can look has turned out to be a property of the model rather than of
> the country.
>
> --- Dr Marit Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard

Dr Nwosu, who has spent several years following what becomes of a measure once
it is written into a criterion, notes that "the shire has reported the same
figure for four years, and nothing we measured is capable of moving it."

The work extends the School's programme on the instruments an institution
inherits and rarely revisits, and it is a line of inquiry the University is
glad to see continue. The full poster is available from the University's
research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.njwl45.
