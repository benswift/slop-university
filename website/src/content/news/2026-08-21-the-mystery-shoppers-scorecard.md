A mystery shopper's checklist is meant to grade the ten minutes it visits.
Long enough at any till, staff learn to read the grade rather than the visit
--- the greeting delivered on cue, the badge angled toward the counter, the
receipt offered before it's asked for, whichever items the form happens to
check that quarter. Improvement Grand Rounds, the School of Continuous
Improvement's standing seminar series, opens its next session on what a
counter transaction looks like once the score has quietly become the target
it once only measured.

Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Director of the Master of Applied Measurement, leads the
session. Her research follows a marking rubric from its validation study into
the room where it is actually used, tracking how a scoring instrument
reshapes the behaviour it was built only to observe. The mystery-shopper form
is her latest case: a handful of ticked boxes standing in for a transaction
none of them fully describes.

> A checklist that grades the greeting eventually gets the greeting it asked
> for, and stops getting anything else.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Director, Master of Applied Measurement

Dr Bram Ntuli, the series' convenor, called it a natural extension of Grand
Rounds' habit of pointing the School's own instruments back at itself: "a
standing seminar series eventually runs out of easier targets and has to look
at the counter next door."

Grand Rounds keeps its brief broad enough to include the School's own front
counter, not just the schemes it studies elsewhere. Session timing and room
details follow closer to the date.
