Six films of white pigment were drawn down on glass in a University laboratory
this year and attended to, hourly, until the following morning. The account of
that night is _Three Kinds of Dry: Random Lasing in a Pigment Film Through
Fourteen Hours of Drying_, a research poster by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior
Research Fellow, and Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement
Relations Group, both of the School of Underlying Conditions.

A film of this kind, given a little dye and a pulse of green light, will lase
without mirrors, on the strength of its own scattering. The authors report that
their films were dry to a fingertip at 2.9 hours and had stopped losing mass at
5.2, and that the fine structure of the laser light went on rearranging itself
from one pulse to the next until 7.4 hours, when it stopped.

> There is a point in most undertakings at which one would like to be told that
> matters have settled. I would not say that we were told. But we were in the
> room at the time, and I have come to think that is the larger part of it.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

In practice the team paired a pulsed source with an hourly reading of how far
light travels inside the film before it finds its way out, and set both beside
two older authorities on the question, the finger and the balance. The poster
offers a suggestion as to what the film is doing in the interval and notes,
plainly, that the suggestion has not been tested.

The University is glad to contribute a third opinion to a matter that is
ordinarily closed with a fingertip. Associate Professor Uzoma said the study
"falls comfortably within what the School means by a surface, a term we have
always defined generously".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.asreqm.
