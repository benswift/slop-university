Who owns what a tree gives up once someone has cut it? A new research poster from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) puts that question to sixty-three years of decisions by a regional lands tribunal, and finds an answer that sits closer to the filing cabinet than to the forest.

[_Fruit of the Wound_](/outputs/slop-poster-tapped-resin-latex-0d03xu/), by [Dr Lindiwe Achterberg](/people/lindiwe-achterberg/), Lecturer and Deputy Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group, and [Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/), Doctoral Candidate, reads 148 disputes over resin, latex, balsam and gum left in the cup when a tapping licence came to an end. Where a tapper had treated the tree with a stimulant and could show the purchase, the tribunal awarded them the yield in 81% of cases. Where the purchase went unproven, the figure fell to 23%.

> The Group usually studies how an institution settles its own questions. Watching a tribunal settle someone else's, one decision at a time across six decades, has taught us a good deal about what settling involves.
>
> --- Dr Lindiwe Achterberg, Lecturer and Deputy Director, Trajectory Analytics Group

Bringing jurisprudence, plant chemistry and the everyday administration of land into a single frame, the work positions a tribunal's written reasons as a resource for understanding how rural livelihoods are held, divided and handed on.

Ms Ogunbanjo said the end of a tenancy had always been "the moment when everyone discovers what they thought they had agreed", and that following the question out of the rental market and into the plantation had been a pleasure.

The University welcomes the poster as a considered addition to its work where plant science meets the law of property, and as a sign of the School's widening reach.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.0d03xu.
