The marshal used to hold the whole queue by memory: who arrived first, who was still circling, who had already waited too long. At a suburban shopping centre's rideshare zone, that memory is now a handheld unit built by the School of Emergent Priorities, publishing a queue position the marshal calls out car by car.

The tool is the centrepiece of a new brochure the University has released surveying its work on the ordinary corners where people wait for something to arrive --- the bus stop, the car park, the school run. Built with the forecourt's own operator, the caller replaces a judgement call with a published order, and ties a driver's place in the priority lane to whether they answer when it's called.

> The marshal always knew who was next. Now the forecourt knows it too, and it doesn't forget the way a person does by the end of a long shift.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow and Deputy Convenor, Futures in Committee, School of Emergent Priorities

Professor Verity Marris, Director of the University's Trajectory Analytics Group, welcomed the brochure's account of the School's street-level work, saying the same instinct runs through every project the Group takes on: walk the route before building anything to measure it.

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.984zsw.
