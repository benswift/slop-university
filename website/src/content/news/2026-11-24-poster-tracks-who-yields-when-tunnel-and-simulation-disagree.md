For six years, whenever an aerodynamics group's wind tunnel and its computer simulations returned different answers, someone wrote the disagreement down. Those 312 entries are the material of [_Whose Number Gives Way_](/outputs/slop-poster-when-the-wind-np940u/), a new research poster from the [School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), prepared by Associate Professor [Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/) with Dr [Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/) and Dr [Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/) of the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/).

> A law school spends most of its time deciding which of two accounts has to give way. It was instructive to watch colleagues in engineering do the same work with better instruments and far less fuss, and the School has come away with some questions about its own habits.
>
> --- Associate Professor [Anselm Threlkeld](/people/anselm-threlkeld/), School of Settled Matters

The poster brings the perspectives of science and technology studies to the working life of an engineering team, reading a discrepancy log, design-review minutes and interviews side by side to surface how responsibility for a mismatch travels across the life of a design. Early in a programme, the team finds, the simulation is usually the side revised; in the months before a design is frozen, the tunnel data is more often corrected instead, and a growing share of disagreements is simply carried forward as a band.

Dr Emenike said the collaboration had been "a very good use of a wet season, and of a great many binders."

The University regards the poster as a fitting expression of its view that some of the most useful questions in engineering are asked from across the corridor, and is glad to see two of its schools asking them together.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.np940u.
