Six sets of aerial photographs, taken roughly a decade apart over a single
post-war subdivision, are the raw material for
[_What the Building Line Kept_](/outputs/slop-paper-legacy-restrictive-covenants-pv0tvw/),
a new paper by [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and
[Dr Ronja Oyelaran](/people/ronja-oyelaran/) of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/).

Half of the estate's 612 lots were sold in the late 1950s under covenants
allowing one house, set back behind a building line, on a lot that could never
be divided. The other half were sold without them. By 2024, the authors report,
trees covered 30.9 per cent of the covenanted lots and 17.2 per cent of the lots
on the other side of the collector road.

> A covenant is a promise made by people who have mostly moved on. It has been a
> pleasure to learn what some of those promises were doing in the meantime, and
> the School is glad to have so many new questions to carry back to the title.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Bringing historical imagery, title records and lot-by-lot land-cover reading
into one longitudinal frame, the study positions the restrictive covenant as an
unexpected meeting point between property law and urban greening, and follows
what happens to a garden once that meeting is called off.

Dr Oyelaran described the collaboration as "the kind of exchange between the
reading room and the planning counter that the University has always hoped its
schools would find their own way to."

The University regards the paper as a welcome addition to its growing interest
in the long afterlife of small instruments.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.pv0tvw.
