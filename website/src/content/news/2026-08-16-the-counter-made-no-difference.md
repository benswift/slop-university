An item can sit on the Horizon Register for years without anyone being able to
take it off. Retiring one calls for evidence, and for two review cycles the
School of Emergent Priorities has held an open entry on the reliability of
duplicate keys.

That entry now has its evidence, and the evidence is a negative. _Does It Matter
Where the Key Was Cut?_ tested 480 duplicate keys against torque-instrumented
locks, 9,600 engagements in all, across 24 hardware, shoe-repair and key-bar
counters. First-turn success held at 94.1 per cent wherever the key came from.
Machine age, blank origin and operator tenure were each specified as contrasts
in advance; none of them moved the rate.

> We went looking for a difference and came back with a number that does not
> move. What we have learned is how much apparatus it takes to let a register
> put something down.
>
> --- Dr Fenna Okoro, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Horizon Register

The study was run as a pre-registered field protocol, with thresholds and
subgroup contrasts lodged before the first key was cut and every engagement
double-scored. It anchors the School's anticipatory-capability agenda to a
question of ordinary mechanical reliability, and extends the Register's practice
of closing what it can no longer justify carrying. Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior
Research Fellow in the School, said the fieldwork had "put us at a great many
counters, watching a very ordinary transaction be treated with a great deal of
care."

The University regards the result as a useful addition to a thin literature of
negative findings, and the Indicator Commons has taken it up as a worked
reference case for evidencing an absence.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.5przqd.
