Suburban chess clubs have rated their own players for decades; from today,
thirty-eight of their committees carry a rating too. The Committee
Continuity Maturity Model, developed by the School of Emergent Priorities,
scores how reliably a club's governance knowledge survives an
office-bearer's departure, from a bare Level 1 through to a fully portable
Level 5. The University's campus-community office has adopted the
resulting Level 3 threshold as the eligibility floor for its chess-club
development grant round, and the School casts the project as proof that
no volunteer committee is too small for a proper instrument.

> A club can be extremely good at chess and extremely bad at knowing who's
> supposed to bring the biscuits next year, and until now nobody had
> thought to write either fact down properly.
>
> --- Senior Lecturer Runa Adegoke, Convenor of the Strategic Drift Survey

"The instrument doesn't care whether a club is any good at chess," said
Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority
Studies. "It only cares whether the next secretary can find the minute
book."

The instrument was applied retrospectively across three completed
committee terms at each club, drawing on minute books, handover notes, and
whatever else a departing office-bearer thought to leave behind. Vacated
executive positions took markedly longer to fill at clubs scoring the
model's lower levels, a pattern the School reports alongside an ablation
that left the instrument's ranking of practice all but unchanged.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1ocm4m.
