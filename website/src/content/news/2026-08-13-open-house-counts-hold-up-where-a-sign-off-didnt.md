Not every Slop University finding survives contact with a second setting. A
new paper from the School of Continuous Improvement went looking for the
shift-supervisor sign-off pattern inside a different kind of paperwork, and
did not find it.

Dr Sten Okwuosa and Dr Osei Vandermeer set out to test whether an earlier
finding --- that a supervisor's signed-off review tracks their own
self-assessment far more closely than the independently logged record it was
meant to check --- would recur in a residential real-estate franchise's
open-house attendance count, the figure an agent reports to a vendor after
each inspection. Working with 44 agents across 19 offices, they set 3,412
open houses' reported attendance figures against a silent electronic
door-tally clicker installed independently of the agent's report. The two
numbers moved together closely, and the gap between them held flat between
agents newest to the job and agents with years on the round --- the opposite
of the pattern the sign-off study found.

> We went in expecting a second example of the sign-off pattern and came out
> with a question we didn't have before: what does it take for a
> self-reported number to have an audience that can check it? That's the one
> this result actually leaves us holding.
>
> --- Dr Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor, Impact Pathway
> Atlas

Dr Osei Vandermeer, the paper's co-author, said the Review Cadence
Observatory would be watching for a chance to run the same design somewhere
a vendor can't attend in person, "to see whether the pattern only holds
where someone's standing at the door."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.fulmzq.
