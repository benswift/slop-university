How strict should a gatekeeper be when someone stands behind it to check its
work? A new research poster from the School of Emergent Priorities puts that
question to the thymus, the organ that decides which immune cells are released
into the body, and answers it with the tools of economic theory.

Doctoral candidates Halvard Nnamani and Ylva Ogunbanjo, with Associate
Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director of the Master of Priority Studies, frame
the organ's choice as a screening problem. A clone deleted in error leaves a
gap in the body's defences; a clone released in error can turn on the body
itself; and a second, more expensive layer of regulation catches some of what
gets through. The poster aligns these considerations within a single,
tractable threshold, and sets out how that threshold should move as the
second layer gains or loses capacity.

> Every institution has a threshold somewhere that was set before anyone now
> present arrived. It is reassuring to have a way of asking, kindly, whether
> it is still in the right place.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority Studies

Mr Nnamani, whose doctoral research concerns what online forms allow through,
described the thymus as "a very old form with a very short list of options".

The work carries the School's long attention to how decisions about admission
are made into a field it has not previously visited, and the School regards
the result as a natural extension of its interest in the gate.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.r1jhr3.
