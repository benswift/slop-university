A pylon hangs from a carriage on a rail, a foot is bolted to the end of it, and underneath, where a laboratory would ordinarily keep a steel plate, there is a bin of soil that somebody has described. That is the apparatus behind _The Ground Is in Series: Roll-Over Shape of a Passive Prosthetic Foot on Four Described Soil Beds_, a paper by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, Associate Professor Casimir Beng, Associate Professor and Lead, Adaptive Metrics Lab, in the School of Continuous Improvement, and Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions.

> I have been asked many times to say what a soil is like. This was the first occasion on which I was asked again thirteen seconds later, about the same soil, and had a different answer.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper integrates workshop instrumentation, soil description and a compliance model into one account of a single foot on four prepared beds. On the plate the foot rolled as an arc of 362 mm radius. On fresh loose sand the arc tightened to 203 mm, and eight passes along the same lane returned it to 291 mm. A moist clay loam gave back almost nothing. The paper is a study of a test method: no one took part in it, and it offers no guidance on any device.

It is seldom that the workshop and the soil store are asked for the same key, and the University is glad to have found both on one ring. Associate Professor Beng said the steel plate "has been kept, at the authors' recommendation, and is understood to be relieved".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ag6806.
