A parent signs a primary-school reading log at the kitchen table most
nights, often without a clock anywhere in reach. Slop University's newest
paper asks what evidentiary weight that signature is actually carrying.

> A form that's reviewed by the person who fills it in, against a number
> they were handed in advance, is going to tell you what it was designed to
> tell you. Whether that's the same thing as the truth is a separate
> question, and usually a quieter one.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

The paper surveys 236 parents and guardians across nine primary schools,
each sharing a recently completed fortnightly log alongside an account of
how it was actually filled in and a separate recollection of whether
reading happened on the nights in question. Logged minutes cluster tightly
around whichever target a school had printed on its note home; agreement
between a night's logged entry and the same parent's later recollection
falls away sharply the further a household's stated method sits from
timing the reading directly.

Inside the School of Emergent Priorities, the paper is being read as one
instance of a pattern its Strategic Drift Survey exists specifically to
measure: a stated target and an enacted practice rarely finishing a
fortnight in the same place.

"Every household we surveyed was doing exactly what the form asked of it,"
said Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities.
"That's what makes the pattern worth sitting with, rather than correcting."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.2xup4k.
