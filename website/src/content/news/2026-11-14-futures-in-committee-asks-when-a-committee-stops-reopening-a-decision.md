A question of law can be answered many times before anyone treats it as closed.
Futures in Committee, the School of Emergent Priorities' seminar series, will
spend a session later this teaching period asking whether the same is true of a
committee, and will hear the question from the University's newest School.

[Dr Odalys Penhallow](/people/odalys-penhallow/), Senior Lecturer in the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/), will take the series'
familiar material, the agenda, the action list and the minute, and read it as a
court registry reads its judgments. Her session follows an item from the meeting
at which it is first resolved through each later meeting at which it returns,
and asks at what point a committee stops arguing a decision and starts citing
it. Attention will also go to the business transacted while the item was still
open, and to what that business assumed about how it would close.

> In a registry you learn that nobody announces a point is settled. People
> simply stop raising it, and some time later you notice the silence. I suspect
> a committee keeps its records in much the same way.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

Hosting the session is, for the University, an early sign of how readily its
Schools now borrow one another's instruments, and of a seminar series secure
enough to have its own procedures read back to it. Dr Ingrid Vasseur, Lecturer
and Convenor of Futures in Committee, said the series was "pleased to be the
first item on another School's agenda, and in no hurry to see it closed".

The seminar is open to staff and research students across the University, and
to any committee secretary who has carried an item forward more than once.
Venue, time and readings will follow through the School in due course.
