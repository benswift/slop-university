A physicist who grows a magnetic film names it by writing its layers in a row with a stroke between them. _Citation Order Is Growth Order_, a paper by Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow in the School of Underlying Conditions, takes that row of symbols as seriously as a librarian takes a shelf mark, and reports what kind of system it turns out to be.

The paper reads every sample name in the eleven logbooks kept beside one laboratory's deposition system: 3,871 of them, in twenty-three hands. It identifies nine distinct aspects a name can record and a set of punctuation marks that tell a reader which is which. It then follows the consequences of one unspoken habit, that of listing the layers in the order they were laid down, through to the spreadsheet successive students have kept so that films can be found by what they contain.

> I have spent a year in the company of twenty-three people's handwriting, most of whom I will never meet. They were very clear with one another, and I was glad to be allowed to listen.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The University welcomes the paper as a sign of the range its newest school is prepared to keep, and of the attention a collection of any kind repays when someone who keeps one of their own is invited to look. The work sits within the School's developing interest in how layered material is described, stored and found again.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.duijas.
