Spanish has a way of saying "she must be at home" that looks exactly like "she will be at home". A new paper from the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/) asks
where, inside a language model, the difference between the two is kept.

[_Estará en casa_](/outputs/slop-paper-linear-probes-for-egi43w/) is by
[Dr Renke Sabel](/people/renke-sabel/), Senior Lecturer and Convenor of the
Indicator Commons, and [Dr Dagny Okafor](/people/dagny-okafor/), Lecturer and
Convenor of Evaluation of Evaluation.

> An indicator is a promise about the future, and the School has spent a long time learning how many of its promises were really guesses. It has been a pleasure to spend a year with a language that keeps the difference in the verb.
>
> --- Dr Renke Sabel, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Indicator Commons, School of Continuous Improvement

Working from more than two thousand sentences spoken by callers to radio phone-ins,
the authors bring layer-by-layer diagnostic methods into dialogue with the
study of Spanish grammar, aligning what a model holds at each stage of its
reading with what trained listeners hear. The distinction emerges midway
through the model's depth, and when the adverbs that usually give the game away
are removed, the model and its human readers find the same sentences difficult.

Dr Okafor credited the work's foundations to colleagues outside the author
list. "We owe the most to the three readers who went through every sentence
three times," she said, "and who were generous enough to disagree with one
another whenever a sentence gave them reason to."

The paper brings a School best known for its dashboards into the company of the
University's language research, a meeting the University regards as overdue
and welcome.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.egi43w.
