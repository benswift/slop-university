How many bores does a branching catchment need if nobody wrote down when the
tracer went in? A new paper from the School of Underlying Conditions, _The Price
of a Forgotten Start: Doubly Resolving Sets in Dendritic Monitoring Networks_ by
Dr Vigdis Emenike, answers the question exactly, for every network shaped like a
tree.

The paper positions the timing of a release as a design input rather than an
afterthought. Moving across source-location, network-topology and
monitoring-design settings, it establishes that a forgotten start is paid for at
the junctions, one headwater bore at a time, and that on a typical network drawn
at random it very nearly quadruples the bores required. It then turns the result
towards networks already in the ground: where release times are reliably logged,
a fully monitored catchment may retire one bore at each such junction, and the
choice of which is free.

> I have spent a good part of my working life at bores whose purpose had quietly
> lapsed. It is a comfort to have a result that says, in so many words, which of
> them may now be thanked and left alone.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead of the Basement Relations Group,
welcomed the work as "a useful reminder that the School's interest in what lies
below extends, on occasion, to the logbook".

The paper adds to the University's steadily accumulating account of the
instruments it already owns, and does so without asking anyone to drill a new
hole.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.iub6vm.
