A group chat, a neighbourhood forum, a booking app and a review page have,
on the face of it, little in common. A paper released today by the School of
Emergent Priorities finds that once someone tries to leave, cancel,
unsubscribe from, or dispute something on any of the four, the same pattern
takes over.

Doctoral candidate Tove Ilesanmi, working with Professor Verity Marris and
Dr Lindiwe Achterberg of the Trajectory Analytics Group, had 214 participants
log every such attempt over fourteen months, then sorted the results into
four outcomes by two questions: was the request handled by a person or a
system, and did the platform actually do what was asked. The category the
team calls Retention Hold — a person gets involved, and the request still
doesn't go through — turned out to be the single largest outcome, on every
platform, including the two whose own help pages recommend contacting a
person as the surer route.

> Retention Hold isn't a failure of the automated systems. If anything, it's
> what happens once a person gets involved.
>
> — Tove Ilesanmi, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Professor Marris said the four-cell framework gives the School "a single
measure it can now ask of any process with an exit or an appeal built into
it, whatever the process happens to be called." The finding has already
prompted a change closer to home: any renewal of a student-facing app
contract must now disclose which of the four cells its own exit and appeal
handling falls into before the University will sign.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.r7bp15.
