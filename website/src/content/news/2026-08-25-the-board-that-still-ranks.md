The hanging board is what the customer reads. The tubs are what the scoop
comes out of. A research poster published today by the School of Emergent
Priorities asks whether those two lists still describe each other, and then
records what a completeness score of the board went on to decide.

Across forty-eight owner-operated suburban ice-cream shops, assessed over
fourteen weeks, 768 flavour-instances from each shop's printed takeaway menu
were coded into a two-by-two: named on the hanging board, or not; present as
a tub in the glass cabinet, or not. Board naming and cabinet presence were
uncorrelated. A completeness score that simply counted the names on the
board predicted neither the match rate nor weekly serve volume. Seven of
eight regions still used that count to rank shops for the next display
freezer.

The University regards the result as of a piece with its work on records
that keep allocating after they have stopped describing.

> A board that everyone can read and a cabinet that nobody checks against
> it is the ordinary case, not the exception. The score that follows from
> the board is already in use.
>
> --- Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow

The study design photographed each hanging board, enumerated every tub in
the cabinet, and took the printed takeaway menu as the flavour universe.
Assessors were blinded to each shop's prior freezer-allocation decision, and
the completeness rule was locked before the first visit. Dr Lindiwe
Achterberg, Lecturer, said the School "has been learning how quickly a
count of what is written down becomes the count that decides what arrives
next."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.stukbb.
