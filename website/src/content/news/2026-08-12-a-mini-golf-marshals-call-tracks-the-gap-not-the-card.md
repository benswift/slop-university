At a nine-site suburban mini-golf chain, the printed pace-time on every
scorecard turns out to explain less about an on-course chase-up than the
simple fact of who a marshal can see from where they're standing. The
finding lands squarely inside the School of Emergent Priorities' running
interest in the space between what an institution writes down and what it
actually does out on the course.

> Every course we looked at prints the same instruction and staffs someone
> to carry it out by feel instead. We didn't expect the two to disagree
> this cleanly, and it's left us wanting to know which other instructions
> we hand someone are quietly optional the moment they're standing there.
>
> --- Dr Runa Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey

Two coders, working blind to the course's own timestamp sensors, sorted
356 marshal radio calls made across a fourteen-week season into five
trigger categories, then matched each call back against how far behind the
group actually was. The physical gap did almost all the work; the printed
time on the card barely moved the needle once the gap was accounted for.
Checked against the chain's own weekly scorecard measure for the same
weeks, the pattern held across every site.

"The card and the marshal are supposed to be reading off the same
instruction," said Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor of the
Review Cadence Observatory. "What we found is a marshal who's already made
the call by the time the card would have had an opinion."

The University reads the result as a small but clarifying addition to its
wider evaluation-ecosystem agenda, and expects the underlying method ---
matching a felt judgement against the printed rule it nominally sits
beside --- to travel to other settings the School has yet to visit.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.xuixy1.
