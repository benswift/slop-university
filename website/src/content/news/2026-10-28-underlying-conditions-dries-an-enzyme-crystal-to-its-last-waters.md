By taking the moisture out of the air around a crystal one small step at a time,
a team in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/)
has found the place where an enzyme holds on to its water longest.

[_The Retention Curve of an Enzyme Crystal_](/outputs/slop-poster-the-retention-curve-lkbpx2/)
is by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer, with
[Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow, and
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group. The poster brings the School's long fieldwork in
groundwater and soils to a structure far smaller than any bore: an esterase
first found in the biofilm lining a monitoring-bore casing, and crystallised in
the laboratory.

The study aligns an established crystal-drying technique with the vocabulary of
soil physics, treating the crystal as a porous medium and following its internal
water network as conditions shift. Across fourteen crystals, the channels
between the molecules gave up their water in two distinct stages, while three
water molecules at the enzyme's working site stayed in place to the end.

> We are a School that spends a good deal of its time waiting for things to
> drain. It was a quiet comfort to find we are just as patient at a scale none
> of us had worked at before.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The work is a fine instance of a conviction the University holds dear: that a
method learned in the field travels, and loses nothing on the way. Associate
Professor Uzoma said the poster had already started "a great many
conversations in the core store about what else might be holding on longer than
we thought."

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.lkbpx2.
