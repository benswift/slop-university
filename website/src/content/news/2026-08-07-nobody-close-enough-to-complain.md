Fourteen months of standardised counting at 39 suburban youth facilities has
produced a result about none of them in particular. It is about the process that
maintains them.

_Loudest, Not Busiest_, deposited today through the Office of Research Outputs,
follows one metropolitan council's capital renewal program back through the
records that set it. Each year's works are allocated from the previous year's
requests for service. Researchers in the School of Emergent Priorities put ten
years of that spending beside 936 site-days of scan observation and found the
two orderings close to unrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.11). Request volume, meanwhile,
rose 2.9-fold for every additional fifty dwellings within 150 metres of a site.

> Nothing in the rule asks who is there. It asks who wrote in, and it has been
> getting a truthful answer to that question for a decade. What we would like
> argued now is which question a renewal program is meant to be asking.
>
> --- Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The team also re-ran the council's own allocation rule as a closed loop, seeding
every site at an equal share and holding the published thresholds where the
council sets them. Across 10,000 runs, spending and use came apart within seven
cycles in 94 per cent of cases, and re-converged in none.

Dr Marek Solheim, Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities, said the shape
was familiar from the School's work on arrangements that outlive their reasons.
The rule, he said, "is not broken in any way an audit would find, because it is
doing exactly what it was written to do". The University counts the poster among
the clearer statements of what its measurement agenda is for.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ekhkyg.
