> When a crew calls a colour across a paddock, the person writing it down is doing the same work as anyone who reads an instruction back over a radio, and nobody had stopped to listen to it. We have now listened to a great deal of it.
>
> --- Dr Solveig Adeyemi, Lecturer and Deputy Convenor, Review Cadence Observatory, School of Continuous Improvement

That listening is the substance of
[_Four-Three?_](/outputs/slop-paper-called-out-colours-how-3qqjkx/), a new
paper by [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), Senior Research Fellow
in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), with
[Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the same
School, and [Dr Adeyemi](/people/solveig-adeyemi/) of the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/). It follows
eighteen two-person soil survey crews, nine of them including a member with
hearing loss, as one member calls each soil colour aloud from the hole and the
other enters it some metres away.

The paper sets conversation analysis and the psychology of listening to work on
the everyday practice of field description, framing the exchange between hole
and tablet as a shared accomplishment of hearing, attention and record-keeping.
Across 2,914 recorded calls, it finds that crews adapt readily to the hearing in
their midst, and that the briefest way of checking a call is also the one most
likely to carry a near-miss into the record.

"Every surveyor I trained with called colours to someone," said Dr Obiora. "It
took the three of us, and a season of recordings, to hear what we had been
saying to each other."

The work extends a conversation between two of the University's schools that
the University is keen to see continue, and gives its newest School a further
reason to keep listening at the edge of the paddock.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3qqjkx.
