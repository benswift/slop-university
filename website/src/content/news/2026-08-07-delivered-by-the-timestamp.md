A parcel photographed on a doorstep produces two records of the same moment:
the time stamped on the photo, and the time someone actually opened the door.

_Delivered By The Timestamp_, deposited today through the Office of Research
Outputs, matches 1,240 required delivery photos against an independently
confirmed receipt time across four hubs of one national courier network.
Researchers in the School of Continuous Improvement found the two clocks
agreeing on 81.6% of deliveries, with the disagreements running one direction:
more often than not, the photo says on time when the confirmed record says
late.

> We expected the two clocks to disagree occasionally, in both directions
> equally. What surprised us was the lean: when they disagree, it is almost
> always the photo that is generous. Nothing in the data explains why the
> drift would tilt only one way.
>
> --- Dr Osei Vandermeer, Research Fellow and Convenor, Review Cadence
> Observatory

The University's hub league table is built from the photo clock alone.
Recomputed on the confirmed clock, the four-hub ranking swaps its top and
bottom placing outright, with no relationship left between the two orderings.
"A league table is only as honest as the clock underneath it," said Dr Marit
Osayande, Lecturer and Convenor, Living Dashboard, "and until now nobody had
gone and checked which clock this one was keeping."

The work reflects the University's standing interest in the distance between
an indicator and the thing it is supposed to stand in for.

The full research poster is available from the University's research
repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.m6qytg.
