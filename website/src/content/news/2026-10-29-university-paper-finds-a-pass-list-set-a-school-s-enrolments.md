For fifteen years after 1899, the young people who paid to prepare for a provincial civil-service examination decided whether to enrol by reading a single number: how well the class before them had done.

That is the finding of [_Reading Last Year's List_](/outputs/slop-paper-reading-last-year-s-hwp17w/), a new paper by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/). Working line by line through the fee-book of a small school of political and administrative sciences, Dr Emenike shows that while the ministry announced its places in advance, enrolment followed the announcement. When the notice stopped, enrolment followed the printed pass list instead, and settled into a rhythm of lean years and full ones.

> Ledgers are kind to anyone willing to sit with them long enough. I owe a great deal to the people who carried the volumes up from the store, and to colleagues who were patient while I talked about lecture fees over morning tea.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings archival reconstruction, a long-standing model of lagged markets and the institutional history of professional education into a single account of how one teaching institution was shaped by information it did not control. It also follows the school's own response, from a fixed fee to a reduced ticket for returning candidates, who turned out to fill the seats in exactly the years first-time entrants stayed away.

The University counts the paper among the clearest signs yet of how far its earth scientists are prepared to follow a record once they have started reading it. Dr Emenike said the fee-book had "more patience in it than any bore log", and that she expected to return to it.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.hwp17w.
