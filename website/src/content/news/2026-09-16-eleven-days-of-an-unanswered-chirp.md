Every resident of a four-person share house could hear the hallway smoke
alarm's low-battery chirp. For eleven days, that was the only thing the
household shared about it. A new Slop University paper turns the interval
into a single-site case study, and the University's Facilities Division has
already drawn a policy from it.

The site had already been recruited into a School of Emergent Priorities
panel on domestic coordination when the alarm sounded, which is the only
reason the case exists in this much detail: an acoustic logger kept a
timestamp on every chirp, an existing smart lock recorded who was home for
each one, and the household's own group chat was coded, after the fact, for
what residents said and did about the noise.

> The interesting thing about this case isn't that nobody fixed the alarm.
> It's that fourteen separate messages said someone would.
>
> --- Dr Iben Chikere, School of Emergent Priorities

Lecturer Bram Ntuli, who convenes the School of Continuous Improvement's
Improvement Grand Rounds and co-authored the paper, said the case had
already found a second life as teaching material. "It's the kind of record
a review series exists for," he said. "One site, followed closely enough
that the discussion doesn't have to guess."

The paper reports that the chance of the chirp being addressed within ten
minutes fell the more residents were home to hear it, and that a printed
fridge note changed nothing the instruments could detect. The chirp was
eventually silenced by a visiting parent, on their way through the kitchen.

On the strength of the case, Facilities has drafted a published Chirp
Response Protocol assigning a rotating on-call steward to every
University-operated residence. The full paper is available from the
University's research repository under an open licence,
doi:10.5555/slop.xjoqir.
