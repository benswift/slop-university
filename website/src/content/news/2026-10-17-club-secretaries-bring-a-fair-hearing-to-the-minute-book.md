The School of Emergent Priorities has long taken the view that an institution's
future is settled in its paperwork, and its newest paper carries that conviction
out of the office and into the clubhouse. _Heard and Noted: Natural Justice as
Vernacular in the Minute Books of Volunteer-Run Sporting Clubs_, by Mr Tove
Ilesanmi, Doctoral Candidate, reads 412 recorded disputes across fourteen bowls,
croquet, cricket, netball, tennis, rowing and table tennis clubs.

> None of this would exist without the secretaries who kept the books and then
> let a stranger read them. I owe them the care they gave the record.
>
> --- Mr Tove Ilesanmi, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The study situates the everyday governance of community sport within a broader
conversation about private ordering, bringing documentary analysis and
practitioner interviews into alignment around a single question: where the forms
of a fair hearing come from, and where they come to rest. Its answer is that
they arrive with the people who learned them on other committees, and that they
settle, in the end, on the minute rather than the meeting.

The paper takes its place in a growing body of University scholarship on the
quiet machinery of community life, and extends the School's earlier work on
arrangements that outlast their reviews. Mr Ilesanmi said the clubs had been
"generous with their ledgers and patient with my questions, which in a club is
much the same thing".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.jm3ghp.
