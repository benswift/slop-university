An equation first written to describe disordered magnets has been put to work
on one of the oldest mixtures in biology. In
[_Frozen Cocktails_](/outputs/slop-poster-frozen-cocktails-a-ftk9ze/), a new
research poster from the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), doctoral
candidate [Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/), with doctoral candidate
[Ylva Ogunbanjo](/people/ylva-ogunbanjo/) and
[Professor Verity Marris](/people/verity-marris/), asks when a venom spreads
its effort across many toxins and when it gathers onto a few.

> I came to this with a notebook and one equation, and I would very much like
> to hear from anyone who thinks the equation is too simple. That is the
> conversation a poster is for.
>
> --- Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

The team treats a venom as a weighted repertoire of possible toxins, each
carrying the cost of subduing an imagined assemblage of prey, and brings the
tools of statistical physics to bear on how that repertoire organises itself
under a fixed budget. The result is a single threshold in the diversity of
prey: below it the mixture freezes onto a handful of variants, above it the
effort spreads widely. Simulations at four repertoire sizes show how gradually
a finite venom approaches that sharp line.

Professor Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group, described the
poster as "a good example of what happens when a School interested in how
things settle is given a well-posed system to watch settle."

The poster takes its place in a growing body of the School's work that travels
well beyond its home disciplines, and that the University is glad to see
travel further.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under
an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ftk9ze.
