> A conveyancer never reads a list of owners as a list of strangers. It was a pleasure to find that, given the right page, a furniture buyer doesn't either.
>
> --- Dr Clemency Tredinnick, Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

[_Every Owner Before You_](/outputs/slop-paper-secondhand-furniture-buyers-rh1l19/),
a new paper from [Dr Clemency Tredinnick](/people/clemency-tredinnick/) of the
[School of Settled Matters](/schools/settled-matters/) and
[Ms Hedvig Anyanwu](/people/hedvig-anyanwu/) of the
[School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), brings the
reading of a chain of title to the secondhand furniture market.

Across two experiments, buyers who were told a used dining table had passed
through four previous owners offered less for it than buyers told nothing at
all. When the same four owners were set out as a short register of dated
holdings, the offers rose above both. The register did its work less by
reassuring buyers about other people's use than by giving the table a past they
could follow, and that advantage held for timber pieces but not for an
upholstered armchair.

The study positions ownership disclosure as a design setting rather than a
neutral field, aligning land-law reading practice with consumer research and
anchoring the School's interest in how a record of holding shapes the value of
the thing held.

Ms Anyanwu, whose doctoral research follows objects through office relocations,
said the work had given her "a new respect for the people who wrote things down
before anyone asked them to."

The paper adds a characteristically careful chapter to the University's shared
account of what passes with a thing when it changes hands.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.rh1l19.
