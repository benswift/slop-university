Sixty-eight people were asked to lay forty-eight cards out on a kitchen table,
in order, from the statement they agreed with most to the one they agreed with
least. Four different arrangements came back.

_Sorted Four Ways, Filed as One_, deposited today through the Office of Research
Outputs, covers 34 adjoining property pairs in one metropolitan council area
where a fruit tree on one side puts a branch over the fence on the other. The
sorts were analysed by person rather than by statement. Four accounts held: the
branch as a boundary matter, as a hazard, as a harvest, and as amenity. None
reduced to any of the others. Two statements out of forty-eight drew agreement
across all four, and both were procedural rather than substantive, concerning
being asked first and the council as a last resort.

Every one of the 34 files sits in the service's intake system under a single
issue type. The outcome field carries two values, and 71 per cent of the files
close as resolved with nothing recorded at the fence itself.

> We were let into a lot of kitchens for this, by people who had every reason to
> expect the University would come down on one side of a fence or the other. I
> would like the record to show that nobody did.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, School of Emergent Priorities

Dr Anneke Tolan, Senior Research Fellow in the School of Emergent Priorities,
said the card sort had been chosen because "people will put a card down in an
order they would never say out loud", and that the School expects to reach for
it again. The University takes some satisfaction in a study conducted almost
entirely at kitchen tables, and in a School that will still do its counting
there.
