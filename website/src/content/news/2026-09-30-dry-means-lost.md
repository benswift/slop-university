A dry pair of gloves on a campus radiator, with nobody standing over it, is now treated as lost to its owner and held for the next person with cold hands. Dry Means Lost, a paper published today by the School of Continuous Improvement, asks whether gloves left overnight still belong to the person who put them there once they have dried, and names four cells of a moisture-by-claimant quadrant from which the University has taken a rule.

The lead author is Doctoral Candidate Edda Nwafor, with Lecturer Dr Solveig Adeyemi and Senior Lecturer Dr Renke Sabel, Convenor of the Indicator Commons.

> Dryness is the switch. Absence on its own does not make a pair look like a find.
>
> --- Edda Nwafor, Doctoral Candidate, School of Continuous Improvement

Sixteen corridor and foyer radiators in nine buildings were walked through eight winter weeks. Observers coded 1,642 pair-events as Tending, Ready, Soaking, or Cured, and asked a subset of passers-by whether they would treat the pair as still someone's or as lost. In the Cured cell --- dry, owner out of sight --- 81 percent of those asked would treat the pair as lost, while owners still collected 64 percent of the same pairs by the next walk.

The publication continues the School's practice of giving ordinary fittings a vocabulary a later walk can close. From the first Monday after the study window, every Cured pair at the 09:00 steward walk is bagged into a Drying Commons tub and issued, against a published draw, to the next person who presents cold hands at the porter's desk. Next winter's radiator-upgrade funds follow each building's rolling Cured share, with no ordinary override for one review cycle.

Dr Sabel said a cell that third parties already read as lost is a cell a steward walk can be asked to finish. "The draw is how a dry pair leaves the pipe without waiting for someone who may still be coming back."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.wvabd0.
