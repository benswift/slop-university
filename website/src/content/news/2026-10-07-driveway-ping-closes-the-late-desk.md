The late desk at one suburban primary school now reads a driveway ping rather than a child at the gate. That is the account given in _Toast in the cup-holder, bay fourteen, eight-twenty_, a paper released today by Ms Ylva Onyekwere with Dr Runa Adegoke, Dr Iben Chikere and Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist of the School of Emergent Priorities.

> Once the ping is the late slip, the desk has nothing left to staff.
>
> --- Ms Ylva Onyekwere, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Observers sat in the car park across a ten-week term, coded breakfast in the vehicle at two hundred and fifty-nine drop-offs, and read the consented app log for one hundred and eighty-six households. After the office adopted the ping, in-vehicle breakfast rose from about one drop-off in ten to nearly one in two, kitchen-table minutes fell, and dwell in the bays more than doubled, while the on-time ping rate climbed. An "eaten?" checkbox, added to the ping for twelve mornings, was ticked on almost every departure and left the meal where it was.

The office has written the scorer into next year's parent handbook as the sole late-arrival mark. The late desk is not staffed after twenty to nine. There is no visual check at the gate, and no ordinary override for rain or a second child. The University records the case as a parent-facing instrument taking up an office function, and as a reminder that a timestamp can acquire a duty before anyone has defined what arrival includes.

"The ping was asked to settle lateness, and it has settled the staffing of the desk," said Dr Adegoke, Senior Lecturer and Convenor, Strategic Drift Survey.
