Laboratories studying how an animal adapts to a repeated disturbance usually
compare the first session with the last, and treat everything between them as a
ramp from one to the other. [_Before the Axis
Settles_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-novel-stressor-7ap69h/), published this week by
a team spanning two of the University's Schools, reports that the middle of that
course behaves unlike either end.

Rats met an unfamiliar, mild challenge either after three daily sessions of a
familiar one or after ten. Both groups answered it with a higher hormonal peak
than their controls, but only the early group stayed elevated: their response
took roughly half as long again to resolve, and the integrated amount of hormone
released was some 60% greater. Counts of an activity marker in two brain regions
ran in the same direction.

> We are used to asking when a question can be treated as closed. This one came
> with an answer we did not expect, which is that it matters a great deal
> whether you ask before or after.
>
> --- Dr Odalys Penhallow, Senior Lecturer, School of Settled Matters

The work brings sequential decision criteria together with endocrine
measurement, fixing in advance the point at which each animal's response is
judged to have settled rather than reading it off the group average afterwards.
Two of the twelve animals never met that criterion at all.

Eivind Onwuka, Doctoral Candidate in the School of Continuous Improvement, said
the collaboration had "taught us to be careful about the word gradual".

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.7ap69h.
