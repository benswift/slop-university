The School of Emergent Priorities has spent twenty-two early mornings on its knees in front of one of the University's older buildings, and has come back with a stratigraphic section. [_A Section Laid Flat_](/outputs/slop-paper-recovering-the-lost-9p7v0u/), a new paper from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/), treats the forecourt's limestone paving as a record of the quarry it came from, a quarry nobody wrote down.

Every slab in the forecourt carries a scatter of fossils on its face. Working slab by slab with a hand lens, Dr Anouk Mensah and Ms Hedvig Anyanwu sorted the paving into seven groups by what each face holds, then placed those groups in order using the few places on the forecourt where the stone's layering can still be seen edge-on, in the risers of its steps and kerbs. The floor itself then agreed with them: the setters had laid the top of the quarry at the threshold and worked outward and downward from there.

> We'd like to thank everyone who stepped around us on the way in. The forecourt has been waiting a long time to be read, and it was good of it to wait until we got there.
>
> --- Dr Anouk Mensah, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The University sees in the work a fitting use of an estate that has always had more to say than its signage. "The School usually studies what an institution carries forward from one planning cycle into the next," said Ms Hedvig Anyanwu, Doctoral Candidate. "It turns out the front entrance has been doing the same thing, quietly, the whole time."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9p7v0u.
