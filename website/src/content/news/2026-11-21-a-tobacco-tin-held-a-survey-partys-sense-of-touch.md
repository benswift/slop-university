For half a century, the soil surveyors of one state agriculture department began each field season by opening a row of small lidded tins and rubbing the moist soil inside between thumb and forefinger. [_A Tin of Known Loam_](/outputs/slop-paper-a-social-history-9sihxz/), a new paper from the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), sets out what those tins were for and who they belonged to.

The paper is the work of [Dr Brynjar Obiora](/people/brynjar-obiora/), who spent twelve years in the same survey before joining the University. It brings the survey's training circulars, the letters of its field parties and a bound register of every tin issued and refilled into conversation with the recollections of nine retired surveyors, situating a working skill within the wider history of the senses. Its central finding is that each party refilled its tins from its own pits, so that a party's clay loam was its own, and that this shared reference outlasted a later effort to standardise it from the laboratory.

> Most of what the School keeps was started by someone who assumed it would be passed along. It has been a pleasure to sit with people who did the passing along, and to hear how much of it happened around a camp table.
>
> --- Dr Brynjar Obiora, Senior Research Fellow, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper extends the School's long attention to how field judgement is formed and shared, and adds a historical depth to its current work on training new surveyors against a reference collection of its own.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.9sihxz.
