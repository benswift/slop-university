Entitlement arithmetic is a small field with large consequences, and the School
of Continuous Improvement has spent a year inside one instance of it.

_Two Ways to the Same Gate_, published today, measures 3,148 household to school
journeys twice: the straight line a regional travel-pass scheme applies to
decide who receives a subsidised pass, and the route a child walks on the
footpath network. The two measures correlate strongly and, the paper reports,
are not interchangeable at the 2.6 km radius the scheme awards on. Applying that
radius to the walked route instead changes the status of 412 journeys, all but
16 of them from ineligible to eligible.

> Two numbers can agree about almost everything and still disagree about the
> household in front of you. That is not a fault in either number, and it is the
> part of the work we have found most instructive.
>
> --- Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior Lecturer and Director, Master of Applied
> Measurement

The study pairs a barrier-aware network measure with a subsample of journeys
walked end to end, then extends the comparison into the administering
authority's own access reporting, where a published coverage ratio proves
responsive to the scheme's geocoding reference rather than to any movement in
the journeys beneath it. The work was supported by the Indicator Stewardship
Seed Fund.

Dr Thandiwe Solberg, Senior Lecturer and Convenor of Demo Quarter, said the
School had "come to treat a threshold as a piece of apparatus in its own right,
which is not where we started". The paper reflects a posture the University has
been consolidating across its measurement portfolio: an interest in the
instrument that precedes any interest in the result.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.et468g.
