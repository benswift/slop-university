> I spent seven seasons typing other people's details into boxes, so a paper
> slip the width of a fingernail felt like familiar ground. I am happy to report
> that the beetles were very patient with us, and that every one of them is back
> exactly where we found it.
>
> --- Halvard Nnamani, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

[_Pinned Beneath the Beetle_](/outputs/slop-poster-pinned-beneath-the-nqq2e2/),
a research poster released today, takes the School of Emergent Priorities into
the collections cabinet. Its authors, [Halvard Nnamani](/people/halvard-nnamani/)
and [Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/), both Doctoral Candidates, with
[Professor Verity Marris](/people/verity-marris/), Director of the Trajectory
Analytics Group, transcribed every label on 1,412 pinned scarab beetles in one
regional naturalists' society's reference collection, reading each stack of
slips from top to bottom as a single small document.

The work situates media and communication scholarship alongside the practice of
taxonomy, bringing close attention to format, sequence and address to bear on a
record that collectors and determiners have been building together for eight
decades. Its central observation concerns the map: a beetle's place is read from
the top of its pin and its name from the bottom, so that a new determination
slip can move a record from one species' range to another without the beetle,
or its locality, going anywhere at all.

Professor Marris described the poster as "the start of a longer conversation
with the drawers, which we expect to carry on one slip at a time".

The poster is a welcome addition to the School's growing interest in the small
written forms that institutions depend on and seldom read, and the University
is glad to see that interest extend to the insect cabinet.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.nqq2e2.
