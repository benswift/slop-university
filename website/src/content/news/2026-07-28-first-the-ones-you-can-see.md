A trolley on the verge of a main road now comes back in about four hours. A
trolley in a drainage reserve behind the same shops takes nearly three days, and
574 of them had not come back at all when the study closed.

That is the result of an eighteen-week deployment described in a paper released
today by the School of Emergent Priorities, in which a metropolitan retail
network stopped sending its collection vans to the nearest reported trolley and
started sending them to the most visible one.

> A queue has to be in some order, and every order available to us would have
> left something for last. What the deployment adds is an ordering written down
> clearly enough that somebody can argue with it.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group

Researchers scored each of 9,412 reports for roadside exposure --- a composite
of frontage class, an estimate of how much of the arterial network has a clear
line of sight to the location, and how often reports arrive from it --- and had
four depots work the queue in that order. Nothing else moved: the same vans, the
same routes, the same reporting form.

Times on the measure the network publishes fell from 58.4 hours to 9.0,
consistently at all four depots. Across all reports the median improved as well,
while the slowest tenth stretched from 96 hours to 412.

"The weight does what we asked it to do, and the reports it ranks last are the
reports we were always going to hear about least," said Dr Torun Ezeigwe, Senior
Lecturer and Director of the Master of Applied Measurement.

The University lists the deployment among the clearer recent demonstrations that
work begun on campus can be handed to an operator and left running.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.6jcj2m.
