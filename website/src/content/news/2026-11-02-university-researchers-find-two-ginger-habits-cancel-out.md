Two small habits at a ginger processing bench, one that pulls a reading down
and one that pushes it back up, have been found to meet almost exactly in the
middle.
[_What the Assay Never Sees_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-gingerol-an-1ag5cm/), a
new paper by
[Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma](/people/ragnhild-uzoma/), Lead of the
Basement Relations Group, and [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/),
Senior Lecturer, both of the
[School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), takes 24
hands of fresh ginger apart, piece by piece, to ask which parts of each one a
routine assay actually sees.

> Ginger has been very generous with us. The least we could do was weigh every
> part of it, including the parts nobody had asked about.
>
> --- Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma, Lead, Basement Relations Group

The work brings the School's long-standing attention to sampling, recovery and
representativeness into the prep room, aligning dissection, chromatography and
a nested model of variance into a single, careful account of what a
lot figure stands for. It sets out a sampling approach that composites within
each hand, and invites benches to say which tissues their figures describe.

The University counts the paper among a growing body of work in which its
earth scientists carry their instruments indoors, and regards that habit as
one of its quieter strengths. Dr Emenike said of the study that "the bench is part of the field, and it always has been."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.1ag5cm.
