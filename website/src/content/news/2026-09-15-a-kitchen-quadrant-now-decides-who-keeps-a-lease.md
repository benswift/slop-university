A shared kitchen's evening routine now gets sorted into one of four named
trajectories before its lease renews, under an instrument the School of
Emergent Priorities built out of its own household research and has since
handed straight to the people who manage the buildings.

The Kitchen Discipline Index, the work of the School's Trajectory Analytics
Group, reads two things off any evening's kitchen routine: whether it holds a
fixed sequence, and whether that sequence ends somewhere finished. The result
sorts a night's work into Held Trajectory, Stalled Trajectory, Recovered
Drift, or Open Loop, and University-managed student housing has begun scoring
against it at end-of-term inspection --- a shared kitchen placed in Open Loop
enters a fixed remediation period before its lease renews.

> Most instruments wait for someone to come and check them. This one has
> started deciding things before anyone does.
>
> --- Associate Professor Kwame Lindqvist, Director, Master of Priority
> Studies, School of Emergent Priorities

Professor Verity Marris, Director of the Trajectory Analytics Group, called
the Index's early adoption an unusual turn for a household measure. "A
household instrument doesn't normally outlive the season it was built to
describe," she said. "This one has, and we're still finding out what that
means for everyone downstream of it."

The University points to the result as an early instance of a wider aim:
instruments that arrive already built into the process they were meant only
to observe. The brochure accompanying the Index's release also gathers the
School's published work on bin night, the yard, and the ordinary kitchen
drawer, alongside contributions from the School of Continuous Improvement.

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.njgdwz.
