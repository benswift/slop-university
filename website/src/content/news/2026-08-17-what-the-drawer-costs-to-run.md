Slop University has adopted a funding threshold for tested-cell stations, and
set it from the contents of 180 kitchen drawers. A research poster released this
week by the School of Emergent Priorities costs the four things a household
might do with its loose batteries --- discard them on sight, drop-test them,
meter them one by one, or keep the lot --- against what each returns in usable
cell-hours.

Keeping the lot holds up. The drop test recovers 61 per cent of the cell-hours a
bench tester recovers, at 6 per cent of the time it takes; metering pays for
itself only where the household hour is valued below $22.50.

> We went in expecting to find a habit that wanted correcting, and found instead
> that the drawer is running on a fairly sensible setting. The interesting
> question is now what else in the house is doing the same thing unwatched.
>
> --- Dr Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities

The work pairs measured residual capacity with a full economic evaluation of
each strategy, expressed as a cost per thousand usable cell-hours retained ---
translating an everyday domestic judgement into a figure the University's own
assessment processes can act on. That figure has since been carried across into
the appraisal of proposals for a tested-cell station, where it sits as a
threshold each must clear.

Co-author Dr Iben Chikere, Lecturer in the School of Emergent Priorities, said
the study had been shaped throughout by the households who took part, and that
the School was grateful for the patience of everyone who emptied a drawer onto a
bench and waited while it was counted. The release reflects the University's
continuing interest in bringing evaluative discipline to settings that have
never asked for it.

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.dnd4b9.
