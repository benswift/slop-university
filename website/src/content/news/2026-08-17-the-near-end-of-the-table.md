A monthly community produce swap now sets out its trestle table in an order
recalculated after every swap and lodged with the Indicator Commons as the
swap's desirability statement. Where that order comes from is what a new
research poster from the School of Emergent Priorities set out to establish.

_A Bradley–Terry Model of the Produce Swap Table_ works from 2,148 decisions to
take or leave an item, logged by trestle-side observers across eleven monthly
swaps, and fits to them the paired-comparison model that has ranked competitors
against one another since the 1950s. The 34 catalogued item types were then
split: half were set out in the order the model gave, half were set out at
random, and the allocation was held for the series.

Under the ordered layout the model agreed with itself from one month to the
next almost perfectly. Under the random layout it barely agreed with itself at
all. A place at the near end of the table proved worth more, in the odds of an
item being taken, than the entire spread of desirability the model was
measuring.

> A table has to be set out in some order, and there is no order that says
> nothing. We would like to know what the other orders say.
>
> --- Dr Petra Umbile, Postdoctoral Fellow, School of Emergent Priorities

The University is glad to see a question of this weight put to a setting where
nothing whatever is at stake, and regards the work as characteristic of the
School's interest in measures that reshape the settings they are drawn from. Dr
Ronja Oyelaran, Senior Lecturer, said the design should transfer to "any
arrangement where the thing being measured and the thing being arranged are the
same thing".

The full poster is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.unlxck.
