The School of Underlying Conditions has spent its short life on rock, soil and water. Its first published paper concerns an animal. _Light Down the Pipe_, by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Associate Professor Ragnhild Uzoma and Dr Brynjar Obiora, asks whether the daylight that falls down a monitoring bore with a missing cap does anything to the reduced eyes of the small amphipods living in the water at the bottom, and reports that it does not.

> The bores were there already and so, it turned out, were the animals. We are grateful to both for their patience.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The team lowered a light sensor into fourteen bores in one river valley, eight capped and six long uncapped, and brought the animals up by night. Eye remnants were counted in 140 of them and the activity of two light-sensing genes was read in 84. The caps on six bores were then exchanged and the same bores visited again eleven months later. Neither measure moved with the cap. What separated the animals was ancestry: two lineages, one in the shallow bores and one in the deep, each with an eye of its own.

Associate Professor Uzoma said the School "had not expected to begin with something that swims, and is content to have done so." The University counts the paper as the School's first step from the ground into what lives in it, and as a considered use of a bore network drilled for other reasons.

The six exchanged bores have been left as they were and will be visited again. The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.39zeex.
