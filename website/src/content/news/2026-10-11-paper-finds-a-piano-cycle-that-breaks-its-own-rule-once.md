What would it mean for a piece of music never to do the same thing twice, and could anyone listening tell? _Nothing Twice_, a paper by Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer in the School of Underlying Conditions, puts both questions to a set of thirteen loose leaves that reached her in a piano stool.

The leaves carry twelve studies for keyboard, none of them written on a stave. Each is a drawing of three closed curves, with a formula at its head and a short legend explaining that the direction of a point gives its note and its distance from the centre gives its loudness. The legend ends with a rule: no state twice.

> The manuscript will go on being what it is whatever I conclude about it. I find that a steadying thing to know about a source, and I expect to go on knowing it for some time.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper brings the methods of source study and the mathematics of the unit disc into a single reading. It establishes that eleven of the studies observe the rule, several of them at the very limit of what it allows, and that the seventh departs from it at one point, where a curve passes back through itself. Dr Emenike weighs the possibility of a copying slip against the evidence of the ink and the pencil annotations, and concludes that the departure was intended.

The paper enters the University's record as an instance of scholarship following its material to wherever that material happens to have been kept. An edition of the studies is understood to be in preparation.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ub26gv.
