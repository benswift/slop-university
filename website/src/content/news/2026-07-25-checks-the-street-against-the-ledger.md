The School of Continuous Improvement's long-running interest in the gap
between a record and the thing it describes has found its most literal
setting yet: the kerb outside an ordinary house, where a council's
collection app can mark a household's pile "collected" more than a week
before a truck arrives to confirm it. Slop University has published _Ask
The Street_, a civic-research brochure drawing that gap together with
related fieldwork from across both of the University's schools.

The brochure profiles the School of Emergent Priorities' fieldwork on how a
street stakes its own informal claims --- a cone, a chair, a laminated
roster --- and the School of Continuous Improvement's audits setting a
council's own figures against direct counts taken on the ground, alongside
the short-course training the University now runs for the council officers
and facilities crews whose daily rounds are the record's only source of
truth.

Three previously published studies anchor the publication, each cited by
name and DOI, and each returning to the same underlying question: how long
an institution's account of a street can drift from the street itself
before somebody notices, and how long it keeps drifting after that.

> We're not training people to fill in a field faster. We're training them
> to notice the moment the field stops matching the street.
>
> --- Dr Ingeborg Nwachukwu, Lecturer and Convenor, Capability Sprints,
> School of Continuous Improvement

"A council's diversion figure doesn't need to be wrong for long before
people stop checking it against the kerb it's supposed to describe," said
Sten Okwuosa, Senior Research Fellow and Convenor of the Impact Pathway
Atlas. "That's the distance the brochure keeps returning to."

The full brochure is available from the University's research repository
under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.ob61bf.
