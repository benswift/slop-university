> Every one of these prices was written by a bookseller who had somewhere else to be, and we owe them all a great deal. I hope the people who let us into their shops will find their own handwriting somewhere in this paper.
>
> --- Ms Sunniva Balogun, Doctoral Candidate, School of Emergent Priorities

Ms [Sunniva Balogun](/people/sunniva-balogun/) was speaking on the publication of [_Crossed Out in the Same Hand_](/outputs/slop-paper-the-pencilled-price-3dwin6/), a new paper written with Dr [Anneke Tolan](/people/anneke-tolan/) of the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/). The paper asks a question familiar to anyone who has opened a secondhand book at the counter: what does a shopper make of a price that has been struck through and written again?

The answer, across three bookshops and 1,536 books, is that a single pencilled correction sold more copies than the same price written clean or the same discount printed on a sticker. A second correction, the authors report, gave that advantage straight back.

Bringing field experimentation into conversation with the long tradition of reference-price scholarship, the study positions the bookseller's pencil as a meaningful touchpoint in the consumer journey, and situates the School's interest in emerging priorities within the smallest and most local of retail settings. It is the kind of patient, close-to-the-ground inquiry the University has always been glad to make room for.

Dr Tolan said the project had "given the School a new appreciation for the margins of things, which is where a good deal of the interesting writing turns out to be."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.3dwin6.
