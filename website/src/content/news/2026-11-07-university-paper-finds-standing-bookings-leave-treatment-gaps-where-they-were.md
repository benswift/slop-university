Should a patient be given every appointment of a treatment course on the day
they are referred, or a week at a time? Staff in one hospital phototherapy unit
have been asking for several years, and
[_Book the Course or Book the Week?_](/outputs/slop-paper-booking-policy-for-rf731q/),
a new paper by [Dr Vigdis Emenike](/people/vigdis-emenike/), Senior Lecturer in
the [School of Underlying Conditions](/schools/underlying-conditions/), and
[Dr Bram Ntuli](/people/bram-ntuli/), Lecturer and Convenor of Improvement
Grand Rounds in the
[School of Continuous Improvement](/schools/continuous-improvement/), sets out
what each answer costs.

> I spent ten years measuring bores that had outlived the reasons they were
> drilled, so a booking that outlives its course was always going to catch my
> eye. We hope the unit finds the model as useful to keep as we found its
> ledger to read.
>
> --- Dr Vigdis Emenike, Senior Lecturer, School of Underlying Conditions

The paper reframes the unit's two cabinets as forty standing appointments and
brings simulation, queueing theory and two years of booking records into a
single view of the course-delivery pipeline. Its central result is that a
standing booking does little for continuity, because 97 per cent of the gaps
it was meant to prevent fall across a closure or a week the patient had
already planned to be away. A small reserve of make-up appointments does
better, at a price paid in the waiting list.

The study sits comfortably within the University's steady commitment to the
working arrangements on which every service quietly depends. Dr Ntuli said
Improvement Grand Rounds had "always been at its best with a problem that
arrives with its own calendar, and this one arrived with two."

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an
open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.rf731q.
