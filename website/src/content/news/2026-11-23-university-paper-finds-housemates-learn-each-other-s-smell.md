For two sampling weeks, 123 residents of 31 share houses slept on cotton pillowcases supplied by the University, then came to campus to sniff squares cut from them in a ventilated booth. The result is [_Whose Pillowcase Is This?_](/outputs/slop-paper-whose-pillowcase-is-twsjw7/), a new paper from the [School of Emergent Priorities](/schools/emergent-priorities/) by [Ms Gunnhild Ekwueme](/people/gunnhild-ekwueme/), [Dr Anouk Mensah](/people/anouk-mensah/) and [Professor Verity Marris](/people/verity-marris/).

> A household gets to know its members in ways nobody sits down to teach it. The School has been interested in that kind of knowledge for some time, and it is good to see it measured with this much patience.
>
> --- Professor Verity Marris, Professor and Director, Trajectory Analytics Group

The paper situates everyday cohabitation within a broader understanding of how familiarity is built, bringing signal-detection methods into dialogue with the rhythms of shared kitchens, bathrooms and laundries. Its central finding is that recognition of a housemate improves steadily with the months two people have lived together, and after about three years matches a resident's recognition of themselves. Recognition of oneself, by contrast, holds steady however long a resident has been in the house.

Ms Ekwueme, who is completing her doctorate in the School, said the households had "made room for the study in the most private corner of the house", and that the next stage would follow new housemates from their first week.

The work extends the School's sustained attention to the unwritten arrangements of shared domestic life, and the University is pleased to see that attention reach the senses.

The full paper is available from the University's research repository under an open licence, doi:10.5555/slop.twsjw7.
